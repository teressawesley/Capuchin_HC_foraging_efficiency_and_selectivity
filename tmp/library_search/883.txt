Here
 we examine data from a two-year research on the use of sticks as probes by two groups of wild
capuchin monkeys (Sapajus libidinosus) in Serra da Capivara National Park (PI), Brazil. The use of sticks
as
 probes is not usually observed among wild tufted capuchin (Sapajus spp.) populations, having been
reported as a customary behavior only in SCNP groups. Probe tools are used to access small prey (insects
or
 lizards) in rock cracks or tree trunks, or honey from wasps’ nests, and also to poke toads and poisonous
snakes. Probe use is, so far, the only known case in which wild capuchins modify objects used as tools:
branches
 are trimmed off, and tips, thinned. Tool preparation episodes involved up to four modiﬁcation
steps.
 Contrary to the stone tools used to crack hard nuts, probe tools don’t present any weight constraint
for
 use
 by females, but there is nevertheless a strong male bias (97%) in the occurrence of probe tool use.
There are also no diet biases that could explain this difference. Although males hunt more often than
females,
 the latter main prey items are lizards, which are also the main targets of probe tool use. One
possibility
 is that females may have fewer social opportunities to learn about probe tools.

1. Introduction
Tool use is widespread in bearded capuchin monkeys (Sapajus
libidinosus, previously Cebus libidinosus –  see Alfaro et al., 2012)
living in savannah-like and semiarid environments (Ottoni and
Izar, 2008). Their most common tools are stones used as “ham-
mers”  to crack open hard-shelled fruits, seeds, and nuts (Ferreira
et al., 2010; Fragaszy et al., 2004; Ottoni and Izar, 2008; Spagnoletti
et al., 2012). Others species of the genus (Sapajus xanthosternos
and Sapajus ﬂavius ) also use stone tools to open hard shelled fruits
(Canale et al., 2009; Ferreira et al., 2009), but the only wild groups
of S. libidinosus known, to date, to regularly use stone tools for
other purposes are those from the Serra da Capivara National Park
(SCNP) population. In this location, bearded capuchins of at least
two groups were previously known to use stones not only to crack
or smash fruits and seeds, but also to dig for roots, underground
storage organs, and spider nests (Mannu and Ottoni, 2009; Moura
and Lee, 2004). Most tools are used to obtain food, but there are
exceptions, like stone banging in threatening displays (Moura,

The
 use of probe tools is reported in several primate species
under captivity, mostly in experimental situations (Shumaker et al.,
2011), but is less frequent in the wild. Among chimpanzees, the use
of probes to obtain food is customary in several populations, espe-
cially in the predation of social insects. Army ants’ dipping consists
in placing a stick on the ants and the consumption of the insects that
climb/grab it (McGrew, 1974; Nishida and Hiraiwa, 1982). There
are variations in this behavior depending on the species predated;
if the ant species is more aggressive, the tools are longer and the
processing technique is different (Humle and Matsuzawa, 2002).
There is also the sequential use of probe tools to obtain food by
some groups: chimpanzees from Goualougo Triangle (Congo) use
a thick stick to perforate the termite nests and then a slender and
longer stick to “ﬁsh”  the insects (Sanz et al., 2004), and chimpanzees
from Loango National Park (Gabon) use a similar tool set to dip for
honey in bee nests (Boesch et al., 2009). Some wild populations
of Sumatran orangutans use sticks to access seeds from the Neesia
fruit, and others use stick probes to extract social insects and their
products from tree holes (Van Schaik et al., 1996; Van Schaik and
Knott, 2001).
Captive tufted capuchin monkeys in experimental settings use
probes to push a reward out of a tube (Visalberghi et al., 1995;

Visalberghi and Limongelli, 1994), and also modify and combine
probe tools when the probes were presented tied together, or cut
in pieces that needed to be inserted sequentially (Visalberghi and
Trinca, 1989). In other experiments where capuchin monkeys had
to probe for molasses in a box, they solved the problem by mak-
ing and using probes with the material provided, wooden dowel
and freshly cut branches from shrubs (Westergaard and Fragaszy,
1987). Similar behaviors were observed in semi-captive and semi-
free animals in experimental settings (Aquino and Ottoni, 2001;
Perondi et al., 1995).
Wild capuchin monkeys often hunt small vertebrates (Butynski,
1982; Fedigan, 1990), as well as a variety of invertebrates (Izawa,
1979). Several invertebrates nest in small spaces in tree trunks,
and small vertebrate prey (e.g. lizards), when chased, may hide in
rock cracks or tree holes, escaping the monkeys’ direct reach. The
only population with known customary use of probe tools to deal
with this problem is found in the Serra da Capivara National Park
(SCNP)(Mannu and Ottoni, 2009; Moura and Lee, 2004; Ottoni and
Izar, 2008), but no detailed description of this behavior has been
done. Another long term study on capuchin monkeys in a similar
environment 300 km apart from SCNP never reported probe tool
use (Spagnoletti et al., 2012). The only other report of a similar
behavior involves just a few episodes (N = 8) of termite nest probing
by a non-typical group (four adult males, one adult female, and one
male juvenile) of S. ﬂavius  in the Atlantic forest (Souto et al., 2011).
Here we describe in detail the customary manufacture and use of
stick probes to cast out prey from hiding places, and to dip honey
from bee/wasp hives, by two, not previously studied groups of S.
libidinosus in the SCNP. We also report the use of sticks to threaten
potentially dangerous animals.
2. Methods
2.1. Study site
Research was conducted in Serra da Capivara National Park
(SCNP; Piauí state, northeastern Brazil). The park is located at
the
 geoclimatic domain of the Caatinga, semi-arid climate, with a
mosaic of xerophytic vegetation and patches of deciduous forest at
narrow wetter valleys surrounded by high cliffs. The rainfall is very
concentrated in the short wet season, from November to March. The
study area is the Boqueirão da Pedra Furada, in the southeast border
of the park (limiting coordinates: North: 8◦49′S, 42◦33′W; South:
8◦50′S, 42◦33′W; East: 8◦50′S, 42◦32′W; West: 8◦50′S, 42◦34′W).
2.2. Study groups
We observed two partially sympatric groups (Supplementary
map 1) of capuchin monkeys (S. libidinosus). The Pedra Furada
(PF) group had, in the beginning of the study, 45 individuals, and
the Bocão (BC) group, 27 individuals. The two groups sometimes
met and foraged in the same area for minutes or hours, but never
had agonistic encounters (occasional agonistic episodes occurred
between some individuals, but never included the whole groups).
The park staff provisioned both groups during the dry seasons. The
provisions consisted of fruits (three to four times a week) and dry
corn (every two weeks). The corn was meant for other animals on
the park, but monkeys also ate it.
2.3. Observation method
During the daily following of the group we registered all occur-
rences of tool use (as deﬁned  by Shumaker et al., 2011) with the help
of a ﬁeld  assistant, by All Occurrences sampling (Altmann, 1974).
Tool use behaviors were registered by voice and/or by video.

3. Results
The groups were systematically followed for 20 days per month,
from initial visual contact in the morning until the end of the day or
the loss of contact with the group. The data from PF group were col-
lected for 23 months (Sep/2007–Jul/2009),  and from BC group, for
12 months (Mar/2008–Feb/2009),  with 1290.23 h of contact time
with the PF group and 426.36 h with the BC group.
We registered 480 episodes of probe tool use for both groups,
and collected the probes used in 177 episodes; the average
length of the probes was 27.94 ± SD 14.97 cm (min. 6.5 cm; max.
98.2 cm). The average thickness was 4.67 ± SD 1.19 mm. There
were no signiﬁcant  differences between the two study groups in
the length (Mann–Whitney,  p = 0.334) and thickness of the tools
(Mann–Whitney,  p = 0.437).
There was also no statistical difference in length
(Mann–Whitney,  p = 0.85), or in thickness (Mann–Whitney,
p = 0.837) between the probes used by these groups and by the
other two studied by Mannu and Ottoni (unpublished data).
The targets of the probing behavior were the hiding places of
small lizards (mostly Tropidurus ssp.) and arthropods, or honey
from bees’ and wasps’ nests (Table 2).

The probes used in each of the three more exploited locations
(rock cracks, trees, and termite nests) had signiﬁcant  different
dimensions (Fig. 1). The termite nest probes were longer and thicker
than tree hole probes, and these were larger than rock crack probes.
A typical probing episode starts with the monkey inspecting
the possible nest/hiding place and then going to a near tree to
take/make a probe. The monkey breaks apart a small branch from
a tree and, while returning, the branch is trimmed. After reaching
the target place, the monkey starts to probe with the stick, insert-
ing it into the prey’s hiding place and moving it in and out. When
successful, the prey comes out and the monkey may catch it (Sup-
plementary movie 1). Or, in the case of honey, the monkey simply
licks the dipped probe.
The sticks were usually detached from nearby trees or picked
on the ground. In most episodes, only one tool was used, but up to
seven tools could be used for the same task (Table 3).
We observed the transport of probe tools in 162 events (73.64%
of the episodes observed from the beginning). The average dis-
tance of transport was 2.47 ± SD 2.39 m and the farthest transport
distance was 15 m. When comparing both groups, we found a
signiﬁcant  difference in the transport distances (Mann–Whitney,
p = 0.004): the PF group transported tools for a greater average
distance.

the monkey could be on the ground or on a rock wall (with a bipedal

The use of probes was relatively common, but less frequent than
the use of stone tools. The rates of digging stone tool use and crack-
ing stone tool use were 0.702 and 0.941 ep/h, respectively, but the
overall rate of probe tool use was 0.269 ep/h.
Most probe tools (63.7%) were used after modiﬁcations  (Table 4),
usually one or two, but 36.3% were just picked up on the ground –
some of them left by previous users.
The probes were sequentially combined with stone tools in 28
episodes. These stones were used to enlarge openings in rock cracks
and trunks (pounding tools) or to dig spiders and termite nests
(digging tools). There were also combinations of two or more stone
tools (Falótico, in prep).
Males performed almost all episodes of probing. Juvenile
females performed only 13 observed events (3.09% of all events
with subjects’ sex identiﬁcation),  and there were no records of adult
females using probes.
The success in resource acquisition was different for each of the
three resources most frequently searched with probes (Table 5).
Adults –  who had a higher success rate than juveniles in all cases,
especially when probing trunk holes –  were less efﬁcient  when
hunting lizards in rock cracks.
When the success was examined for each level of modiﬁcation
(Table 6) there was not much difference within adults/subadults
success until level 2, but the juveniles were relatively more suc-
cessful when they did not modify the tool. We did not register
any successful episodes for tools in modiﬁcation  levels 3 and 4,
but these correspond to just 6.88% of the total of events (Table 4).
The observation of probe use by conspeciﬁcs  happened in 20%
(N = 96) of the episodes, and most observers were juveniles and
infants (79.16%). The observers manipulated the discarded tools
after the user left the site in 38 events.
3.1. Other stick tools
Most stick tools were used as probes, but some, not included
in the previous analyses, were used to poke potentially danger-
ous animals during threat events (Supplementary movie 2). We
recorded 11 events where male monkeys used sticks (two of them
much longer than the average probes) to touch animals they were
threatening or exploring. These cases are summarized in Table 7.
We also observed two events of individuals using sticks to groom
themselves. In one case an adult male scratched his own head (Sup-
plementary movie 3) and in another, an adult female used a stick
to scratch/poke the individual she was grooming.
One stick tool was used by an adult male, without the removal
of its terminal branches, to scatter and take down bees (Apis sp)
from a honeycomb. The monkey ate some of the bees.

in tool shape or usage between groups. Compared to ant/termite
dipping/ﬁshing  of the chimpanzees (Humle and Matsuzawa,
2002; Lonsdorf, 2005; Yamakoshi and Myowa-Yamakoshi, 2004),
capuchins usually use the probes to expel prey from their hiding
places, not to make it climb or attach itself to the probe. On the other
hand, the technique of dipping for honey is very similar to the chim-
panzees’. Although we observed probing in termite nests, none of
our subjects used an ant-ﬁshing  or ant-dipping technique like the
chimpanzees’, and the few cases when we observed a monkey pro-
bing termite nests were unsuccessful. The monkeys ate termites
and ants, but they did that by directly destroying the nests or during
the ants’ nuptial ﬂight,  at the beginning of the rainy season.
Stick probes are the only kind of tools wild capuchin monkeys
are known to manufacture by modiﬁcation  of raw materials. Most
probes used are modiﬁed,  usually during its use, although prepa-
ration can be done off-site, while the monkey is transporting the
tool. The modiﬁcations  done while using the tool suggest that the
individuals were reacting to feedback from local/immediate cir-
cumstances and the low success of juveniles with more modiﬁed
tools indicates that there is a learning process involved in the man-
ufacture/modiﬁcation  of efﬁcient  probe tools.
Sequential tool use by capuchin monkeys was observed in some
experimental contexts (Westergaard and Suomi, 1993), but the
SCNP groups were the ﬁrst  wild groups regularly observed doing
this (Mannu and Ottoni, 2009) –  not only the sequential use of tools
of the same kind (replacing a probe or a percussive tool by another),
but also the combination of different kinds of tools (stones and
probes) in a single task.
The different average length of probes for each target location
(trees, rock cracks, and termite nests) imply that the monkeys
choose or modify tools differently in each location, maybe relat-
ing to the different preys and/or physical properties of the place
probed. We could not separate the data by prey type because in
many events the monkeys were not successful and it was not pos-
sible to positively identify the prey.
When compared to other groups in the area (Mannu and Ottoni,
2009), the probes used by our study groups appears to be similar
in their average length. But the probe targets could be different,
because in previous studies there was no mention of vertebrate
predation using probes, although the other prey items are men-
tioned.
For stone tool use, a male bias in frequency has been observed
in this and other populations (Moura and Lee, 2010; Ottoni and
Mannu, 2001; Spagnoletti et al., 2011), but the gender bias is even
greater in the use of probe tools: females almost never use probes
(only 3.09% of the events, none of them by adult females). There is a
big difference in proﬁciency  between genders in percussive tool use
when the cracking target is very hard and requires heavy tools to
open it (Spagnoletti et al., 2011). The hard palm nuts from Cerrado
vegetation, like the ones cracked by the groups in Fazenda Boa Vista
(Gilbués –  PI), require heavy stones (average 1096 g) to be cracked
open (Visalberghi et al., 2007). In this case, a heavier body mass is
a good predictor of success (Fragaszy et al., 2010; Spagnoletti et al.,
2011), and there is indeed a male bias in tool use, as expected. There
are no differences in efﬁciency,  however, when lighter stone tools
are used to process softer materials (Falótico, 2011). This should
also be the case of the probe tools, which are very light and don’t
require great strength to be successfully used. In the laboratory,
females may use probe tools efﬁciently  and there seems to be no
such gender bias (Visalberghi and Limongelli, 1994; Visalberghi and
Trinca, 1989). In short, this gender difference in probe tool use is
probably not related to any physical or cognitive constrains on the
females.
It was suggested that the male bias exists due to parental invest-
ment differences (Moura and Lee, 2010): males would use tools, but
females would prefer to scavenge from the efforts of the males, thus
saving energy. This explanation may apply to the use of stone tools
in nutcracking sites, where there is usually plenty of leftovers, and
females may scrounge it. The use of probe tools, though, yields a
very low success rate, does not produce many leftovers, and we
seldom observed the sharing of prey so obtained.
A diet bias could attract more males to resources related to pro-
bing. In the groups of SCNP, males predate vertebrates much more
frequently than females (79.1% of episodes; Falótico, unpublished
data), but the females also hunt, and lizards are the females’ major
vertebrate prey (61.1%). So, the diet bias does not seem to be a suf-
ﬁcient  explanation for the absence of probe use by females in our
study groups.
Another possibility is that females are less exposed to predated
items and tools left by others, which could diminish their learning
opportunities compared to males, but until now we have no evi-
dence of that. Our ongoing study on the ontogeny of probe tool use
in this population may clarify if there is an opportunity learning
bias favoring males (e.g. males spending more time together) or
whether the females are also able to observe the use of probe tools
but do not use them for other reasons.
