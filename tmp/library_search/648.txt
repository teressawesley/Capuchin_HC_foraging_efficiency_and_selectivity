Stone-Tool Usage by Thai Long-Tailed Macaques
(Macaca fascicularis )
SUCHINDA MALAIVIJITNOND1/C3, CHARIYA LEKPRAYOON2,
NONTIVICH TANDAVANITTJ2, SOMSAK PANHA2, CHEEWAPAP CHEEWATHAM3,
AND YUZURU HAMADA4
1Primate Research Unit, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand
2Center of Excellence in Biodiversity, Department of Biology, Faculty of Science,
Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand
3Laemson National Park, Ranong Province, Thailand
4Section of Morphology, Primate Research Institute, Kyoto University, Inuyama, Japan
In January and March of 2005, we conducted surveys of long-tailed
macaques at Piak Nam Yai Island, Laem Son National Park (91 N 34–350,
981 E2 80), Ranong Province, situated in southern Thailand. Two of the
three troops of long-tailed macaques found on the island were observed
using axe-shaped stones to crack rock oysters, detached gastropods
(Thais tissoti , Petit, 1852), bivalves ( Gafrarium divaricatum , Gmelin,
1791), and swimming crabs ( Thalamita danae , Stimpson, 1858). They
smashed the shells with stones that were held in either the left or right
hand, while using the opposite hand to gather the oyster meat. Some
monkeys used both hands to handle the stones. According to Matsuzawa’s
1996 hierarchical classiﬁcation of tool usage (levels 0–3), the tool usage by
Thai long-tailed macaques could be characterized as either level 1
(cracking rock oysters with stones) or level 2 (cracking drifting mollusks and
crabs with stones by placing them on a rock). Our discovery of stone-tool
usage by Thai long-tailed macaques provides a new point of reference for
discussions regarding the evolution oftool usage and the material culture of
primates. Am. J. Primatol. 69:227–233, 2007.

INTRODUCTION
Long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis) are extensively distributed from
the lower northern and northeastern regions (ca. 161 N) to the southernmost part
(ca. 61 N) of Thailand [Hamada et al., 2004; Malaivijitnond et al., 2005]. To date,

temples. They are provisioned by humans and rarely forage for natural foods.
However, long-tailed macaques are known to be omnivorous and their sources of
nutrition include varieties of fruits, seeds, leaves, ﬂowers, insects, crabs, bivalves,
snails, and other small vertebrates [Fooden, 1995]. Thus, they are considered to
be versatile in their feeding behavior, which may contribute to their ecological
adaptability; however, detailed studies on this subject have not been carried out.
Similarly, chimpanzees and capuchins are known to show versatile feeding
behaviors, such as using tools for termite-ﬁshing and nut-cracking [Biro et al.,
2003; Fragaszy et al., 2004; Goodall, 1964]. The use of tools to obtain food has not
been reported for macaques, with the exception of food-processing behaviors, such
as potato-washing by Japanese macaques [Kawai, 1965]. These behaviors have
also been studied in detail from the viewpoint of object-handling. Recently,
Hayashi et al. [2005] emphasized that only great apes and capuchins frequently
exhibit behaviors related to object-object combinations, while other primates,
such as lemurs and macaques, do not exhibit such behaviors. However, in a once-
neglected report, Carpenter [1887] described tool usage in Andaman sea islet-
dwelling long-tailed macaques (i.e., using stones to crack oysters to obtain meat).
We recently discovered a similar behavior in long-tailed macaques, which also
inhabit the Andaman sea islets in Thailand.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study Sites and Observations
In the months following the tsunami disaster on 26 December 2004,
Chulalongkorn University, Thailand, conducted surveys on the impact of the
tsunami on the fauna and ﬂora in affected areas along the Andaman coast. A
survey of primates (mainly of long-tailed macaques) was included in this project
and involved various aspects, such as population, ecology, and foraging behavior.
Three national parks (NPs) situated along the Andaman sea coast of southern
Thailand (Sirinat NP in Phuket Province, Khao Lam Pi-Hat Thai Mueang NP in
Phang Nga Province, and Laem Son NP in Ranong Province) were chosen as the
study sites. We conducted preliminary surveys in those three NPs in January
2005 and found that the populations of long-tailed macaques had survived the
tsunami. We unintentionally discovered two adult female long-tailed macaques
using objects to crack mollusk shells on Piak Nam Yai Island at Laem Son NP.
Unfortunately, we could not perform a detailed observation because we observed
the monkeys from a long distance ( 450 m), while standing on a boat using
binoculars and a video camera. In addition, the monkeys appeared to be
frightened by the presence of humans on the island and ﬂed rapidly into the
forest. However, we found evidence in the form of pieces of oysters along with
some axe-shape stones resting on the rocks when we landed on the island.
Thereafter, in March 2005, we carried out a ﬁeld observation at Piak Nam Yai
Island and neighboring islands (Piak Nam Noi and Thao Islands), where we were
able to observe the monkeys from a close distance (approximately 15 m) while
standing on a small boat using binoculars and a video camera. During our journey
in January and March 2005, we found long-tailed macaques performing such
foraging behavior only on Piak Nam Yai Island. An intensive survey was later
conducted at Piak Nam Yai Island (ca. 5.2 km
2, peak height of 205 m, 91 N 34–350,
981 E2 8 0). We landed on the island and surveyed the areas while the monkeys
played and groomed after foraging.

Interviews With Local People and NP Rangers
We interviewed rangers of the NPs, as well as local people regarding the
habits of long-tailed macaques on these islands. In addition, we also conducted
brief surveys in two offshore islands of Ranong City, Pracharatrangsarith Temple
(91 N5 7

RESULTS
Feeding Behavior
We observed long-tailed macaques foraging along the rocky intertidal
coastline and sandy shore, which is exposed during periods of low tides. Monkeys
at Piak Nam Yai Island were observed to persistently forage for seafood, such as
oysters and especially crabs, which are found throughout the island on both rocky
and sandy shores. We observed one male monkey standing, peering into the water
and presumably looking for crabs. In addition to the oysters and crabs, they also
ate wild ﬁgs and other fruits and leaves. Furthermore, one lactating mother
and a juvenile consumed young leaves of Parkia periosa , which were planted by
local people.
Oyster-Cracking Behavior
Of the three troops of long-tailed macaques found on Piak Nam Yai Island,
two troops consisting of 24 and 16 individuals, respectively, were observed using
stone tools to crack shells of mollusks. Seven monkeys in troop 1, which ranged in
age from juvenile to adult, were simultaneously observed performing the cracking
behavior at one site (9 1 N3 5
0 32.60 and 981 E2 80 07.80). They smashed the rock
oysters rapidly, four to ﬁve times, with an object held in either the left or right
hand, while using the opposite hand to gather the oyster meat. They held stones
(in a ‘‘power grip’’) and efﬁciently smashed shells with the edge of the stones
(Fig. 1a). They were also observed to carry their hand-held stones when they
moved to other hunting locations.
Around the same time, at a site approximately 2 km from troop 1 (9 1 N3 4
0
25.20 and 98 1–280 05.80 E), members of troop 2 (one adult male and one aged
female) performed similar cracking behavior. The latter used both hands to
handle the stone and hit the ﬂatter surface (Fig. 1b).
After we landed on the island to survey the areas, we discovered rocks upon
which the macaques had foraged for oysters. These substrate rocks were
approximately 2–3 m in diameter, and 5–10% of the rocks were covered by
oysters (Saccostrea cucullata, Born 1778); the remaining 90–95% of the area was
covered by rock barnacles. From the presence of emptied oyster shells we inferred
that the monkeys intentionally cracked them opened for food. We also found loose
sandstone, rhyolite, or andesite river pebbles, characterized by oval and ﬂat
shapes, that resembled hand-held axes (hereafter called ‘‘axe stones’’). These
stones had been used by the monkeys and left behind on the substrate stones.
Interestingly, the monkeys used these axe stones to crack the oysters but not the
rock barnacles.
We observed macaques using axe stones of various sizes. Nine axe stones

and weight and diameter measurements were taken. The weights ranged from

After an intensive investigation of the substrate rocks, we found that the
long-tailed macaques used the axe stones to crack not only the oysters attached
to the rocks, but also detached gastropods ( Thais tissoti , Petit, 1852), bivalves
(Gafrarium divaricatum , Gmelin, 1791), and even swimming crabs ( Thalamita
danae, Stimpson, 1858). Pieces of gastropods, bivalves, and swimming crabs,
which showed signs of being smashed by a hard object, were found on the
substrate rocks along with the axe stones resting nearby.
Interviews With Local People and NP Rangers
The local people and rangers of the Laem Son NP reported during interviews
that the monkeys display the oyster-cracking behavior throughout the year and
have been thus observed for over 20 years on both Piak Nam Yai and Thao
Islands.
We also interviewed the local people on offshore islands near Ranong City
(91 N5 4
0,9 81 E3 40), though we were unable to locate the macaques during the
surveys. Local people at Baan Koh Lao Island reported that long-tailed macaques
use discarded oyster shells to crack open other oysters attached to the roots and
lower branches of the mangrove trees. At the Pracharatrangsarith Temple,
located at the fringe of a mangrove forest, the local people also told us that long-
tailed macaques used to crack oysters using oyster shells, but they ceased that
behavior after they were provisioned by humans.
DISCUSSION
This is the ﬁrst report of oyster-cracking behavior using stone tools in Thai
long-tailed macaques. Similar behaviors were observed over 100 years ago in long-
tailed macaques inhabiting the islands of the Mergui Archipelago, southern
Myanmar [Carpenter, 1887], which is in close proximity to our study areas. That
brief report has long been neglected, and, in the past, human primates were
believed to differ from nonhuman primates on the basis of language, culture, and
tool usage. However, the distinction between humans and other primates by tool
usage was deﬁnitely contradicted by Jane Goodall’s [1964] discovery of termite-
ﬁshing behavior in wild chimpanzees in Gombe, East Africa. Apart from
chimpanzees, tool usage has been observed in other hominoids (e.g., gorillas
and orangutans [Mulcahy et al., 2005; van Schaik & Knott, 2001]) and
platyrrhines (e.g., capuchins [Fernandes, 1991; Fragaszy et al., 2004; Visalberghi,
1987]). Similarly to chimpanzees, wild capuchins use stones to crack the
tough shells of nuts [Fragaszy et al., 2004], and use a piece of oyster shell to
crack oysters [Fernandes, 1991]. Our discovery provides a new point of
reference for discussions regarding the evolution of tool usage and the material
culture of primates. The discovery of stone-tool usage by wild long-tailed
macaques, a species belonging to the Cercopithecoidea [Groves, 2001], and to
Anthropoidea, contributes valuable insight into the evolution of primate
intelligence.
Matsuzawa [1996] hierarchically classiﬁed tool-using behaviors in chimpan-
zees into four levels (levels 0–3). The most complicated level (level 3) was observed
only in chimpanzees that involved meta-tool-usage in nut cracking. The nut-
cracking behavior of capuchins was classiﬁed as level 2 [Fragaszy et al., 2004;
Visalberghi, 1987]. With regard to this hierarchy, the tool usage by Thai long-
tailed macaques could be characterized as level 1 or level 2. The level-1 behavior
(two detached objects) is the use of axe stones (ﬁrst detached object) to crack the
rock oysters (second detached object). The level-2 tool use (three detached objects

Our interviews at Baan Koh Lao Island and Pracharatrangsarith Temple
indicated that long-tailed macaques living in mangrove forests would use oyster
shells to crack other oysters instead of using stones, which were not available. Of
all primates in Thailand, the long-tailed macaque is considered the only
permanent resident of the mangrove forests [Lekagul & McNeely, 1988]. The
versatility of the feeding behavior of long-tailed macaques, including the ﬂexible
use of hard objects to obtain food, contributes to their adaptability in such
habitat.
We observed that the monkeys performing the cracking behavior ranged in
age from juveniles to adults. Compared to the potato-washing behavior observed
in Japanese macaques on Koshima Island, Japan [Kawai, 1965], stone-tool usage
is perhaps a more complex form of tool use, since it involves a sequence of actions
in two spatial relations. Many questions regarding the acquisition and
dissemination of stone-tool usage in Thai long-tailed macaques should be
thoroughly addressed, and we should make an effort to preserve their precious
habitat.
