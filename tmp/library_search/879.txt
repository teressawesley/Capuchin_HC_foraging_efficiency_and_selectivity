Abstract
Tufted capuchin monkeys ( Sapajus spp.) are the only Neotropical Primates that
regularly use tools in the wild, but only one population of bearded capuchin
monkeys ( Sapajus libidinosus ) is known to habitually use sticks as probes. In this
population, males are typically the onl y sex to use stick tools, something un-
expected, since there are no obvious phy sical constraints, and females do use
stone tools in the wild and sticks in experim ental conditions. We investigated the
development of probe tool use in eight in fants to clarify whether social influ-
ences on learning varied between the s exes, as tool use learning by capuchin
monkeys is a socially biased process. We found that in the first 10 months of age,

males begin to manipulate them at higher frequencies. We examined if social
connections —as opportunities for social learning —could explain this difference
and verified that, on close distance soc ial networks, infant males and females
have similar connections with older male s. However, males observe probe tool
use events more often than females when close to such events. The higher fre-
quency of manipulation of sticks, as well as the higher rates of probe tool use
observation, appear to be the key to understand why only males are probe tool
users in this population. Since there are only male potential models of probe use,
a sex motivational bias could explain the s ex difference in observation; a bias in
observation could explain the d ifferences in manipulation —and manipulation
rates would certainly influence the chances of individual, trial ‐and‐error learning
(a case of “local/stimulus enhancement ”).

1 | INTRODUCTION
Probe tools are known to be used by a few primate species, but this
behavior is not equally distributed within populations, even when
raw material is abundantly available in the environment. Some
chimpanzee communities use sticks to fish for termites (Boesch
et al., 2020; McGrew & Collins, 1985; Sanz et al., 2004) or algae
(Boesch et al., 2016), and to dip for ants (Humle & Matsuzawa, 2002).
A few communities even use a stick tool set to first perforate the
nest and then dip for termites (Sanz et al., 2004) or honey (Boesch
et al., 2009). The chimpanzees from the Fongoli community hunt for
galagos using wooden “spears” to hurt the prey (Pruetz &

Bertolani, 2007; Pruetz et al., 2015), a behavior rarely observed in
other populations. Although apparently ubiquitous in some popula-
tions, probe tool use can have marked differences even between
neighboring groups, like two Ugandan communities, where one
(Kanyawara) uses stick probes to solve an experimental problem,
while the other (Sonso) does not (Gruber et al., 2009). More inter-
estingly, even when the use of sticks by Sonso community was ex-
perimentally facilitated, they still did not present this behavior,
solving the problem with their customary use of leave ‐sponge tools.
Although the use of stick tools is observed in both sexes in
chimpanzees, there is a female bias (as in tool use in general), both
in frequency and in success in the use of tools, in most studied
populations (Boesch & Boesch, 1990;G r u b e re ta l . , 2010;
McGrew, 1979). Moreover, in Gombe, there is a sex difference in
the development of termite ‐fishing, a socially learned skill: young
females learn faster, and their techniques match more that of the
mothers, contrary to males (Lonsdorf, 2005). Interestingly, though,
during their development, male chimpanzees exhibit higher rates
of object play manipulation than females. Females' manipulation,
though, was more diverse and directed to exploration (Koops
et al., 2015). However, not all chimpanzee populations present this
pattern. A study on the ontogen y of termite gathering in chim-
panzees from the Goualougo Triangle showed no significant sex
differences on the acquisition of this behavior by the immatures
(Musgrave et al., 2020).
Wild orangutan populations do not have a broad toolkit, al-
though they perform many complex m anipulations on objects (van
Casteren et al., 2012; van Schaik et al., 2003). Only populations
from Sumatra use stick tools in the wild, mostly to take out seeds
from Neesia sp. protected fruits and to extract honey or social
insects from nests in trees (van Schaik et al., 2003, 2008). There is
a bias in tool use for seed extraction toward females, probably
because the larger males can ac c e s st h ef r u i t sb e f o r et h ep r o -
tective stinging hair has matured. However, there are no sig-
nificant sex differences for honey/insect extraction (van Schaik
et al., 2008). During the development of manipulatory skills, im-
mature orangutans often perform peering (attentive close range
watching) at adults in contexts where social learning would be
expected, and the frequency is co rrelated with the complexity of
the task, showing that observational social learning over several
years is a critical factor for the acquisition of subsistence skills
(Schuppli et al., 2016).
The case of tufted capuchin monkeys is more perplexing. Al-
though the use of stone tools is observed in most studied savannah
populations (Falótico et al., 2018; Ottoni & Izar, 2008), the use of
stick as tools is, to date, only known to be customary (Whiten
et al., 1999) in the Sapajus libidinosus population from Serra da
Capivara National Park (SCNP), where these monkeys use sticks to
probe for prey, honey or water (Falótico & Ottoni, 2014; Mannu &
Ottoni, 2009; Moura & Lee, 2004), defensive/exploratory poking
(Falótico et al., 2018), and self ‐care (Haslam & Falótico, 2015
). Other
than that, stick tools have only been reported anecdotally in a group
of Sapajus flavius (Souto et al., 2011) and one of Sapajus nigritus
(Aguiar et al., 2014), and is absent on at least one long term research
site of Sapajus libidinosus , Fazenda Boa Vista (Cardoso &
Ottoni, 2016; Ottoni & Izar, 2008).
For stone tool use, as in other primates, there is a slight sex bias,
in the capuchin case towards males, although females do use stone
tools customarily. A puzzling fact, however, is that adult females were
never observed using probe tools for foraging at SCNP (Falótico &
Ottoni, 2014). This is not a simple bias; it is a near absence of this
behavior among adult females in that population. Nonetheless, fe-
males are known to learn to use probe tools in captivity and urban
parks when offered a problem apparatus that needs probing to be
solved (Cardoso & Ottoni, 2016; Manrique et al., 2011; Visalberghi &
Limongelli, 1994).
Some behavioral differences can be linked to sexual dimorphism.
Capuchin monkeys' groups that use stones to crack open hard re-
sistant nuts show a marked efficiency difference between sexes,
because males are, on average, bigger, they can lift heavier stones
and thus open those nuts more efficiently (Spagnoletti et al., 2011).
The probe sticks, however, do not pose this physical constrain. One
of the possibilities is that the difference is due to a learning bias
during ontogeny, such as some individuals or one sex exhibiting more
interest in observing or manipulating such tools.
Tool use and object manipulation are behaviors that, in primates,
have a high degree of social learning influence (Whiten & van de
Waal, 2018). During ontogeny, the influence of other individuals can
affect the opportunities of the immatures to interact with certain
objects or resources (Coelho et al., 2015; Ottoni et al., 2005).
Tufted capuchin monkeys are known to use social information
when learning to use stone tools (Coelho et al., 2015; Fragaszy
et al., 2017). The young monkeys take several months to learn how
to proficiently position the resource on top of an anvil and use a
loose stone as hammer to hit and open seeds or nuts. The main
learning processes capuchins use in this case appear to be local and
stimulus enhancement, that is, when a naïve individual is drawn to a
location or to objects by the actions of conspecifics (Fragaszy
et al., 2013). The high tolerance capuchin monkeys exhibit, specially
towards young monkeys (Fragaszy & Visalberghi, 2004) facilitates
this process. It allows youngsters to come closer and observe the
objects used as tools, manipulate them, and eventually scrounge for
food leftovers—a potential reinforcer in the learning process (Ottoni
et al., 2005). However, a recent study on interindividual distances
between capuchins performing extractive foraging (including stone
tool use for palm nut ‐cracking) did not find a correlation between
proficient individuals and skilled versus unskilled partners' distance,
as a measure of opportunity for observation of the activity (Howard
et al., 2018). The authors, however, did not specifically analysed
stone tool use and they point that nut cracking in their study group is
a very conspicuous activity, and that close visual observation may
not be so important for observation learning of this behavior, as it
could be for more cryptic foraging activities.

identification and use of discarded tools, because, differently from
chimpanzees' termite fishing sites (Pascual ‐Garrido, 2017), there is

2 | METHODS
2.1 | Study group
We studied a S. libidinosus group named Pedra Furada on the south
border of SCNP, northeast of Brazil.
During the study (March 2014 to October 2015) the group
ranged from 34 to 38 individuals, which we categorized according to
the following age classes: infant (0 –1.5 year); juvenile 1 (1.5 –3 years);
juvenile 2 (3 –5); adult/subadult (>5 years).
We analysed data of eight infants from the ages 6 –101 weeks
(1.5 months –1.9 years). We split the data within eight age blocks,
with 12 weeks each (Table 2). We collected data from most months,
but there was a gap between January 2015 and April 2015, a period
that the research assistant had difficulty to find and follow the group.
2.2 | Focal sampling
To record the manipulatory behavior, we followed the study group
for 20 months, registering by focal animal sampling (Martin &
Bateson, 2007) the activity of all individuals of the group. To orga-
nize the focal sampling, we used focal lists to sample three times
each infant and one time every other individual per list. Each list
included all individuals in the group and defined the order that the
focal sampling was done. The order was semirandom to avoid se-
quential sampling of infants (we first sorted randomly the infants,
them the full group, and, finally, the infants again). Each list took
between 2 and 3 weeks to complete, depending on the amount of
time we had contact with the group. Each focal sampling lasted
10 min, and we did a 5 min interval between focals. All registers were
done with handheld camcorders (Canon Vixia HF R52). The video
files were transcribed by Carolina Q. Bueno on Noldus Observer
XT12.5 following a previously tested ethogram (Table S 1). To access
coder reliability, Tiago Falótico coded 15 random focal samples and
compared them to the ones coded by Carolina Q. Bueno. The Cohen
κ was above 0.80 for all comparisons.
To test P1 and P2, the general manipulation frequencies of
sticks and stones was coded for all infants on the videos of the
animal focal sampling. We used the total count of manipulation
events (manipulation of a single object from the moment the
individual grabbed it until it wa s dropped) over the time sampled
for the monkey on each block as manipulation rate. To test P3 we
counted the “Probing with stick ” events within the stick manip-
ulation behavior and used this nu m b e ro v e rt h et i m es a m p l e df o r
each monkey as probe tool use rate ( “Probing with stick ” events
occur during “stick manipulation ” events but are counted
independently).
2.3 | Scan sampling
As social proximity data, we recorded all the individuals within 1 m of
the focal animal at each completed minute of the focal sampling (10
scans per focal). This register was done recording on camera audio
the identity of each individual within 1 m of the focal monkey, or
reporting no one was in that range.
The audio of the scans was transcribed by TF on matrices (one
matrix per focal list), using columns as individuals and each line as

2.4 | All occurrences sampling
We registered all tool use events observed in and outside the
focal sampling, by all individuals in the group. We video recorded
the whole episodes until the capture of the resource or the
abandonment of the site. To test P5, we coded the videos to
identify the user, all the individuals nearby (<0.5 m), and how
many of those nearby individuals were peering at the probe tool
user (attentive close range watching for at least 5 s to the probe
tool being used). We counted all the events that had observers or
not, and, for the former, we calculated the frequencies for males
and females.

variable. We used the number of manipulation episodes (de-
limited by the behaviors “Grab/hold object ” and “Drop object ”)b y

was used as fixed effect (predictor).

3 | RESULTS
3.1 | Object manipulation
Stick manipulation was present since the first age block for two
infants (one male and one female), and the frequency increased for
males after block age 66 –77, when they also begin to increase the
use of those sticks as probe tools (see Figure 1), remaining low for
females and for one of the males (Table 3).
Stone manipulation was present since the first age block for two
infants (one male and one female), and frequency was highly variable
(Table 4). To the exception of Verruga, that showed a sharp increase
of stone manipulation in the last age block, no apparent pattern
emerged as observed in the case of stick manipulation.
The GEE analysis involved a regression of the infants' block age
manipulation rate, showing sex was a significant predictor for both
rates of manipulation (Table 5).
The estimated means shows that males manipulated more fre-
quently sticks and stones than females (Table 6), supporting P1 and
P2 (males will manipulate stones and sticks more frequently than
females since infancy).
3.2 | Tool use
The use of stone tools was rare for the infants in this study. We
registered 93 events of stone tool use. Probe tool use (events of
probing/hour) was much more frequent ( N = 286), being first ob-
served in two infant males in the 42 –53‐week age block and be-
coming more frequent in the 78 –89‐weeks age block (Figure 1). Only
one female was observed probing, but with very low frequency
compared to the males.
3.3 | Social networks
To test P4 (which predicted immature males would have more
connections than immature females with older males), we coun-
ted the connections of “older males ” (adults, subadults, and ju-
veniles 2) and “immatures ” (infants/juveniles 1) for each social
network ( N = 27). Comparing the connections between immature
males and females, we found that immature males had more
connections to “older males, ” on average, than immature females
(Males, Avg = 7.1, SD =2 . 5 ; F e m a l e s , A v g=6 . 2 ,SD =2 . 7 ) . H o w -

immatures was not a significant predictor for the number of
connections, thus not supporting P4.

4 | DISCUSSION
Probe tool use by wild capuchin monkeys in SCNP is a highly sex ‐
biased behavior, being observed almost only in males. This was un-
expected, as females are proficient tool user in other situations and
there are no physical constrains to the use of probe tools. We ex-
amined factors that could be related to this difference: different
manipulation rates of stones and sticks, social opportunities to ob-
serve probe tool use, and the motivation in observing this behavior.
Our results show a significant difference between immature
males and females during their development regarding stick and
stone manipulation, and probe tool use.
4.1 | Immature males will manipulate objects
more frequently than immature females
The predictions for this hypothesis were that we would find a dif-
ference in manipulation of sticks and stones (P1 and P2) between
immature males and females once they begin to manipulate objects.

probing, but with a very low frequency compared to the males.
These results show that young females exhibit lower manipula-
tion frequencies of both stones and sticks, but they do use stones as
tools as adults, so lower early manipulation rates do not seem to fully
explain the lack of probe tool use later in life, although it could be a
factor involved. Female capuchins are known to present a lower
stone tool use rate, particularly when using large stones (Falótico &
Ottoni, 2016; Spagnoletti et al., 2011), but it seems related to body
mass, strength, and tool weight, so it does not provide a good ex-
planation for the sex bias observed in probe tool use in this popu-
lation. The lower early stone manipulation rates did not prevent
females from being proficient stone tool users, as previous studies in
the area showed (Falótico & Ottoni, 2016; Falótico et al., 2017;
Mannu & Ottoni, 2009; Moura & Lee, 2004).
The absence of stone tool use among the infants in the study was
expected. Other studies on the same topic showed that infant ca-
puchins take a long time (2–3 years) to begin to use stones proficiently
as tools (Resende et al., 2008), longer than the immatures' age in this
study. The use of sticks as tools starts earlier than that of stones, maybe
because it takes less interactions with objects to be effective. Using
stones to crack open seeds/nuts takes the coordination of three objects
(anvil, hammer, and target), but a probe only requires the coordination
of two (probe and target). Furthermore, the effective use of percussive
tools requires greater strength/body mass.
4.2 | Immature males have more social
opportunities to observe probe tool use by proficient
individuals (older males)
Tool use learning is socially biased in capuchins. During the devel-
opment of stone tool use, stimulus and local enhancement seem to
be the main processes that direct the attention of youngsters to the
objects that are manipulated and later used as tools after an in-
dividual learning process. This same situation also provides scroun-
ging opportunities that potentially reinforce the behavior (Coelho
et al., 2015; Ottoni et al., 2005).
Social observation opportunities are, consequently, important in
the development of tool use. However, differently from nut cracking
and most other stone tool use behaviors, probe tool use is less noisy
and does not generate long ‐lasting tool use sites with tools and food
leftovers, making probe use learning even more dependent on the
direct observation of the tool use event itself.
Our prediction P4 was that young males would be more fre-
quently close to older males, and, thus, have more opportunities to
observe and learn about probe tool use than young females, resulting
in the strong sex bias in this behavior that we observe in this po-
pulation. However, the results showed that the immatures of both
sexes had similar spatial connections to adult males, indicating that
young females do not have very different network connections than
males. In other words, older males (the models for probing tool use
behavior) were similarly present in infant males' and females' social
networks.
4.3 | Males observe more probe tool use than
females
Our last hypothesis was that males would observe more probe tool
use when near an event of such behavior and, doing so, have more
direct interaction with the probing and increasing the chances of
learning this behavior, their attention drawn to the sticks and the
target locations of their use.
Prediction 5 (males observing probe tool use when near such
events more often than females in the same situation), was supported
by the data. While males close to probe tool use events were usually
observing the probing, females were seldom seen observing, and that
was evident not only in the data from the immatures (Table 10), but
also in the analysis including the adults as well (Table 9). Although we
cannot say for sure that this is the primary reason for the sex bias
observed later in life, our results present a strong evidence that moti-
vation (or the lack of) to observe a b ehavior can lead to different re-
pertoires between sex classes in capuchins.
Although males are more manipulative toward sticks and stones
than females, this difference may be potentialized, in the case of
sticks, by the bias caused by the males' higher frequency of probe
tool use peering. Our tentative explanation is that, because of this
situation, females end up not manipulating sticks as much in foraging
contexts and, not doing so, never learn to use probes. This
“motivation‐based” learning bias could be the cause this behavioral
tradition to remain restricted to the males in this population, al-
though females are capable of using sticks as probe tools. Our study
is limited by the small sample, so it remains an open question whe-
ther, in case other capuchin populations were found to exhibit cus-
tomary probe tool use, the same bias would be observed.

Our results also lead to new questions; the first regards the
motivation for males to observe probe tool use. Or, alternatively,
why females are not so interested in it.
A simple and parsimonious explanation would be the existence
of a general sex ‐biased attentional preference in capuchin monkeys.
An experimental study about preferences on visual attention de-
monstrated that each sex paid more attention to pictures of same
sex (Lonsdorf et al., 2019). Maybe the same happens in capuchin
monkeys' observation of conspecifics' behavior (including probe tool
use or manipulatory behaviors) —their attention being more driven to
same sex targets.
Other explanations could involve an observation bias specifically
related to manipulation and tool use behaviors. Probe tool use is
much less rewarding than stone tool use. While foraging using stones
for cracking and digging in SCNP usually yields more than 70% of
success in obtaining food (Falótico & Ottoni, 2016; Falótico
et al., 2017), the use of probes only returns food in around 20% –25%
of the cases, for adults, and less than 5% for juveniles (Falótico &
Ottoni, 2014). Females may be more driven for nutritional con-
sequences, not being so interested in a behavior that does not return
food very often. Moreover, differently from stone tool use, probe use
seldom produces food remains that can be scrounged —implying less
attraction to the tool use site and to the tools, which amounts to
lesser effects of local and stimulus enhancement, particularly if, in
the case for the females, the motivation is more food ‐related.
Another explanation could be related to sex differences in diet.
However, male and female capuchins eat the same resources and
prey on the same animals, although with different frequencies. In a
study about diet in S. libidinosus , although the food items consumed
were the same, it was found that females ate more invertebrates and
roots than males, and males consumed more palm nuts than females
(Peternelli‐dos‐Santos, 2015).
Future research to answer these questions should look for ob-
servational sex biases on stone tool use and object manipulation
behaviors. If females peer at stone tool use as much as males,
especially on tools that produce few leftovers, it could indicate that
stone tools per se are more attractive to females than the probes.
Otherwise, if females are also less attentive to stone tool use events,
this could suggest that other factors, such as the availability of
leftovers, attract them to tool use sites where they end up learning
how to use those objects as tools.
Our tentative explanation for the observed patterns is that the
more frequent observation, by the immature males, of probe use
events performed by older (male) individuals, does not lead to the
direct copy of these behaviors (which would characterize “imitative”
learning), but elicits more frequent and intensive manipulation of
sticks ( “stimulus enhancement ”), which creates more opportunities
for the individual learning of tool ‐aided probing.
