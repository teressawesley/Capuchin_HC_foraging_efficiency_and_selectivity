Abstract: The close relationship between hermit crabs` ﬁtness and the size and species of gastropod shells they occu-
py is largely based on studies of young and adult crabs. When hermit crabs settle as megalopae, they too require a shell, 
but there are few studies of shell selection and use by this early life stage. We examined shell selection by megalopae 
and early juveniles of the hermit crab Pagurus ﬁlholi for three gastropod shell species, Nassarius fraterculus, Batillaria 
cumingii, and Homalopoma sangarense, all common in the study area and used by young and adult P . ﬁlholi. The early 
crab life stages did not prefer any shell species. Shell species had no signiﬁcant eﬀect on the survival of megalopae or 
early juveniles that were exposed to high water temperatures, nor on crab growth rates or vulnerability to predators. 
Under desiccating conditions, megalopae and early juveniles had signiﬁcantly lower survival in Homalopoma shells 
than in Batillaria  shells. These results suggest that variation in ﬁtness across shell species has not selected for shell 
species preferences by early life stages of P . ﬁlholi perhaps explaining why they occupy shell species more equitably 
compared to later life stages for which ﬁtness varies with the species of shell they occupy.
Key words: ﬁtness, magalopae, ontogenetic shift, shell availability, shell preference
Introduction
The gastropod shells hermit crabs occupy help protect 
them from predation and reduce crab mortality from des-
iccation and exposure to high temperatures (e.g., Hazlett 
1981, Yoshino 2021); crabs cannot survive without such 
shells. Hermit crabs also use gastropod shells as oviposi -
tion sites in which they incubate eggs attached to pleopods. 
The size and species of gastropod shell that crabs occupy 
aﬀects crab growth, fecundity, and survival. For example, 
crabs in smaller or heavier shells have lower growth rates 
than those in larger or lighter shells (Fotheringham 1976a, 
Bertness 1981a, Wada 2000, Yoshino & Goshima 2001). 
The volume of space inside a shell sets the upper limit 
on the number of eggs that a crab can incubate (Wait & 
Schoeman 2012); occupants with smaller or heavier shells 
have fewer eggs than those with larger or lighter shells 
(Fotheringham 1976, Bertness 1981a, Yoshino & Goshima 
2001). Such relationships indicate that crab ﬁtness is close-
ly related to the features of the shells they occupy includ-
ing size and shell species.
Almost all studies on shell use by hermit crabs have 
been carried out on young and adult crabs. Few studies 
have been conducted on megalopae, the life stage at which 
crabs ﬁrst need a shell. Hermit crab megalopae ﬁnd shells, 
occupy them, and then metamorphose to the ﬁrst crab 
stage (Worcester & Gaines 1997, Oba & Goshima 2004). It 
is not known whether the ﬁtness of megalopae varies with 
shell features and megalopae therefore select certain shells 
over others. Alternatively, megalopae may be relatively un-
selective because they need to ﬁnd a shell quickly and 
they occupy the shell only brieﬂy before they molt, thus 
limiting their exposure to predators and other sources of 
mortality while in their ﬁrst shell (e.g., Harvey 1996). The 
present study examines the selectivity for the ﬁrst shell 
that megalopae and ﬁrst crab instars use. We determined 
which shell species and sizes megalopae and early juve -
niles of the hermit crab selected in laboratory experiments 
and in ﬁeld observations, and examined the inﬂuence of

Materials and Methods
The hermit crab Pagurus ﬁlholi  was sampled on a ﬂat 
intertidal rocky shore at Kattoshi located at the south -
west side of Hakodate Bay, Hokkaido, Japan (41°44′34″N, 
140°36′08″E). At this location, this species of hermit crab 
breeds from February to August, primarily from March 
to July (Goshima et al. 1998), and settlement of the mega -
lopa stage occurs from August to November, primarily in 
August and September (Oba & Goshima 2004). Over 90% 
of juvenile and young crabs (less than 1.5 mm in shield 
length) are found in the shells of the gastropods Nassarius 
fraterculus, Batillaria cumingii , and Homalopoma sanga -
rense (Ohmori et al. 1995; Yoshino et al. 2001; Oba & Gos-
hima 2004) (hereafter we will use only the generic name 
for these shell species). These gastropods are abundant at 
Kattoshi (Ohmori et al. 1995; Yoshino et al. 2001) and we 
used their shells in the following experiments.
Ovigerous females of the hermit crab were collected at 
Kattoshi on May 2002 and brought to the laboratory. Each 
ovigerous female was held in a 120-mL plastic bottle with 
seawater at room temperature (approximately 18–25°C) un-
til the female released her zoeae larvae. The larvae from 
20 females were pooled in a 400-mL plastic container with 
seawater and reared in an incubator set at a constant tem -
perature of 18°C until the megalopal stage. Zoeae were 
fed nauplius larvae of Artemia salina , and the seawater in 
the container was changed daily. Megalopae obtained from 
this rearing procedure were used in the following experi-
ments. We used each megalopa only once.
Shell size preference test
We prepared many empty shells of the three species col-
lected at Kattoshi. Shell widths ranged from 1.7 to 4.3 mm 
for Nassarius, 1.5–4.3 mm for Batillaria, and 2.0–5.0 mm 
for Homalopoma. The shells were divided into 10 size 
classes with 0.26 mm interval for Nassarius, 0.28 mm in -
terval for Batillaria, and 0.30 mm interval for Homalopo-
ma. Forty shells of each species, 4 in each of 10 size class-
es, were prepared for the shell size preference test. One 
megalopa and the 40 shells of one species were placed into 
a 400-mL experimental container in an incubator set at a 
constant temperature of 18°C. Megalopae were observed 
until they selected a shell and metamorphosed to the ﬁrst 
crab stage. The size of the shell used by the megalopa 
when it molted to the ﬁrst crab was designated as the pre-
ferred size. This test was repeated 25 times for each shell 
species. During the experiment, no food was provided be-
cause megalopae of Pagurus do not feed (Dawirs 1981). 
The water in the container was not changed to avoid any 
disturbance. For each shell species we ﬁtted the sizes of 
the 25 selected shells to a normal distribution and then de-
termined the preferred size range by excluding the smallest 
30% and largest 30% shells from the curve.
Shell species preference test
We determined whether megalopae preferred a species 
of shell by conducting pairwise tests using two shell spe -
cies at a time. The tests were conducted in 400-mL con -
tainers in an incubator at 18 °C. We placed one megalopa 
into the container with seawater and 10 shells of the pre-
ferred sizes, ﬁve each from two of the three species. We 
recorded which shell species was occupied when the larva 
metamorphosed to the ﬁrst crab stage. This experiment 
included 30 replicate trials for each pairwise shell species 
combination. Megalopae were not fed during the experi -
ment and the water was not changed.
Growth experiment
We conducted experiments to determine whether mega-
lopae grow at diﬀerent rates in shells of the three species. 
We placed one megalopa, 16 shells of the preferred size 
and a few slightly larger shells into a 120-mL plastic bottle 
with seawater in an incubator set at a constant temperature 
of 18°C. Early juveniles were fed an artiﬁcial diet for ju-
venile ﬁsh (Chubu Shiryo Co. Ltd., Nagoya), and seawater 
was changed every day. The experiment was continued 
until the megalopa metamorphosed and molted into the 
third crab stage. We recorded the days from megalopa to 
the third crab stage for 20 individuals for each of the three 
shell species and we measured the shield length of the third 
crabs using a micrometer after ﬁxing them in 5% formalin.
Resistance to desiccation
We conducted an experiment to determine the ability of 
the ﬁrst crab instar that used diﬀerent shell species to resist 
desiccation. Each megalopa was provided one of the three 
shell species and allowed to metamorphose to the ﬁrst crab 
stage. From 8 to 10 ﬁrst crab instars were used with each 
shell species. The ﬁrst crabs with each shell species were 
placed on wet sand (grain size, approximately 1 mm in 
diameter) in a plastic container for 6 h in an incubator set 
at a constant temperature of 18°C. After 6 h, we examined 
each crab and determined whether it was dead or alive. We 
conducted ﬁve replicate trials for crabs in each of the three 
shell species. Megalopae leave their shells when stressed 
making it impossible to run such tests using this earlier life 
stage.
Resistance to high temperature
We conducted an experiment to measure survival of ﬁrst 
crab instars that used diﬀerent shell species when exposed 
to an elevated water temperature. Each megalopa was pro -
vided one of the three shell species and allowed to meta -
morphose to the ﬁrst crab stage. Ten ﬁrst crabs in each shell 
species were placed into a 400-mL plastic container with 
seawater at 37°C, an extreme high temperature observed in

Resistance to crushing
We measured the resistance of each shell species to 
simulated crushing by predators. Each empty shell of the 
preferred size range was ﬁxed by a bench vise and the vise 
was tightened until the shell cracked. The force required to 
crack the shell was measured using a force gauge (CNYST, 
YST-200, China) connected to the vise. We continued to 
tighten the vise until the shell cracked again and measured 
the force. The two measurements were averaged for each 
shell because the ﬁrst crack by crushing predators is not 
always fatal to the megalopa. This experiment included 15 
replicate trials for each shell species. This method indirect-
ly assesses the resistance of each shell species to the shell-
crushing predators such as varunid crabs, Gaetice depres -
sus, Hemigrapsus sanguineus , and H. takanoi , which are 
often observed at Kattoshi (Yoshino et al. 2002). These 
predators crush the small shells used by small hermit crabs 
whole. They do not chip shells along the margins as they 
do for the shells used by larger hermit crabs.
Shells used by megalopae in the ﬁeld
We examined shell occupancy by megalopae and early 
postsettlement juveniles (ﬁrst crab stage) of P. ﬁlholi at 
Kattoshi. Quadrat sampling was carried out once a month 
from December 1999 to December 2000. Twenty quad -
rats (20 cmʷ20 cm) were placed on the intertidal ﬂat each 
month and all gastropod shells in each quadrat were col-
lected. Samples were sorted under a microscope into meg -
alopae, early postsettlement individuals, and vacant shells 
(see for details, Oba & Goshima 2004). Crab species iden-
tiﬁcation is diﬃcult at the megalopal stage; thus, we reared 
them separately in the laboratory until they molted to the 
ﬁrst crab stage, enabling us to identify crabs at a species 
level among several Pagurus  species present at the study 
site (Quintana & Iwata 1987, Konishi & Quintana 1988, 
Oba et al. 2006). We recorded the shell species used by the 
megalopae and ﬁrst crab instars of P. ﬁlholi and calculated 
the Pielou`s index of equitability (J′) of the occupied shells 
using the following equations:

We also examined the eﬀects of the shell availability and 
shell species preference on the observed shell use pattern 
of the megalopae and ﬁrst crab instars at Kattoshi. The 
number of vacant shells obtained in the quadrat sampling 
from April to July 2000 at Kattoshi was used as the shell 
abundance of each species (Table 1 in Oba & Goshima 
2004). We compared the relative number of shells of each 
species occupied by megalopae and ﬁrst crabs in the Au -

relative abundance of shells available assuming no diﬀer-

Statistical analyses
All statistical analyses were performed by R ver. 4.1.2 
(R Core Team 2021). The proportions of the two select -
ed shell species in the shell species preference test were 
compared using a binomial test. Time from megalopae to 
the third crab stage, shield length of the third crabs, and 
forces exhibited to crack shells were compared among the 
three shell species by one-way ANOVA. The proportions 
of crabs that survived desiccation and the high tempera -
ture were compared among individuals inhabiting diﬀerent 
shell species using quasibinomial logistic regressions to 
cope with overdispersions. If the overall eﬀect was sig -
niﬁcant, then multiple comparisons were performed using 
Tukey contrasts and Type I error corrections following the 
Westfall method using the package lmultcompz. We exam-
ined the eﬀects of the shell availability and shell preference 
on the observed shell use pattern of the megalopae and 
early juveniles at Kattoshi, by comparing the number of 
occupants with the abundance of each shell species. We 
used the Kendall rank correlation and Fisher`s exact prob-
ability test extended for larger than the original 2ʷ2 table.

Discussion
Megalopa did not prefer any gastropod shell species 
among the three that were oﬀered. There were no diﬀer-
ences in tolerance to high seawater temperature, growth 
rates, body length, and risk of vulnerability to predators, 
among the megalopae and early postsettlement juveniles 
inhabiting diﬀerent shell species. Under only the desic -
cation condition, individuals inhabiting Homalopoma  
showed signiﬁcantly lower survivorship than those in -
habiting Batillaria shells. However, they may seldom die 
from desiccation on the shore at Kattoshi because they live 
in the middle and lower intertidal zone (Oba & Goshima 
2004) and are exposed to the air only about 4 h per low 
tide (Japan Meteorological Agency 2023). In addition, 
moist conditions under many stones and rocks provide ref-
uge from desiccation. We found no evidence that megalo-
pae select a shell following strict preferences but rather oc-
cupy shell species in proportion to their availability where 
they settle in the ﬁeld at Kattoshi. Megalopae and ﬁrst crab 
instars occupied shell species more equitably than larger 
and older hermit crabs. Although only 23 individuals from 
the ﬁeld were examined, megalopae and ﬁrst crabs used 
six shell species, while 54 larger and older hermit crabs 
used predominantly two and rarely more species (Table 3). 
The early life stages use shells opportunistically rather 
than selectively as crabs do later in life (Straughan & Gos-
selin 2014).
Opportunism in shell use may be related to the short 
time megalopae use shells. Depending on water tempera -
ture, megalopae of Pagurus molt to the ﬁrst crab from 
about 4 to 10 days (Roberts 1971, Dawirs 1981, Quintana 
& Iwata 1987, Harvey & Colasurdo 1993, Harvey 1996, 
present study). Pagurus megalopae do not feed (Dawirs 
1981, Harvey & Colasurdo 1993, Harvey 1996) and do not 
extend the megalopal stage (Harvey & Colasurdo 1993) 
even if it cannot get a shell (T. Oba, personal observation). 
Megalopae may need to ﬁnd a shell quickly for immedi -
ate protection (any shell of suitable size) without spending

and ﬁnd a shell by tactile cue and enter it at any time dur-

Pagurid megalopae generally have little trouble locating

teristics and behavior probably cause the usage pattern of 
inhabiting various shell species in early life.
We know of only one paper dealing with shell prefer-
ences of megalopae of a hermit crab (Worcester & Gaines 
1997). Megalopae of P. hirsutiusculus  prefer speciﬁc shell 
species, but the eﬀect of shell availability on the shell use 
pattern were not thoroughly explored. We therefore ana-
lyzed the association between the abundance of each shell 
species available and shell species occupied by megalopae 
using data shown in the paper. Shell availability aﬀected 
shell use (Kendall rank correlation coeﬃcient, τ =  0 . 8 6 7 ,  
p = 0.017, n = 6), as we found in this study of P. ﬁlholi.
This study and that of Yoshino et al. (2001) indicate a 
major ontogenetic shift in shell use (with the caveat that 
the exact number of shell species used by the life stages 
in Yoshino`s study is not known because rare species were 
pooled). Pagurus ﬁlholi  megalopae do not prefer some 
shell species over others, in contrast to a clear preference 
in adults (Yoshino et al. 1999). The number of commonly 
used shell species and the evenness index progressively 
decrease with the increase in crab body size. The avail -
ability of suitably sized shells of the diﬀerent species likely 
decreases with crab size. Whereas small shells of all spe -
cies may be relatively abundant, there may be fewer larger 
gastropod species and hence larger empty shells than small 
ones (e.g., Fotheringham 1976b). The evenness index ( J′) 
takes into account the diﬀering number of available shell 
species among size classes. The decrease in this index with 
increasing crab size (Table 3) indicates the dominant use 
of some gastropod species by the medium and large hermit 
crabs under the constraint of decreased shell species avail-
ability.
The most likely driving force for the dominant use of 
a particular species by young and adult hermit crabs is 
shell species preference, which may improve survival and 
reproduction (Yoshino 2021). According to Yoshino et al. 
(2001) and Yoshino (2021), in P. ﬁlholi  at Kattoshi, small 
males primarily use Nassarius and Homalopoma  (Table 
3) because they grow faster in lighter shells and relatively 
quickly obtain the beneﬁts of larger size in intraspeciﬁc 
competition and mating (Wada et al. 1999, Yoshino & Gos-
hima 2002, Yasuda & Koga 2016). Meanwhile, medium 
females also use Batillaria that maintain water to incubate 
eggs within taller spires (Bertness 1981b). These preferenc-
es are peculiarly reﬂected by the higher use of Batillaria in 
females than in males of medium size and the decrease in 
evenness (J′) of medium males (Table 3).
The ontogenetic decrease in the number and diversity of 
inhabited shell species is often observed (Asakura & Kiku-
chi 1984, Ohmori et al. 1995, Yoshino et al. 2001, Pardo et 
al. 2007, Oba et al. 2008, Straughan & Gosselin 2014, but 
an exception in Gherardi et al. 1994). The very brief time 
that megalopae and rapidly growing early crab stages use 
shells should minimize eﬀects of shell species on compo -
nents of ﬁtness; all species may provide suﬃcient tempo -
rary protection. As crabs grow and use their shells for lon-
ger periods variation in other ﬁtness components with shell 
species may begin to select for shell species preferences 
(Yoshino et al. 2001; Yoshino 2022). Such preferences will 
further restrict the number of shell species used under de-
creasing shell species availability with increasing hermit 
crab size, making a decreasing trend in the number and 
diversity of gastropod shell species used with ontogeny. 
This mechanism might explain the observed ontogenetic 
decrease in the diversity of inhabited shell species.
