abstract
The parallel evolution of increased sensorimotor intelligence in humans and capuchins has been linked
to the cognitive and manual demands of seasonal extractive faunivory. This hypothesis is attractive on
theoretical grounds, but it has eluded widespread acceptance due to lack of empirical data. For instance,
the effects of seasonality on the extractive foraging behaviors of capuchins are largely unknown. Here we
report foraging observations on four groups of wild capuchins ( Cebus capucinus ) inhabiting a seasonally
dry tropical forest. We also measured intra-annual variation in temperature, rainfall, and food abun-
dance. We found that the exploitation of embedded or mechanically protected invertebrates was
concentrated during periods of fruit scarcity. Such a pattern suggests that embedded insects are best
characterized as a fallback food for capuchins. We discuss the implications of seasonal extractive fau-
nivory for the evolution of sensorimotor intelligence (SMI) in capuchins and hominins and suggest that
the suite of features associated with SMI, including increased manual dexterity, tool use, and innovative
problem solving are cognitive adaptations among frugivores that fall back seasonally on extractable
foods. The selective pressures acting on SMI are predicted to be strongest among primates living in the
most seasonal environments. This model is proffered to explain the differences in tool use between
capuchin lineages, and SMI as an adaptation to extractive foraging is suggested to play an important role
in hominin evolution.

Introduction
Brain size, sensorimotor intelligence and extractive foraging
Adaptive hypotheses for the evolution of relatively large brains
among primates have fueled decades of debate ( Jolly, 1966; Jerison,
1973; Parker and Gibson, 1977; Clutton-Brock and Harvey, 1980;
Milton, 1981; Martin, 1984; Cheney et al., 1986; Gibson, 1986; Byrne
and Whiten, 1989; Sawaguchi, 1990; Aiello and Wheeler, 1995;
Dunbar, 1998; Byrne, 2000; Deaner et al., 2000; Tomasello, 2000;
Johnson, 2001; Reader and Laland, 2002; Russon and Begun, 2004;
Barton, 2006; Dunbar and Shultz, 2007; Herrmann et al., 2007;
Cosmides et al., 2010; Navarrete et al., 201 1; Barton, 2012 ). An
emerging consensus from this debate is that foraging skills have
played an important role in cognitive evolution (Parker and Gibson,
1977; Reader et al., 201 1; Barton, 2012 ). One particular skill e the
manual extraction of embedded foods from hidden locations or
protective matrices e requires exceptional conceptual abilities and
enhanced motor control of the forelimb. Together, these demands
on the cerebral cortex might have favored a larger brain size; a
concept termed the sensorimotor intelligence hypothesis ( Parker
and Gibson, 1977, 1979).
Sensorimotor intelligence (SMI) describes cognitive functions
that pertain to object eenvironment interactions reliant on sensory
perceptions and motor activities. The concept of SMI was developed
initially to study the cognitive development of children ( Piaget,
1952, 1954) and has been subsequently expanded upon, drawn
from, and revised to explain processes of cognitive development in
humans, nonhuman primates and other animals (e.g., Siegler and
Ellis, 1996; Thelen et al., 2001 ; invited commentaries thereof;
Schafer et al., 2006; Vilarroya, 2012 ). Contributing to this work,
Parker and Gibson (1977 , 1979) used an ecological sensorimotor
framework to suggest that persistent and generative object
manipulation has evolved repeatedly among primates as an

object manipulation is additionally associated with problem solv-

effective extractive foraging.
Embedded foods eaten by humans and other primates include
nuts ( Visalberghi et al., 2007 ), tubers ( Hernandez-Aguilar et al.,
2007; Marshall et al., 2009 ) and, perhaps most commonly, adult
and larval invertebrates that are embedded within protective exo-
skeletons or that reside in mounds, hives, thorns, crevices,
branches, under bark, or furrowed in leaves ( McGrew, 1983;
Erickson, 1995; Fragaszy and Boinski, 1995; Isbell and Young,
2007; Melin et al., 2007, 2010 ). Invertebrates provide a valuable
source of protein and fat, which are essential and often limiting
resources to primates ( Conklin-Brittain et al., 1998; Milton, 1999;
Marroig and Cheverud, 2005; McCabe and Fedigan, 2007;
Deblauwe and Janssens, 2008; Valenta and Melin, 2012; Isbell
et al., 2013; Raubenheimer and Rothman, 2013; Rothman et al.,
2014; Vogel et al., 2012 ). Increased handling time as well as
specialized anatomy, behaviors, or tools are required for removing
embedded prey from their enclosures. Extractive foraging is a far
less common mode of faunivory than opportunistic gleaning
among primates; however, it is well known among humans and
great apes, which are accomplished at making and using tools for
this purpose ( Dufour, 1987; van Schaik et al., 1999; Backwell and
d’Errico, 2001; Solavan et al., 2006; Sanz et al., 2009 ). Other pri-
mates known to successfully ﬁnd and extract hidden invertebrates
with regularity are aye-ayes ( Daubetonia) and capuchins ( Cebus,
Sapajus). The former group possesses a specialized middle ﬁnger
for this task ( Erickson, 1991; Moritz and Dominy, 2012 ), and the
latter are renowned for their large repertoire of extractive tech-
niques ( Rose, 1994; Fragaszy and Boinski, 1995; Moura and Lee,
2004; Ottoni and Izar, 2008; Melin et al., 2010; Souto et al., 201 1 ).
It has been suggested that enhanced SMI, and correspondingly
large brain volumes, manual dexterity, laterality and ﬁne hand
control, have been favored independently in capuchins and great
apes to facilitate their extractive diets in the absence of anatomical
specialization ( Parker and Gibson, 1977 , 1979; Costello and
Fragaszy, 1988; Fagot and Vauclair, 1988; MacNeilage, 1990;
Pouydebat et al., 2005; Cashmore et al., 2008; Uomini, 2009 ).
However, there is a surprising paucity of research exploring the
ecological correlates of extractive foraging among capuchins; an
oversight that we begin to address with the current study.
Seasonality and fallback foods
A fundamental component of the omnivorous extractive
foraging hypothesis concerns seasonality in resource acquisition.
Gibson (1986) predicts that embedded foods are available to pri-
mates year-round, but only utilized seasonally by some taxa during
periods of food scarcity. This closely matches the de ﬁnition of
fallback foods ( Marshall and Wrangham, 2007; Marshall et al.,
2009), which play a critical role during periods when preferred
foods, usually ripe fruit, are less abundant and are believed to have
a disproportionate effect on ﬁtness. Because fruit abundance is
linked to seasonal climatic variation ( Fleming et al., 1987; Levey,
1988; Milton et al., 2005; van Schaik and Pfannes, 2005 ), fallback
foods are especially important in strongly seasonal environments
characterized by more intense periods of fruit dearth. The majority
of the work to date focuses on vegetative fallback foods ( Yamashita,
1998; Vogel et al., 2008, 2009; Doran-Sheehy et al., 2009; Grueter
et al., 2009; Wrangham et al., 2009; Wright et al., 2009; Norconk
and Veres, 201 1 ), while invertebrates have received surprisingly
little attention (but see Lambert, 2007; Bogart and Pruetz, 201 1;
Isbell et al., 2013 ). Speci ﬁcally, the seasonal inclusion of in-
vertebrates in the diet, particularly those that must be extracted
from environmental substrates or protective exoskeletons, is
largely unknown for primates relative to the use and abundance of
fruit. This information is critical for assessing the sensorimotor
hypothesis and informing our understanding of the evolution of
primate cognition.
Current study
To better understand the ecological conditions underlying the
evolution of sensorimotor intelligence, we investigated relation-
ships among seasonality in temperature, precipitation, fruit abun-
dance and diet in capuchin monkeys. Capuchins are an ideal
organism for this study as they use tools and are widely heralded
for their intelligence ( Fragaszy and Visalberghi, 1989; Souto et al.,
201 1), they reside in highly seasonal habitats ( Campos and
Fedigan, 2009; Carnegie et al., 201 1; Gogarten et al., 2012 ), and,
in addition to fruit, they consume a variety of invertebrates,
including many that are embedded or heavily protected ( Fragaszy
and Boinski, 1995; Young, 2005; Young et al., 2008; Melin et al.,
2010). Our goals were to examine: (1) if and how patterns of
invertebrate and fruit consumption vary over the annual cycle in
relation to climatic seasonality, (2) how use of invertebrate prey is
related to patterns of fruit use, and (3) whether capuchins exhibit
seasonal variation in consumption of invertebrates based on cap-
ture method (gleaned from surfaces or extracted from substrates)
or degree of mechanical protection (handling time to extract from
protective covering). The signi ﬁcance of these results for under-
standing the evolution of ecological cognition in humans and
hominins is discussed.
Materials and methods
Study site and subjects
This study was conducted in Sector Santa Rosa (part of the larger
Área de Conservación Guanacaste) in northwestern Costa Rica
(10
/C14 450 to 11/C14 000 N and 85 /C14 300 to 85 /C14 450 W). The site consists of
108 km 2 of tropical dry forest that is largely deciduous ( Janzen,
2002). A strong degree of climatic seasonality at our study site
has frequently been described. Here we use a new approach to
assess it quantitatively. Two distinct seasons exist in this region.
During the warmer dry season (December e mid-May), little to no
precipitation occurs. The wet season ( wmid-MayeNovember) is
cooler and accumulates most of the annual 900 e2400 mm of rain
(Fedigan and Jack, 2001; Campos and Fedigan, 2009 ). During the
late wet season, offshore tropical storms bring many days of
pervasively heavy rainfall.
We observed four groups of white-faced capuchin monkeys
(Cebus capucinus ), ranging in size from eight to 35 members. All
study subjects were individually identi ﬁed by body size and
distinctive characteristics of their faces, fur pattern or scars. These
groups are part of a long-term ﬁeld project and are well habituated
(Fedigan and Jack, 2012 ). Because small juvenile capuchins are less
adept at capturing invertebrates than adults ( Robinson, 1981;
Fragaszy and Boinski, 1995 ), we only included invertebrate
foraging data for adults and for juveniles that were at least three
years of age at the time of observation (i.e., the ‘large immature’ age
class, Fedigan and Jack, 2001 ).
Data collection
Rainfall and maximum and minimum temperatures have been
recorded daily in Santa Rosa as part of the research center ’s long-
term data collection project since 1979 (Fedigan, unpublished
data; Janzen and Hallwachs, unpublished data). Although our

From January 2006 to December 2010, fruit production was
monitored via phenology trails that were sampled within the ﬁrst

50%, 51e75%, and 76e100%. For each species in each year, the peak
fruiting month was assigned based on the month when the greatest
abundance of ripe fruit was present following Carnegie et al. (201 1).
Behavioral data on invertebrate foraging by capuchin monkeys
were collected over a six-year period: May eJuly 2003; January e
August 2004; JanuaryeApril 2005; FebruaryeMay and Septembere
December 2007; January and May eAugust 2008. Behavioral data
on fruit foraging were collected during the latter portion of this
study to overlap with our records on fruiting phenology: Februarye
May and September eDecember 2007; January and May eAugust
2008. Each capuchin group was observed for two to eight days per
month for a total of >2000 contact hours. The majority of data were
collected during full day follows, from dawn until dusk, using
PSION/C211Workabout computers loaded with Behavior /C211software.
Inter-observer reliability was evaluated throughout the study to
ensure consistency.
We recorded all observed occurrences of feeding on fruit pulp or
aril as a new fruit foraging bout. Capuchins are also known to engage
in extractive frugivory (e.g., pounding Luehea fruits, Perry, 2009 );
however, because these behaviors are extractive, infrequent, and
potentially confounding (capuchins may target embedded insects in
addition to Luehea seeds) we have omitted them from our analyses.
Following Leighton (1993), we recorded a single bout for each new
fruit source, regardless of the number of monkeys feeding or the
length of time the group remained there. Most bouts involved single
trees. However, we also recorded single foraging bouts when the
monkeys fed on clumped non-tree sources, such as bromeliad
patches or vines spanning several trees. A separate bout was
recorded for the same food source in a single day if the group left the
general area ( >50 m) for longer than 1 h before returning to it.
To quantify invertebrate foraging, we conducted 10-min focal
animal follows ( Altmann, 1974) collecting a total of 436 h of focal
data. Our methods for recording invertebrate foraging differed from
those of recording fruit foraging because, in contrast to fruit tree
visitations, invertebrate faunivory is relatively cryptic and requires
intense observation of one individual at a time to witness and
identify feeding records. When a focal monkey ate an invertebrate,
we recorded the capture methods as gleaning from a surface or
extracting from a substrate, and the type of invertebrate according
to a classiﬁcation system based on mobility, body size and handling
time ( Table 1 ). For the purposes of this study, we de ﬁned an
invertebrate foraging bout as a single invertebrate capture and
consumption event. However, a single foraging bout was also
recorded for each instance of feeding on colonial invertebrates.
Data analysis
We employed directional analyses to test for seasonality in
rainfall, temperature, fruit abundance, invertebrate feeding and
fruit feeding, where months of the year are represented as angles of
a circle ( Janson and Verdolin, 2005; Carnegie et al., 201 1 ). The
analysis is based on vectors, which are comprised of an angle, ‘q’
and length, ‘r’. The sample mean is obtained by calculating the
mean vector angle q of the observations, which can be back-
transformed to a month (January eDecember) or day of the year
(e.g., 1 ¼ January 1st, 365 ¼ December 31st). The mean vector
length is used to calculate the concentration of observations around
the mean, which is an indication of the degree to which data are
seasonally clumped. Length values range from 0 to 1, where 0 in-
dicates uniform distribution of angles around the circle, and 1 in-
dicates that all data share the same angle.
Analyses were run with Oriana 3.0 statistical software. Ray-
leigh’s Uniformity test was used to test whether the distribution of
data is signi ﬁcantly different from a von Mises distribution, the
equivalent of a uniform linear distribution ( Batchelet, 1981 ).
Because directional analyses require that data points are ordinal in
scale ( Janson and Verdolin, 2005; Morellato et al., 2010 ), we
analyzed counts of the number of days that: (a) exceed the mean
daily rainfall volume, (b) exceed the mean daily maximum tem-
perature ( Tmax), and (c) are less than the mean daily minimum
temperature (Tmin) from the 31-year sample. To quantify monthly
fruit abundance, we calculated a monthly fruit value based on peak
phenology and weighted by habitat-wide abundance, following
Carnegie et al. (201 1) . For behavioral data, we reported the fruit
foraging bouts and invertebrate foraging bouts by month. To
standardize sampling effort across months, we randomly selected
10 full-day follows in each month and included all bouts recorded
on those days. For invertebrate foraging bouts, we calculated the
number of invertebrates caught each month per 30 h of observa-
tion, which was the mean number of focal follow hours per month.

Seasonal patterns of frugivory and faunivory
Fruit abundance varied signi ﬁcantly across the annual cycle
(Fig. 2 a), overlapping with, but slightly offset from, the seasonal
precipitation pattern. Fruit abundance increased in April, one
month before the onset of the rainy season, and decreased in
October, one to two months prior to the onset of the dry season. The
mean vector rested on the border between June and July ( r ¼ 0.363,
concentration ¼ 0.780). To examine the inter-annual consistency of
this pattern, we analyzed the phenology data for each year (2006 e
2010) separately. In accordance with the overall pattern reported
above, each year showed signi ﬁcant seasonality ( p < 0.02) and the
mean vector of peak fruit production fell between mid-June and

We analyzed 2612 fruit patch visits recorded over the length of
our study. Capuchin frugivory ( Fig. 2 b) was signi ﬁcantly seasonal
(Z ¼ 154.703, p < 0.001) and closely matched variation in fruit
abundance. The mean vector for fruit feeding bouts fell within June
(r ¼ 0.243, concentration ¼ 0.502), and the maximum monthly
peak of fruit bouts occurred in July.
Capuchin faunivory was also signi ﬁcantly seasonal. We recor-
ded 6223 invertebrate foraging bouts. Embedded invertebrates
(n ¼ 1971 records) made up 32% of the total invertebrate foraging
bouts. Signi ﬁcant deviations from a uniform distribution were
observed for both embedded and gleaned captures ( Fig. 2 c and
d). Extractive captures were heavily concentrated during the
months of low fruit abundance. The mean vector for embedded
bouts landed within November, with high values from September
through January ( r ¼ 0.306, concentration ¼ 0.642). Invertebrate
gleaning overlapped extensively with periods of high fruit abun-
dance in the early wet season and also extended into the late wet
and early dry season, when fruit was less abundant. The mean
vector for gleaned bouts fell within October with high values from
July through December ( r ¼ 0.301, concentration ¼ 0.630).
Of the 6223 recorded bouts, we could determine the inverte-
brate type for 5162 bouts (83%). In Table 2, we present the contri-
butions of each known invertebrate and a summary of the results
from our directional analyses. The seasonal distribution of bouts
was variable, depending on invertebrate type ( Fig. 3 aef). Small
mobile invertebrates and embedded grubs were eaten most
frequently in the late wet season. Extractive foraging on colonial
invertebrates was also focused on the late wet season and extended
into the early dry season, as were snail foraging bouts, although
they were the least temporally clumped. Caterpillar consumption
began in the early wet season and carried on throughout the late
wet season. Large mobile invertebrates were captured nearly
exclusively in the dry season.
Discussion
Seasonal extractive foraging among capuchins
The capuchins in our study showed distinct seasonal patterns of
extractive faunivory, a ﬁnding that supports a central premise of
the sensorimotor intelligence hypothesis (Parker and Gibson, 1977).
Additionally, the extraction and consumption of protected in-
vertebrates by capuchins was inversely related to frugivory and
more closely related to seasonal variation in fruit abundance than
to precipitation or temperature. In contrast, the peak consumption
of caterpillars, which are typically gleaned from leaf surfaces and
easy to process, overlapped with periods of high fruit abundance.
These results are in accordance with the hypothesis that embedded
invertebrates are a fallback food.

A model for the evolution of ecological cognition
Large relative brain size and high performance across a diverse
array of cognitive tasks has arisen independently multiple times
across primate lineages in Hominoidea and Cebus, and also in
Macaca and Papio (Reader et al., 201 1). This has been attributed to
natural selection favoring either a high level of general intelligence
(Reader et al., 201 1) or similarities in evolution of domain-speci ﬁc
cognitive modules ( Fodor, 1983; Cosmides et al., 2010 ), due to
shared socioecological pressures in these lineages. The extent to
which cognition has evolved as a modular process remains an
ongoing source of debate among neuroscientists (recently
reviewed in Charvet and Finlay, 2012; Vilarroya, 2012 ). However,
given the importance of distinct ecological skills needed to solve
different foraging challenges (e.g., spatial memory for relocating
fruit, innovative problem solving for tool use) and new data
correlating sensorimotor intelligence with a speci ﬁc brain struc-
ture, the cerebellum ( Barton, 2012), we hypothesize that distinct
cognitive skills, driven by different ecological challenges, were
favored in some primate lineages over the course of evolution.
In Fig. 4 , we present a model of ecological cognition and brain
size evolution in primates. It should be noted that our model does
not consider the evolution of social or tactical cognition, or their
effects on brain size, which may be shaped by differing evolu-
tionary processes. Primates were primitively small-bodied and
likely foraged for surface-dwelling insects opportunistically,
perhaps resembling modern treeshrews ( Emmons, 2000 ), and fed
on foods within the terminal branches of angiosperms ( Sussman,
1991; Sussman et al., 2013 ). With the evolution of larger body
size, some became primarily frugivorous and others primarily
folivorous. Among the folivorous primates, morphological adapta-
tions for food processing were favored, such as complex stomachs,
while among primarily frugivorous primates, cognitive adaptations
and enlargement of the ‘executive brain ’ (Keverne et al., 1996;
Reader and Laland, 2002 ) evolved in response to demands of fru-
givory, such as improved spatiotemporal memory. Opportunistic
gleaning of surface-dwelling insects is not precluded among fru-
givores, rather it is likely. During times of fruit dearth, frugivorous
primates either fall back on abundant vegetation such as leaves or
hard seeds, again leading to digestive or masticatory specializations
(Chivers and Hladik, 1984; Lambert et al., 2004; Vogel et al., 2008;
Altmann, 2009; Constantino et al., 2009; Vogel et al., 2009 ) or turn
to extractive foods, such as embedded invertebrates. In the latter
case, increased sensorimotor intelligence and associated features,
including tool use, manual dexterity and laterality and the ability to
solve syntactical problems arose as cognitive adaptations and were
associated with brain expansion, including the cerebellum ( Phillips
and Hopkins, 2007 ). Further expansion of SMI as a cognitive buffer
occurred among primates facing increasing seasonality where the
importance, variety or dif ﬁculty of embedded fallback foods is also
increased ( Sol, 2009; van Woerden et al., 2012 ). Finally, among
hominins, expanded technology, material cultural and language
may have built on pre-existing sensorimotor adaptations ( Barton,
2012).
One implication of this model is that in addition to preferred
foods (fruits) favoring intelligence as suggested by Marshall and
Wrangham (2007) , we predict fallback foods to be a selective
force shaping cognitive evolution. While fallback foods are typically
thought to exert strong selective pressures on morphological ad-
aptations of the digestive system and teeth for food processing, we
believe this is more accurately applied to non-embedded, vegeta-
tive fallback foods and anticipate that increasing brain size is
favored in more seasonal environments, speci ﬁcally among
extractive foragers. This may be particularly true of primates that
obtain embedded foods that are proteinaceous, such as in-
vertebrates, given the importance of amino acids to brain devel-
opment and function ( Milton, 2003; Bourre, 2006 ). As an example
of how variation in seasonality may have affected the evolution of
SMI and associated traits, we discuss differences between the two

Variation among capuchin lineages and links to evolution of SMI
Aside from physical appearance, perhaps the most notable dif-
ference between the robust (tufted, genus Sapajus) and gracile
(untufted, genus Cebus) capuchin lineages is their variation in tool
use. While Cebus monkeys frequently use substrate-object manip-
ulations, (e.g., banging snails or fruits against branches), records of
innovative, sophisticated tool use and modi ﬁcation are known only
among the Sapajus lineage (Moura and Lee, 2004; Ottoni and Izar,
2008; Souto et al., 201 1 ). These differences may re ﬂect variation
in ecological pressures, speci ﬁcally the extent of seasonality and
reliance on embedded resources as fallback foods, acting on the
evolution of SMI ( Moura and Lee, 2004; Wright et al., 2009; van
Woerden et al., 2012 ). Time-tree analyses of the mitochondrial
cytochrome b gene suggest that the SapajuseCebus diversiﬁcation
occurred in the late Miocene, about 6.2 Ma ( Lynch Alfaro et al.,
2012a). From their reconstruction of the diffusion of capuchins
across South and Central America, Lynch Alfaro et al. (2012b) argue
that the Cebus radiation has historically and consistently occupied
tropical rainforests, whereas the Sapajus lineage spread from their
origins in the Atlantic rainforest into drier, more temperate and
seasonal habitat types. We predict that the last common ancestor of
Cebus and Sapajus had a level of SMI more closely resembling
extant Cebus monkeys, and that further expansion of SMI evolved in
the robust lineage to facilitate increased access to varied embedded
fallback foods, necessitated by more intense periods of fruit
shortage. Given the links with manual dexterity, tool use, and
extractive foraging, we would additionally predict that current
robust capuchins have higher manual dexterity than gracile spe-
cies; however, there are currently no data available that test this.
Implications of seasonal extractive foraging for hominin evolution
Although the fossil record is expected to obscure the effects of
seasonality on behavior, stable isotope analyses indicate seasonal
variation in diet for at least one hominin, Paranthropus robustus
(Sponheimer et al., 2006 ). This variation agrees well with evidence
of tool use for extractive foraging by South African hominins,
including P. robustus (Backwell and d ’Errico, 2001; d ’Errico and
Backwell, 2009 ) and C 4 isotopic signatures of some extractable
foods such as termites and plant underground storage organs
(Sponheimer et al., 2005; Yeakel et al., 2007; Dominy et al., 2008 ).
Perhaps compellingly, the consumption of termites by modern
chimpanzees, an important referential model, is seasonal and
especially important in harsh environments ( McGrew, 1979;
McGrew and Collins, 1985; Moore, 1996; Bogart and Pruetz,
2008). Links between extractive faunivory and fruit abundance
are seldom investigated (but see Bogart and Pruetz, 201 1 ); how-
ever, chimpanzees use tools to access other embedded foods
(Hernandez-Aguilar et al., 2007 ), including during periods when
ripe fruit are scarce ( Yamakoshi, 1998).
Modern humans frequently consume insects, which are
seasonally important when other animal foods are limited ( Dufour,
1987). Fluctuations in prey abundance are also reported to in ﬂu-
ence insectivory in modern humans ( Hamilton and Busse, 1978;
Sileshi et al., 2009 ) and chimpanzees ( Uehara, 1982; Yamakoshi,
1998; Pruetz and Bertolani, 2007; O ’Malley and Power, 2014 ),
suggesting that an understanding of prey dynamics will further
elucidate the patterns and signi ﬁcance of primate faunivory.
Nevertheless, opportunistic exploitation of prey during periods of
overall food abundance does not preclude a critical role of
embedded invertebrates for subsistence. Even though the nature of
the evidence is sparse, studies of fossil hominins and modern
chimpanzees are consistent with the idea that seasonal fallback on
embedded foods, including insects, selected for a suite of adapta-
tions including sensorimotor cognition, tool use and manual dex-
terity and laterality. In hominins, this may have set the stage for the
emergence of material technologies, language and reason ( Barton,
2012). Thus, our work supports that of others in suggesting a crit-
ical role of invertebrate faunivory ( Sutton, 1995; McGrew, 2001 )
and embedded fallback foods ( Laden and Wrangham, 2005 )i n
hominin evolution.
