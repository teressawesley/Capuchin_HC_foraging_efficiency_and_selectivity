Wild Capuchins Show Male-Biased Feeding
Tool Use
Antonio C. de A. Moura & Phyllis C. Lee
Received: 13 July 2009 / Accepted: 16 October 2009 /
Published online: 8 May 2010
# Springer Science+Business Media, LLC 2010
Abstract Relatively few studies have explored sex differences in the use of foraging
tools among primates other than apes. Although male primates are thought to be
more innovative, researchers have reported a female sex bias in the use of feeding
tools in wild chimpanzees. We investigate here the nature and extent of sex
differences in foraging tool use over 12 mo in a free-ranging group of bearded
capuchins (2 males, 5 females, and 3 juveniles) living in the dry Caatinga forests of
the Serra da Capivara National Park, Piaui, Brazil. These capuchins used 3 major
types of feeding tools: 1) tools for probing; 2) tools for pounding/cracking; and 3)
digging stones to extract tubers or roots. Adult males performed 63% ( n=134) of all
events of tool use and used tools significantly more frequently than did females,
although male bout lengths across all tools (57 s±7.9 SE) were equivalent to those of
adult females (47.3 s±12.6 SE). Both sexes used digging and cracking tools,
although at different rates, whereas adult males used sticks to probe for prey and
other rewards far more than females. Differential opportunities to use tools were not
apparent: >71% of tool-use events occurred on the ground, and males and females
spent equal time on the ground. We suggest that sex differences in tool use may
function as opportunities for male signaling of investment quality.

Introduction
Among wild nonhuman primates, sex differences in the use of tools to obtain food
have been detailed for chimpanzees (Boesch and Boesch 1984; Lonsdorf et al. 2004;
McGrew 1992). Explanations for these sex-specific use of tools are tentative but
numerous. One suggestion highlights energetic constraints on females (McGrew
1992); because females incur additional energy costs during pregnancy, lactation,
and rearing young, their more frequent use of tools is a strategy to enhance foraging
efficiency and to allow access to otherwise inaccessible, embedded resources. As an
alternative premise, male primates have a propensity to be more innovative than are
females (Reader and Laland 2001) and potentially more so in a tool-use context,
because males can afford to sustain the energetic costs associated with failure to
extract resources. Other explanations for sex differences in tool use emphasize the
proximate social context. Male chimpanzees can be distracted from tool use to
follow or monitor other males (Boesch and Boesch 1984; Lonsdorf et al. 2004);
hence, their tool-use frequency and proficiency tends to be lower than that of
females. An additional social hypothesis suggests that males make and use tools as a
form of sexually selected display (Kohn and Mithen 1999).
Researchers have observed male predominance in tool-using behavior in Cebus
apella in captivity (Visalberghi 1987), naturalistic captive settings (Jalles-Filho
1995; Jalles-Filho et al. 2001), semifree- ranging groups (Ottoni and Mannu 2001),
and recently in 2 wild groups in Boa Vista (Visalberghi pers. comm. 2008), although
Westergaard et al .( 1998) found no sex differences in tool use over the long-term.
Mannu and Ottoni ( 2009) found a higher rate of overall tool use in Cebus libidinosus
(0.97 events/h compared with 0.43/h in earlier study; Moura and Lee 2004) and
noted the existence of sex differences in tool use, but did not provide a sex-specific
breakdown by context.
For either sex, the use of foraging tools reduces the time costs of processing large
or embedded foods and thus increases the return rate from foraging activities
(Yamakoshi 1998). The energetic benefits of using tools thus should be high in areas
with either limited time for foraging or limited food abundance, as is the case for the
Brazilian Caatinga dry forest (Moura 2003, 2007a; Moura and Lee 2004; Moura
Ottoni and Izar 2008). During a 6-mo dry season, most trees are leafless, whereas the
proportion of trees bearing fruit was 4-fold greater during the wet season (Moura
2004). High seasonality and low fruit and leaf availability are associated with small
group sizes and low densities of marmosets and howlers in this area (Moura 2004),
but the complex foraging strategies, including tool use, of capuchins made them
relatively less affected by these ecological constraints (Moura 2007a; Moura and Lee
2004). In such a harsh habitat, sex differences in foraging tool use could be predicted
with females expected to use tools more frequently to meet the additional energy
costs of reproduction. Here, we explore an alternative hypothesis that males will
innovate and thus use tools more in a social or signaling context. The predominance
of male tool use in captivity has been explained in terms of exclusion of others by
the dominant male that was able to monopolize the tool apparatus (Jalles-Filho
1995), while Boinski ( 2004) speculated on males using objects as displays. Given
the relative rarity of detailed investigations of individual tool use in free-ranging
non-ape species, and the high levels of tool use in this and adjacent Caatinga

Methods
Study Site
We conducted the study in the Serra da Capivara National Park (8°26 ′–8°54′S and
42° 19 ′–42°45 W), which covers an area of 129,953 ha. Emperaire ( 1984), in a
detailed analysis of the geomorphology and associated vegetation, recognized up to
8 different types of habitat within the Park, each with characteristic vegetation types
(Moura and McConkey 2007).
Mean annual rainfall over 7 yr was 781±177 mm (Moura 2007a). Total and
monthly amount of rainfall varied considerably from year to year, and the
coefficient of variation ranged from 98.2% to 154.8%. V egetation studies
suggested that dry months were those with ≤60 mm of rainfall (Clinebell et al .
1995;T r e j oa n dD i r z o2002). During the dry season, >70% of trees were leafless,
and fruit availability was only 25% of that in the wet season (Moura 2004). We
defined the dry season as the 6-mo period from when the trees start to lose their
leaves (May) until they begin to leaf again in October; rainfall throughout this
period was considerably <50 mm/mo (Moura 2004).
Focal Group
In October 2000, Moura commenced habituation of and data collection on 1 group
of Cebus libidinosus . Initially, the group was composed of 1 adult male, 3 adult
females, and a juvenile male. Only the original adult male was wild; the others were
reintroduced from individuals confiscated by Park authorities from nearby villages.
On December 24, 2000, a wild group appeared in the area and engaged in agonistic
interactions with the habituated group. The original male disappeared, but the
remaining reintroduced individuals (Table I) merged successfully with the wild
group (2 adult males, 2 adult females, 1 juvenile male, and 1 juvenile female),
forming a stable group of 10 individuals (Oitenta group). Moura observed this group
systematically until January 2002 with additional observations in February and
March 2002. The data presented here cover the period of January –December 2001.
In November 2001, 1 adult male left the group and in December, 2 captive females
died (Table I). The group was provisioned once a month during the dry seasons,
when the observer was away, with maize (about 50 –70 corns) and dry manioc tubers
(ca. 10 kg) placed in a restricted area. We made all observations outside of the
provisioning area.
Sampling
Moura followed the group from dawn to dusk (although occasionally it could only
be located for the first time several hours after sunrise) for an average of 5.7 d/mo
(range 3 –7 d/mo, mode 7 d). Behavior of individuals in the group was observed

using slow scan sampling at each half-hour, as detailed by Robinson ( 1986)a n d
Miller ( 1996), who also observed capuchins living in dry habitats. Slow sampling
is an alternative to an instantaneous scan, and allows for the location of all
individuals over the scanning period. In January, it was extremely hard to locate
and follow the group, because they were still not well habituated, but from
February onward the number of individuals scanned increased and the time spent
locating individuals reduced. It took an average (±SD) of 16.56±7.2 min to
locate individuals (median 15.5 min). The average (±SD) number of individuals
in scans was 7.3±1.43 out of the 10 present, including juveniles. When an
individual was located, we recorded only the first observed behavior that lasted
for ≥5 s in the scan. In addition to scans, we conducted both continuous records
of rare behaviors and 60-s interval focal animal sampling during 10-min
sessions. We performed focal samples only on adult individuals and recorded
interactions, proximity, nearest-neighbor identify, and location. We recorded tool
use during focal samples, scans, and as all occurrences during continuous
sampling.
We analyze only the use of tools associated directly with obtaining food or that
subjects used to facilitate an activity subsequently related to obtaining food. These
capuchins also used tools in a variety of contexts such as aggressive displays (hitting
a rock surface with a stone) and in play (Mannu and Ottoni 2009; Moura 2007b). We
use the term events for all observations of tool use, including those wherein we
recorded no duration, and bouts when the activity was prolonged and we could
record the durations. Capuchins used tools for obtaining food in 3 contexts: 1)
probing— branches or twigs used to probe tree holes and rock crevices for obtaining
items such as honey, arthropods, or lizards (16% of tool-use events; n=134); 2)
cracking/pounding— use of stones to crack open diverse materials (seeds, dry
branches, etc.; 19% of events); 3) digging — the use of a stone for digging up tubers/
roots (65% of events; see Moura and Lee 2004). When using a stone to dig, an

During tool use, we recorded the identity of the performer, its location, and
information about tool type, food, or context of tool use. In the analyses, we
combined the data from the scan and focal samples with all occurrences of tool
use derived from the total observation time spent with the capuchins. We
scanned all adult individuals equally and they were therefore visible during the
continuous observations. We scanned individual females (mean per adult female =

the conspicuousness of digging both by sight and sound, we missed only few
such events. We could have missed probing and cracking between successive
30-min scans; however, as we continuously monitored all adults to conduct focal
samples and to ensure they were ready for subsequent scans, it is unlikely we
disproportionately or differentially mi ssed these rarer tool-use types between
individuals.
We calculated individual hourly rates of tool use, based on the total observation
time with the group in each month. In analyses of nearest neighbor identity and
proximity, we used data from scan sampling. For some analyses, we pooled the
data derived from different individuals into total sex-specific observations.
Pooling data is problematic because it can be biased by individuals who are
more active or disproportionately represented over the mean (Martin and
Bateson 1986). However, Leger and Didrichsons ( 1994) found that pooling can
provide reliable estimates across a population. We tested for normality, and
used parametric tests where data were nor mally distributed. We have presented
means in figures for large sample sizes, even if skewed, so as to indicate
midpoints.
We set p=0.05 and all statistical tests are 2-tailed. Statistical power of our
analyses was calculated from G*Power 3.0.10 (Faul et al . 2007)a s0 . 0 8f o r

Results
We observed a total of 134 events of feeding tool use in Oitenta group during 312.3
contact h, with 21 during focal and 30 during scan sampling. The remaining
observations derived from all-occurrences sampling ( n=83). Males used tools more
frequently than did females on an individual by individual basis (Fig. 1). There was
considerable variability in the frequency of use of different types of tools among
individuals (Table II), but adult males were responsible for the majority of tool use
(Figs. 1 and 2), performing >63% of all tool use observed ( n=134). The second
ranking male (Adt) used tools half as often as did the alpha male did (Boludo;
Table II).
Males exhibited significantly higher rates of tool use overall than did the other
age-sex classes both in the dry (Kruskal Wallis, H=13.31, df=2, p=0.001) and wet
seasons ( H=24.13, df=2, p<0.001; Fig. 2). Probing was performed almost
exclusively by adult males; the only female to exhibit this behavior was Peta (a
reintroduced female), who once used a twig as a probe very briefly (for ca. 15 s) and
she used both hands. Adult males typically manipulated the probe with 1 hand and
for longer durations. When the alpha male engaged in foraging activities, adult
females and juveniles were in closer proximity to him than to the subordinate male
(H=26.67, df=1, p<0.001), especially during tool use ( H=6.91, df=1, p=0.009;
Fig. 3). In 24% of events of tool use by adult males that resulted in obtaining food
resource ( n=25), juveniles and adult females scrounged some food remains.

Discussion
Adult male capuchins used tools significantly more frequently than did females, and
probing was almost exclusively done by adult males. This difference might simply
be due to chance, given our small sample size and presence of reintroduced females.
However, because researchers have observed sex differences in tool use in Cebus
apella and C. xanthosternos in naturalistic settings (Jalles-Filho and Cunha 2008;
Ottoni and Mannu 2001) and suggested for wild groups in similar habitats
(Visalberghi pers. comm . 2008), this may be a generalized pattern for tool-using
tufted capuchin monkeys.
Digging was the most common form of tool use by both sexes, and there was
widespread evidence of its regular use in the form of holes scattered throughout the
capuchins’ range. Thus, most tool use at this site occurred on the ground. Fragaszy
(1990) suggested that females might be more vulnerable on the ground, and hence
predation risk could explain sex differences in tool use. However, here males and
females spent equal amount of time on the ground. Males overall engaged in more
frequent and longer use of heavier digging tools. Although males may simply have
been inept at selecting an appropriate tool and therefore need to invest greater effort
to obtain rewards, this seems unlikel y; wild capuchins consistently use the
appropriate tool for the job (Visalberghi et al. 2009). Indeed, when using digging
tools, a male was observed changing tools only twice (Moura 2004).

The main reward from digging was the extraction of underground tubers, chiefly
Thiloa glaucocarpa (Moura and Lee 2004). Although the nutritional value of Thiloa
tubers is unknown, tubers generally have a high energy value in the form of
carbohydrates (Laden and Wrangham 2005). Thus, the payoff for the time and
energy invested in tuber extraction could be considerable, potentially representing a
key resource (O ’ Connell et al. 1999).
Liu et al. (2009), in a detailed analysis of tool-use kinematics in wild capuchins,
reported that the weight of stones used to crack nuts represented ≤77% of capuchin
body mass, whereas in our area, the average weight of a stone tool represented only
ca. 8% of a capuchin female ’ s body mass. The use of large stones is demanding in
terms of energy expenditure, and rewards, like those for female chimpanzee nut-
cracking, are high and reliable only once individuals are skilled (Boesch and Boesch
1984). However, the repeated, sustained use of lighter tools would still have energy
costs, and the probability of failure to obtain a food reward via this energetic
investment was relatively high for both sexes. A low energy return rate might thus
limit investment in costly tool use by females (Gunther and Boesch 1993; Tebbich et
al. 2002). Return rates from obtaining tubers could be high enough to compensate
for energy and opportunity costs (O ’ Connell et al. 1999), and these may have been a
driver for generalized tool use by both sexes of capuchins in these dry habitats
(Moura and Lee 2004; Ottoni and Izar 2008). The question remains as to why males,
and especially the alpha, engaged in more tool use than other group members.
One explanation for the observed sex differences is that tool use acts as a
form of male provisioning and paternal investment (Janson 1984). Females can
scavenge from the effort of the males, eating food remains and saving the energy
that they would expend if they used the tools themselves. In addition, the male
bears the opportunity costs of failure. In our study group, the females and juveniles
were in close proximity to males, and especially so when the alpha male was using
tools. These onlookers scrounged food remains in 24% of males ’ successful tool-
use activities. Similar proportions of sc rounging (35%) from male nut-cracking
activities have been observed elsewhere (Ottoni et al . 2005). Males are relatively
bigger and stronger than females and able to obtain resources unavailable to
females and immatures (Robinson 1986). Greater tool use by males thus could be
the result of sexual selection for general male inventiveness (Reader and Laland
2001) or as a display of male quality (Kohn and Mithen 1999)i nt h ec o n t e x to f
signaling paternal investment ability. T he propensity for more tool use by the alpha
male is suggestive support for social signaling of quality (Boinski 2004).
Additional support for this hypothesis c ould be provided if tool-using groups with
a larger number of males again found a predominant role of the alpha male in that
