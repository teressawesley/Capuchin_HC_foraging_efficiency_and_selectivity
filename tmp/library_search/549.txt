Abstract
Coastal populations of long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis) display remarkable behavioral flexibility in exploiting 
intertidal food resources. Tool-using groups in Thailand are known to crack marine shellfish with stones, yet the behav -
ioral repertoire of non-tool-using populations remains poorly documented. Here, we present the first report of long-tailed 
macaques preying on aquatic hermit crabs without using tools, based on observations at Wat Takiap, Hua Hin District, 
Prachuap Khiri Khan Province, Thailand. During low tide, macaques were observed employing a sequence of substrate-
assisted manipulations: repeatedly rubbing gastropod shells against rocks or concrete to thin and weaken the shell wall, 
followed by fracturing it with their teeth or by striking it against hard surfaces, and finally extracting and consuming the 
hermit crab’s appendages. This behavior represents a novel, non-tool extractive technique for accessing encased prey—a 
strategy intermediate between manual shell handling and percussive tool use. Such manipulation indicates refined motor 
control and problem-solving in a foraging context where encased prey are abundant but challenging to access. Given the 
absence of long-term data, these observations provide a valuable record of a potential pre-emergent stage in the evolution 
of tool use. Continued longitudinal and comparative studies will be essential to determine whether such substrate-assisted 
behaviors may serve as precursors to habitual stone-tool use in coastal macaques.

with the observational conditions.
Materials and methods
This study was conducted through noninvasive observations 
from public coastal areas of long-tailed macaques inhabiting 
Wat Takiap and its surroundings in Hua Hin District, Pra -
chuap Khiri Khan Province, Thailand. This site is located 
approximately 60 km from Khao Sam Roi Yot National 
Park, where stone-tool use by long-tailed macaques has 
been studied. Wat Takiap is a temple situated atop a rocky 
hill; at the hill’s base are a mooring area for fishing boats 
and a seafood market. The vicinity of the harbor encom -
passes an intertidal environment consisting of contiguous 
sandy beaches, cobble–boulder fields, and rocky shore plat-
forms (Fig. 1). Although the exact number of social groups 
and individuals is unknown, public records (administrative 
documents and complaint/report logs) suggest a rough esti-
mate of 5–6 groups totaling 600–1000 individuals, with 
uncertainty due to seasonal fluctuation and anthropogenic 
factors. At this location, opportunistic observations by 
author AT have been conducted intermittently since 2022, 
and prior consumption of marine foods had been confirmed. 
However, many of those prior instances involved feeding 
on small crabs removed and discarded by fishers during net 
cleaning, or on crustaceans stolen from a seafood market, 
such that anthropogenic influences (provisioning, waste use, 
theft-foraging) could not be excluded.

In the present filming and observations, while noting 
such human-dependent events, we focused on the explora -
tion and processing of prey under natural conditions. The 
observation period spanned 2–19 September 2025, with 
observation windows secured daily in principle from 06:00 
to 18:00 (from sunrise to sunset). During the filming period, 
we identified and observed four groups of macaques that 
regularly visited the beach. These groups were named as 
the Market Group, City Group A, City Group B, and City 
Group C. The number of identifiable individuals in each 
group was nine, twelve, nine, and ten, respectively. Among 
them, City Group A, City Group B, and City Group C 
were subject to capture operations conducted by the Hua 
Hin municipal authorities. Because the Hua Hin municipal 
authorities were conducting monkey-trapping and removal 
operations in the urban area—on some days capturing as 
many as five macaques—the exact group sizes were vari -
able and could not be reliably determined. We recorded for-
aging on marine invertebrates ad libitum. We concentrated 
on intertidal use around low tide, consulting local tide tables 
for the Hua Hin area on the preceding day and early each 
morning to determine low-water times, and initiated film -
ing when individuals were observed interacting with marine 
organisms on the beach, cobble fields, or rocky platforms. 
Observers maintained a distance of approximately 5–20 m. 
Video was recorded using a Sony AX-60. Identification of 
prey as hermit crabs was based on the shape of the gastro -
pod shells held by monkeys, analysis of the video footage, 
and the morphology of extracted soft parts (soft abdomen, 
asymmetrical chelae, absence of a carapace).
Results
Based on repeated counts during the observation period, 
numerous long-tailed macaques inhabit the rocky hill at 
Wat Takiap; of these, those regularly using the seafood mar-
ket and adjacent shoreline at the hill’s base comprised at 
least five social groups totaling fewer than 100 individuals. 
Among the shoreline-using groups, at least three maintained 
sleeping sites not on the hill but within the urban area and 
appeared to commute daily from the town to the coastal 
zone.
During the observation period, long-tailed macaques 
along the shoreline of Wat Takiap were repeatedly observed 
interacting with naturally available intertidal invertebrates 
during low tide (Fig. 2). Note that during the present obser-
vation period, low tide occurred on 13 days within our daily 
observation window. Because the rocky substrate is not 
exposed unless the tide falls sufficiently—and macaques 
are therefore unlikely to capture marine invertebrates under 
non–low-tide conditions—the time window with the high -
est potential for the behavior to occur can reasonably be 
approximated as within about one hour before and after low 
tide. On this basis, the total observation time during which 
marine invertebrates foraging was most likely to be detect -
able amounts to approximately 13 h in our dataset. The most 
frequently encountered items were rock oysters, followed 
in decreasing order by small brachyuran crabs and hermit 
crabs (spp. unknown). No tool use was observed in any oys-
ter-feeding event. Instead, oysters were processed primar -
ily by (i) fracturing the shell with the teeth, (ii) forcefully 
striking the oyster against rock surfaces to induce breakage 
(Fig. 3), and (iii) incidental splitting of the shell when the

Discussion
This paper reports previously undocumented predation 
on hermit crabs by long-tailed macaques and provides a 
detailed description of the associated capture and process -
ing sequence. Our observations of non-tool predation on 
marine resources by long-tailed macaques—specifically, 
tooth-mediated breakage, forceful impacts against bed -
rock, and shell thinning via abrasion followed by fracture 
and manual extraction—add an important complement to 
the prevailing depiction of coastal long-tailed macaques as 
shells—excluding rock oysters—against the ground. These 
actions did not always culminate in successful extraction, 
and in many cases the shell was abandoned mid-process. 
Upon inspection, some of these discarded shells were found 
to still contain hermit crabs, indicating that predation was 
not always successful. However, the possibility cannot be 
excluded that some of these interactions were not for forag-
ing but instead represent non-nutritive object manipulation 
or exploratory play with the shells.

a flexible repertoire of contingent pre-processing actions 
that are deployed according to the condition of a food item. 
Interpreted in this light, the shell-rubbing observed during 
hermit-crab predation may not only contribute to thinning 
and weakening the shell wall but could also plausibly func-
tion to damage surface projections (e.g., spines) and thereby 
improve grip and handling efficiency prior to fracture and 
extraction.
Behaviors such as “rubbing” and “pounding” have 
often been positioned as precursor actions to tool use, or, 
alternatively, as a potential “missing link” between purely 
bodily processing and materially mediated (tool-assisted) 
processing. Drawing on chimpanzee research (Boesch 
and Boesch 1990; Matsuzawa 1994) as well as work on 
capuchins(Fragaszy et al. 2004), several authors have pro -
posed relatively explicit evolutionary scenarios in which 
non-tool processing can scaffold the later emergence of 
tool-assisted food processing. In this context, it is notewor -
thy that both rubbing and pounding were observed in the 
Hua Hin long-tailed macaques. Functionally, the observed 
sequence—abrasion-driven thinning of shell regions pre -
sumed to overlie soft tissues, followed by fracture using 
teeth or by striking the shell against hard surfaces, and finally 
digital extraction—solves the same engineering problem as 
percussive hammering: creating a controlled breach in a 
rigid enclosure. Within frameworks that explain the emer -
gence of tool use via necessity and opportunity, several vari-
ables should shift behavior toward carried percussion: (i) the 
defensive strength and attachment mode of the prey’s shell, 
archetypal stone-tool users for shellfish processing. Prior 
studies repeatedly document customary stone hammering 
to open encased marine prey, including rock oysters and 
other mollusks, as well as crabs (Gumert and Malaivijit -
nond 2012; Malaivijitnond et al. 2007; Tan et al. 2015). Our 
records show that the immediate mechanical prerequisite 
for accessing such prey—localized weakening and fracture 
of protective shells—can also be achieved without handheld 
tools by relying on teeth and environmental substrates. This 
clarifies the threshold conditions under which stone tools 
become necessary or advantageous.
Among our observations, one particularly notable feature 
is the repeated rubbing of shells to weaken them through 
thinning. Rubbing as a form of food pre-processing has clear 
conceptual parallels with the classic “sweet-potato washing” 
tradition first reported in Koshima Japanese macaques, in 
which individuals do not merely immerse potatoes in water 
but also rub them manually to remove adhering soil (Kawai 
1965). Hirata and colleagues later re-evaluated this behavior 
and discussed the possibility that its function shifted over 
time from simple cleaning to “seasoning” with seawater, as 
well as the processes by which the behavior may be socially 
transmitted (Hirata et al. 2008). Comparable rubbing or 
wiping behaviors have also been reported in long-tailed 
macaques, including cases in which individuals use leaves 
in a tool-like manner to rub food items, or rub items using 
cloth or plastic bags, apparently to remove undesirable sur-
face features such as spines, hairs, or dirt (Pal et al. 2018). 
Collectively, these studies suggest that macaques possess

availability, such as hunger or energetic shortfall).
At the same time, we must consider environmental and 
behavioral constraints that decrease the payoff of adopting 
stone tools. Our prior opportunistic observations at this site 
included feeding on small crabs removed and discarded dur-
ing net cleaning or crustaceans pilfered from a seafood mar-
ket—contexts that reduce prey defenses and processing costs 
and thus lower the marginal benefit of tool transport. More 
generally, long-tailed macaques are known to shift toward 
human foods in anthropogenic habitats (e.g., provisioning, 
discarded human foods), which may relax innovation pres -
sure consistent with the necessity hypothesis (Fuentes 2011; 
Sha and Hanya 2013). Even in coastal groups, preferences 
can fluctuate with fruit abundance and human inputs (Yea -
ger 1996). Accordingly, the probability that stone tool use 
will emerge or increase at Wat Takiap likely covaries with 
reductions in anthropogenic subsidies, seasonal scarcity of 
alternative foods, higher availability of suitable stones, and 
greater social exposure to effective handling sequences.
The behaviors we recorded—rubbing shells on rock sur-
faces and using the substrate as an anvil—already align with 
percussive task demands and may serve as proto-percussive 
stepping stones from environmental substrates to carried 
hammers. However, given that macaques at this site appear 
to have access to anthropogenic foods provided by local 
residents and fishermen, and that diverse plant resources 
(e.g., tamarind) are also available in the surrounding ter -
restrial habitat, the level of “necessity” pressure that would 
promote a transition to tool-assisted extraction may remain 
comparatively low.
Given the brief observation period and the absence of 
long-term baselines, our findings represent a provisional 
snapshot of an early, pre-tool phase of intertidal foraging. 
Importantly, these records did not derive from a dedicated, 
research-driven field program; rather, they were obtained 
opportunistically as a by-product of permitted television 
filming, which necessarily constrained both sampling stan -
dardization and the scope of quantitative inference. To
