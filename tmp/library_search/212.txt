alters group cohesion

Abstract1
Tool-aided extractive foraging expands access to novel foods, but its effects on social dynamics are less2
understood. When tool-use resources are monopolizable, tool use may increase intragroup competition.3
While intragroup competition encourages reduced cohesion, this comes at the cost of increased vulnerability4
to predation and intergroup conflict. We examined how use of spatially fixed, monopolizable resources,5
anvils, influences cohesion by comparing groups of tool-using and non-tool-using white-faced capuchins6
(Cebus capucinus imitator ) on Jicarón Island in Coiba National Park, Panama. Jicarón lacks terrestrial7
mammalian predators and stone tool use is locally restricted to a∼2 km stretch of coast. We deployed two8
grids of camera traps to compare daily activity patterns, and temporal variation in party size, party9
composition, and spatial cohesion. We compared observed patterns with simulations of groups exhibiting10
different cohesion regimes. We found that tool-using capuchins showed smaller, less variable party sizes than11
non-tool-using capuchins. Adult females and adult males were less likely to co-occur in a sequence.12
Tool-using and non-tool-using capuchins differed in their patterns of spatiotemporal cohesion. Simulations13
support that these patterns are consistent with the tool-using group engaging in fission-fusion subgrouping.14
Although only male capuchins use tools, the entire tool-using group shows reduced cohesion, suggesting that15
increased competition for one sex can have cascading effects within the group. Our findings suggest that16
habitual tool use relying on monopolizable resources incentivizes lower group cohesion. This creates17
differences in the social environment of tool-using and non-tool-using animals sharing a habitat, and reveals18
culture as a potential driver of social organization.19
Keywords: culture; primates; behavioral ecology; social structure; intragroup competition20

Introduction21
Across human societies, culture (i.e., socially-learned behaviors transmitted across and within generations) is22
intertwined with social structure. An individual’s learned behavioral repertoire is shaped by their close23
associations [1, 2] and culture influences how and with whom they associate [3–5]. Culture is also widespread24
in non-human animals [6–8]. The roles of social tolerance and social demography in the spread of cultural25
behavior have been considered theoretically [9–11] and experimentally [12–14]. However, the reverse26
influence, how culture shapes social structure, remains understudied [15]. Most research on this topic has27
focused on cetaceans [15, 16] with limited empirical evidence for culture affecting social structure. One28
example is the finding that dolphins who adopted a cultural behavior (a foraging technique of following29
trawling boats) became temporarily socially segregated from group members who did not adopt [17].30
Understanding the bi-directional relationship between culture and social bonds is key as both can affect31
fitness [7, 18–21]. Further, expanding this research to other animal taxa can help identify general32
mechanisms linking culture and social structure, also informing hypotheses about the role of these processes33
in hominin evolution. One way to examine the effect of culture on social structure in wild animals is by34
contrasting comparable groups of the same species with and without a particular cultural trait.35
Tool use and intragroup competition36
Percussive tool use is a tradition uniquely suited to this approach, because there is great within- and37
between-population variation in its expression. Hammerstone and anvil tool use is known to exist in at least38
four primate taxa chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes) [22], macaques (Macaca fascicularis spp.) [23, 24], robust39
capuchins (Sapajus spp. [25]), and gracile capuchins (Cebus capucinus imitator ) [26, 27]). While there is40
variation among taxa in which populations of the same or related species perform percussive tool use [25, 28,41
29], only gracile capuchins in Coiba National park show this variation at the species level in neighboring42
groups living on the same islands [26, 27].43
The presence of hammerstone and anvil tool use may affect social dynamics. A habitual reliance on tool44
use at fixed anvils creates repeated access to spatially fixed and monopolizable resources. In group-living45
species, such conditions are predicted to increase intragroup contest competition (i.e., direct competition over46
monopolizable resources), yet this has rarely been tested empirically. Evidence from bearded capuchin47
monkeys (Sapajus libidinosus) suggests increased competition in tool-using females, as they showed higher48
aggression rates at anvil sites, but the social structure of the study population was comparable to49
non-tool-using populations [30]. This hypothesis has not yet been evaluated in other species, perhaps due to50
the difficulty of ruling out ecological factors that might explain the lack of tool use and thus identifying51
appropriate comparisons. If tool use on monopolizable resources can indeed increase intragroup competition,52
how could this competition be mitigated, and what effect would it have on social dynamics?53
Group cohesion and fission-fusion dynamics54
Living in a social group is beneficial in myriad ways, including decreased predation risk, higher discovery55
rates of novel habitats and resources, and increased mating opportunities [31, 32]. However, living in groups56
also comes with drawbacks, such as more resource competition within the group, and higher risks of disease57
[31]. One way to reduce contest competition is by reducing spatial-temporal overlap among group members,58
thereby decreasing opportunities for direct competition over resources. This is consistent with findings from59
feral horse populations, where monopolizable resources (e.g., water) increased within-group contest60
competition and led to reduced social cohesion [33]. However, this reduced cohesion comes at the cost of61
increased predation vulnerability and intergroup competition. Social groups may balance these tradeoffs of62
group size via flexible spatiotemporal associations with group-members.63
The extent of variation in spatiotemporal cohesion of group members are called fission-fusion dynamics—64
a multi-dimensional, flexible concept reflecting temporal variation in i) spatial cohesion among group65
members, ii) party size and, iii) party composition [34]. Fission-fusion behavior is characterized by fission (a66
group splitting into smaller subgroups) and fusion (subgroups merging into a larger group) events [35]. It is67
flexible and occasionally inconsistent within species [34]. A higher degree of fission-fusion behavior can occur68

in response to environment, such as reduced predation risk, or more scarce or dispersed food resources. One69
study comparing two species ofSapajus capuchins found that the species experiencing less predation and70
more dispersed, low-quality resources foraged in subgroups more frequently (i.e., showed a higher degree of71
fission-fusion behavior) than the species with more predation and clumped, high-quality resources [36]. In72
various primate species, fissioning was found to occur more frequently when food resources were scarce73
[37–39]. Comparable fission–fusion dynamics are observed across non-primate taxa, where variation in74
predation risk and resource distribution shapes grouping decisions in ways consistent with ecological75
constraints models [31, 32, 40].76
A natural experiment in tool use and social dynamics77
Disentangling the effects of cultural behavior from ecological variation on social structure is challenging,78
because both often covary across populations and environments. One system where this can be addressed is79
white-faced capuchins (Cebus capucinus imitator ) living on Jicarón island, Coiba National Park, Panama.80
This island and the neighboring island of Coiba are the only two sites in the world where natural populations81
of gracile capuchins habitually perform percussive hammerstone and anvil tool use [26, 27]. On Jicarón, tool82
use is further localized to an approximately 2 km stretch of coast, occupied by a maximum of three capuchin83
groups [41]. Despite there being no physical boundaries, differences in material availability, or other clear84
ecological differences, habitual hammerstone and anvil tool use does not occur in capuchin groups living in85
other parts of the island [26]. It is therefore possible to directly compare tool-using and non-tool-using86
groups of the same species in the same habitat.87
Both tool-using and non-tool-using groups have minimal predation risk due to an absence of terrestrial88
predators [42] and show decreased vigilance and neophobia. Capuchins also live at high density [26, 43],89
which may lead to more relaxed intergroup interactions [44] — though on Barro Colorado Island, capuchins90
occur at high density yet have more intense intergroup encounters [45]. Predation pressure and intergroup91
competition are two major factors driving group cohesion [32, 46], and relaxation of these pressures on92
Jicarón means we expect the capuchins to be overall less socially cohesive than mainland capuchins. In93
long-term study sites on the mainland, white-faced capuchins are generally described to forage as a cohesive94
group and resting together (e.g. grooming, playing) at the hottest part of the day [47]. Capuchins might95
scatter widely while foraging [48, 49], but individuals do not generally travel independently of other group96
members and remain in contact via frequent vocalizations [49]. As such, they are not described as a species97
with a high degree of fission-fusion grouping.98
Due to the variation in tool use behavior within Jicarón island, we can directly assess whether presence of99
tool use is linked to differences in social dynamics. Because terrestrial predators are absent on Jicarón and100
both groups experience similar environmental conditions, this system offers a rare opportunity to isolate the101
potential effects of competition over monopolizable resources. Comparable to findings by [30], there might be102
increased competition over spatially fixed, usurpable anvil sites, favoring solo foraging or less cohesive103
grouping to mitigate this competition. The stone tool use on Jicarón appears to be a largely solitary activity104
[50], and during in-person observations the tool-using group appears very spread out, providing some support105
for this hypothesis. Furthermore, since tool-use is an exclusively male activity on Jicarón [51], only males106
would be predicted to experience increased intragroup competition, which could have cascading effects on107
both within- and between-sex social interactions.108
Aim109
Here, we systematically assess this question by comparing one group of tool-using and one group of110
non-tool-using white-faced capuchins of similar size and composition living on Jicarón island in Coiba111
National Park, Panama. Both groups border the coast, where similar resources are available (e.g., coastal112
species like sea almonds, as well as intertidal resources). Using two grids of camera traps, we compare daily113
activity patterns and fission-fusion dynamics between tool-using and non-tool-using capuchins. Following114
[34], we quantify the degree of fission-fusion dynamics through temporal variation in three dimensions: 1)115
party size, 2) party composition, and 3) spatial cohesion. We developed an agent-based group movement116
model to test specific, quantitative predictions based on our hypotheses. Agents could either move (a)117

Results124
Daily activity patterns125
The daily activity of tool-using and non-tool-using groups overlapped considerably (coefficient of overlap =126
0.857, 95 % CI [0.854-0.860]) (Fig. 2). The non-tool-using group showed slightly more activity in the127
morning, and the tool-using group considerable more activity in the afternoon. A striking difference is the128
presence of nocturnal terrestrial (after sunset and before sunrise) activity by the tool-using group, which was129
absent in the non-tool-using group. Six sequences (a sequence is all images from a camera trap triggered130
within 30 seconds of one another) show capuchins from the tool-using group traveling on the ground between131
19:00 and 05:00, when it is dark. For the tool-using group, there was also one camera location where132
capuchin sightings were two times higher in the morning (< 7:00) and evening (> 17:00) than at other133
cameras, which might be located near a likely sleep-site. For the non-tool-using group there was no single134
camera location with much greater capuchin activity in the early morning and late evening.135

Party composition163
Due to camera traps’ limited view and the quick passage of many individuals, reliable aging and sexing of164
individual capuchins was not feasible in all sequences. Adult sex was most reliably classified (sex assigned to165
93% of observed adults), and as such we focused on presence of adult males and adult females to compare166
party composition. Adult females were more likely to be seen together in the non-tool-using group than in167
the tool-using group (for model estimates see Table S3). Our model estimated a higher mean number of168
adult females present in a sequence in the non-tool-using group (0.36, 95% CI [0.31, 0.42]) than in the169
tool-using group (0.27, 95% CI [0.22, 0.31]), and evidence for this effect was strong (PP > 0: 0.98). Adult170
males were also more likely to be seen together in the non-tool-using group than in the tool-using group (see171
Table 4 for model estimates). Our model comparing the number of adult males estimated strong evidence172
(PP > 0: ≈ 1) for a higher mean number of adult males present in a sequence in the non-tool-using group173
(0.32, 95% CI [0.26, 0.38]) than in the tool-using group (0.18, 95% CI [0.15, 0.22]). Both models estimated a174
different relationship between the number of adult females and number of adult males present for the175
tool-using and non-tool-using group (see Fig. S6). In the non-tool-using group the models estimated a176
positive relationship: the more adult males were observed, the more adult females were observed. In the177
tool-using group, the relationship was negative: the more adult males observed, the fewer adult females.178

Spatial cohesion179
Co-occurrences180
When the time between subsequent detections was high and the distance too great for a capuchin to have181
been able to cross it in that time, these “co-occurrences” likely do not reflect the same party being captured.182
We therefore used a threshold of detections >150 m apart within 1 minute, which would require movement183
speeds of ∼9 km/h, well above observed white-faced capuchin travel speeds of∼0.3–2 km/h during active184
movement bouts [53]). When such co-occurrences occurred within a group’s known range, we interpreted185
them as evidence of subgrouping events. Since we hypothesize the tool-using group is less cohesive to avoid186
competition over anvil sites, we considered whether anvils are actually used at the same time. We looked for187
such evidence of subgrouping in the tool-use group by taking a sample of camera traps on anvil sites in their188
known range and using a cut-off time of 60 seconds at 150 meters between sightings to identify189
co-occurrences. Over a 20-month period, we found 217 such co-occurrences (Fig. S7).190
Spatiotemporal cohesion191
We assessed spatiotemporal cohesion by focusing on distance (in meters) and time (in seconds) between192
subsequent capuchin sightings within the same day, reflecting how capuchins trigger cameras as they move193
through the grid space. Different spatiotemporal patterns emerged for the tool-using and non-tool-using194
group (Fig. 4, see Table S5 for model estimates). If the distance between subsequent sightings was 0 meters,195
the next capuchin sighting was at the same camera. Non-tool-using capuchins were more likely to trigger the196
same camera again than tool-using capuchins (Fig. 4C). We found strong evidence (PP > 0:≈ 1, Fig. 4D)197
for the probability of a subsequent sighting at the same camera being higher in the non-tool-using group198
(estimated probability of 0 = 0.35, 95% CI [0.28, 0.43]) than the tool-using group (estimated probability of 0199
= 0.29 95% CI [0.21, 0.35]). If the subsequent sighting was at a different camera (distance > 0), the distance200
to the new camera was slightly higher in the tool-using group (264 m, 95% CI [238 m, 294 m]) than the201
non-tool-using group (244 m, 95% CI [218 m, 269 m]), though evidence for this effect was moderate (PP >0:202
0.82, Fig. 4F & G). For both tool-users and non-tool-users, increased time between subsequent sightings was203
associated with greater distances between the cameras being triggered. However, tool-users showed more204
sightings short in time but further in space than the non-tool-users, resulting in different patterns in the link205
between distance and time (Fig. 4A).206
To interpret these patterns, we again compared to the most similar condition in agent-based simulations207
(20 capuchins, 25 cameras). Here too, we found that varying levels of cohesion result in distinguishable208
variation in the relationship between distance and time between subsequent sightings (Fig. 4B, see Table S6209
for model estimates). Simulated capuchins moving together had a higher probability of subsequent detections210
at the same camera than capuchins moving in two subgroups (PP > 0:≈ 1), Fig. 4E), while capuchins211
moving in subgroups had higher distances between subsequent detections not at the same camera (PP > 0:212
≈ 1), Fig. 4H). Varying capuchin number (10–50) and camera coverage (4–81 cameras) led to qualitatively213
similar trends (Figs. S8 & S9). The different spatiotemporal patterns of the tool-using and non-tool-using214
capuchins are also visible in our animation of capuchin sightings (online supplement).215

Discussion216
We investigated whether increased intragroup competition from habitual tool use in white-faced capuchins is217
linked to reduced group cohesion. Using two grids of camera traps, we compared the social cohesion of one218
group of tool-using and one group of non-tool-using white-faced capuchins sharing the same habitat and219
located 3 km apart on the same island. We found that, despite large overlap in daily activity patterns and220
only 3 kilometers of distance between them, there are marked differences in group cohesion between the221
tool-using and non-tool-using groups.222
We measured cohesion following [34] as temporal variation in i) party size, ii) party composition and iii)223
spatial cohesion. The tool-using group had a higher probability of singleton detections, and smaller observed224
party sizes than the non-tool-using capuchins. Simulations show that these patterns emerge with reduced225
cohesion (subgrouping), and these relationships were robust across a wide range of group sizes and camera226
configuration. The differences in party composition between the two groups—despite comparable group227
composition—also supported the hypothesis of a more cohesive non-tool-using group and a tool-using group228
which is less cohesive. In any one observation, capuchin parties in the tool-using group had fewer adult males,229
fewer adult females, and fewer adult males and females together than parties in the non-tool-using group.230
Patterns of spatiotemporal cohesion further supported this interpretation. For the non-tool-users, capuchin231
detections often occurred at cameras neighboring the camera of the previous sighting, in line with cameras232
being triggered by a group traveling in a specific direction. In contrast, for the tool-using group, we see that233
subsequent detections are not usually at a neighboring camera, but rather somewhere else on the grid entirely,234
as expected when capturing several subgroups rather than one big group, as supported by our simulations.235
An advantage of using two comparable grids of camera traps is that they allow direct comparison of236
cohesion between the tool-using and non-tool-using groups under similar sampling conditions. However,237
because these grid cameras do not capture tool use itself, they do not directly link reduced cohesion to its238
hypothesized driver: competition over tool-use sites. The numerous simultaneous sightings at different anvils239
within the tool use group’s range provide direct behavioral evidence of spatially separated subgroups and are240
consistent with the interpretation that competition over tool-use sites promotes fission–fusion dynamics in241
the tool-using group. While the non-tool-using group shows social cohesion comparable to that described for242
the species, the tool-using group exhibits a more fission–fusion-like structure than is typically described for243
white-faced capuchins in long-term field studies. Importantly, reduced cohesion was not limited to the males244
that perform tool use. Differences in party composition and female co-occurrence suggest that altered245
grouping patterns extend across the group, indicating that the effects of this behavioral tradition may246
cascade beyond the individuals directly engaging in tool use.247
Tool use linked to reduced group cohesion248
Our findings provide evidence for a potential link between habitual tool use at fixed, monopolizable resources249
and reduced group cohesion. The tool-using group and non-tool-using groups inhabit the same habitat with250
comparable resource availability. Yet they differ behaviorally: the tool-using group has a habitual stone tool251
use tradition which has persisted over two decades [26]. Here, we show that the social cohesion of these two252
groups also differs. On Jicarón, where predators are absent and capuchins are more terrestrial [42], the253
selective pressure to maintain tight social cohesion may be relaxed. However, because both groups share this254
environment, predation cannot explain the observed between-group differences. Instead, these differences are255
consistent with a role of habitual tool use on monopolizable resources in shifting social organization towards256
being less cohesive and more fission-fusion-like. In the tool-using group’s range, at least 10 heavily used anvil257
sites near sea almond or coconut trees are fixed and monopolizable, previously hypothesized to generate258
intragroup contest competition [26], as observed in bearded capuchins [30]. Such competition can be259
mitigated through agonistic displacement, as seen in mainland white-faced capuchins where higher ranking260
individuals secure greater energy intake than lower ranking individuals by displacing them from feeding261
patches [54]. However, on Jicarón, direct agonistic interactions at anvils are incredibly rare [50, 51]. The262
intensity of intragroup competition can also be mitigated via reduced group cohesion [55–57], which appears263
to be the case on Jicarón, where competition over anvils may be reduced through spatial separation.264
Comparative data from mainland and other island populations will be necessary to disentangle the relative265

Differences in space use could influence camera detections and therefore represent an alternative267
explanation for some observed patterns. Mainland white-faced capuchins in neotropical dry forests in Costa268
Rica occupy home ranges with median estimates of 1.82km2 [58], with slightly lower estimates from the269
tropical moist forests of Barro Colorado Island, Panama [45, 59]. We lack precise data on the home range270
sizes of capuchin groups on Jicarón, but the minimum extent appears comparable to in other tropical moist271
forests (at least 1km2)[45, 59]. Nevertheless, differences in home range configuration and movement272
patterns, potentially associated with concentrated use of anvil sites and intertidal foraging by tool users [41],273
could influence detection patterns and mean that our grids sampled different portions of each group’s range.274
This is supported by the presence of nocturnal detections in the tool-using grid, absent in the non-tool-using275
grid, indicating that we likely sampled sleep sites in the former, and/or that they have different nocturnal276
behavior. More generally, variation in space use, resource distribution, and social cohesion are likely277
interconnected, and future work integrating spatial ecology with behavioral observations will be needed to278
disentangle these effects.279
Previous studies have shown that fission-fusion dynamics are highly flexible [34] and can vary seasonally,280
for instance in response to fluctuations in food availability [37–39]. Tool use on Jicarón also has a seasonal281
component [26]; we observe peaks in tool use frequencies in the transitions between wet and dry season (see282
[50]) The camera traps used in this study were deployed for 7 months, overlapping in time with at least one283
of the peaks in tool use activity. As such, the reduced cohesion we observed by the tool-using group may284
reflect their grouping dynamics only during this time period of increased competition. It is possible that285
cohesion increases when tool use frequency decreases. Further research is needed over larger timescales to286
shed light on seasonal components of grouping dynamics, also taking into account fluctuations in availability287
of other food resources which may affect intragroup competition and cohesion for tool-using and288
non-tool-using capuchins on Jicarón alike.289
Cascading effects of a sex-biased tool use tradition290
Despite tool use on Jicarón being restricted to males [51]—and thus only males experiencing contest291
competition at anvils—our results suggest the whole tool-using group shows reduced group cohesion. This292
has several important implications for their social system and behavior. First, in well-studied populations of293
white-faced capuchins at mainland sites,females are typically philopatric while males typically disperse [47],294
leading to social bonds of females being having large impacts on coalition formation, foraging access, and295
survival [60] . While we did not measure detailed social behavior in this study, we do see that adult females296
are much less likely to co-occur in the tool-using group than in the non-tool-using group and thus seemingly297
have fewer opportunities for social contact. This would be a fruitful area for future research. Second, the298
male-exclusive nature of tool use in this population combined with reduced cohesion likely results in some299
degree of sexual segregation in space, at least at anvil sites, where females are observed less frequently than300
other age-sex classes [51]. This segregation, combined with the reduced group cohesion, affects social learning301
opportunities for immature individuals who are still dependent on their mothers, as it limits their302
observations of tool use and interactions with male group members. Our previous research found that social303
attention during tool use events is relatively rare [50], yet the persistence of the tool use tradition for at least304
20 years suggests young individuals are capable of acquiring the behavior.305
Implications for intergroup dynamics306
The reduced group cohesion in the tool-using capuchins may make them less competitive during intergroup307
conflicts. In mainland sites, white-faced capuchins are known to be xenophobic with hostile intergroup308
encounters [47, 53, 61]. On Jicarón, we know little about the nature of interactions between groups, personal309
observations suggest intergroup competition may be more relaxed, as on other islands [62, 63]. Due to the310
small size of the island and its genetic isolation, it is possible the capuchins have a high degree of inbreeding,311
and, as a result are less capable of differentiating in-group from out-group members, resulting in lower312
out-group aggression [64]. However, higher relatedness would account for differences in behavior between313
capuchins on Jicarón and mainland capuchins, not between tool-using and non-tool-using capuchins within314
10

PDF PAGE 11
Jicarón island. More information is needed to assess whether intergroup conflicts on Jicarón is comparable to315
that observed in mainland populations, and to test if the tool-using group suffers a competitive disadvantage316
from reduced group cohesion. The observed difference in group cohesion within such a small geographic scale317
(a 20 km2 island) also raises interesting questions regarding dispersal. While the details of the dispersal318
system on Jicarón are still unclear [26], dispersal between the tool-using and non-tool-using group is likely319
possible, as they are only 3 kilometers apart. As such, a non-tool-using capuchin migrating into the tool-using320
group would not only be faced with the novel behavior of tool use, but also a less cohesive social group than321
it was used to. Whether migrants would adapt to the cohesiveness of their new social group is a question that322
has not previously been explored, but could provide fascinating insights into the flexibility of social systems.323
Estimating cohesion using camera traps324
Estimating group cohesion from camera trap data presents several challenges. Detection is constrained by325
camera-specific fields of view, and identification of individuals is often limited by image quality. This326
challenge is compounded in arboreal species such as capuchins, where a lack of detections by ground-level327
cameras does not necessarily indicate true absence. Arboreal camera traps on Jicarón indicate diel variation328
in arboreality in non-tool-using capuchins [42, 65], suggesting that patterns of vertical space use may further329
influence detection probabilities. Placing arboreal camera traps in the tool-using group’s range would be330
necessary to assess whether such patterns differ between groups. Improved individual identification, for331
example using deep-learning approaches, could further enable reconstruction of social networks from camera332
trap data, as demonstrated in other primate systems [66, 67]. While we accounted for some of this variation333
statistically (e.g., by including camera identity as a random effect and focusing on numbers of individuals334
detected), camera trap data alone cannot definitively resolve whether observed patterns reflect true335
differences in cohesion or sampling artifacts.336
To address these limitations of camera traps, we combined empirical data with agent-based simulations337
that explicitly model how groups with different levels of cohesion would be detected by a grid of camera338
traps. These simulations provide a mechanistic link between underlying social structure and observed339
spatiotemporal detection patterns, allowing us to interpret empirical differences in terms of variation in340
cohesion rather than sampling bias alone. Importantly, the simulations also revealed that the ability to341
distinguish a cohesive group from sub-groups depends on sampling intensity: under lower camera coverage,342
detection patterns increasingly overlap (Figs. S2, S3, & S8). More broadly, this approach demonstrates how343
integrating camera trap data with process-based simulations adds rigor, and can extend the inference space344
of camera trap studies, particularly for species where direct observation of group structure is difficult.345
Conclusion346
Our results suggest that habitual tool use at fixed, monopolizable resources is associated with reduced social347
cohesion in wild white-faced capuchins. By comparing two groups of the same species sharing the same348
environment, but differing from one another in their tool-use behavior, we provide evidence that tool use349
itself could drive this difference in social cohesion. Given that group-living is a balancing act of advantages350
and disadvantages, this reduced group cohesion can likely only emerge in specific environments where the351
costs of fissioning are low, such as environments with few predators. On Jicarón, the absence of predators352
may allow the benefits of fissioning to use tools without competition to outweigh the costs of reduced353
cohesion. While tool use is restricted to males, its effects on social cohesion appear to extend beyond males,354
influencing group-wide dynamics, including adult social bonds and juvenile learning opportunities. Future355
research should aim to better contextualize the observed patterns by comparing island to mainland groups to356
assess the influence of island-living on group cohesion, and by conducting longer-term monitoring to capture357
potential seasonal variation in both tool use and cohesion. Studying these dynamics provides rare empirical358
insight into the bidirectional relationship between culture and social structure, highlighting how culturally359
mediated behaviors might reshape patterns of social association.360

Methods361
Site362
Jicarón island (20 km2) is located in Coiba National Park, an UNESCO World Heritage site 60 kilometers off363
the Pacific coast of Panama. It is uninhabited and only sees infrequent human activity in the form of364
scientific research and, rarely, ecotourism.365
Capuchins on Jicarón use hammerstones and anvils to access a variety of resources, ranging from fruits366
like sea almonds (Terminalia catappa), coconuts (Cocos nucifera) and palm fruits (Bactris major ) to367
invertebrates like Halloween crabs (Gecarcinus quadratus), hermit crabs (Coenobita compressus), and nerite368
snails (Nerita sp.)[26]. Habitual stone tool use is limited to a∼2 km stretch of coast inhabited by an369
estimated 3 social groups [41]. Of these three groups, only one shows tool use at high accumulation sites (i.e.,370
anvils). The other two neighboring groups have been observed to use tools in the intertidal zone and riparian371
banks primarily producing ephemeral sites (C. Monteza-Moreno, pers. comm., 2023; B. Barrett pers.372
observation).373
The group of capuchins who use tools at anvil sites, referred to as the ‘tool-using group’, have been374
performing hammerstone and anvil tool use since at least 2004 [26]. They have been monitored using375
unbaited camera traps since 2017, with most sampling efforts focusing on 12 frequently used anvil sites.376
Grid deployment377
To compare the cohesion of the tool-using group to a non-tool-using group, we placed two grids of 26 camera378
traps with 100 meter spacing in both the tool-using group’s range and at a location on the other side of the379
island (approximately 3 kilometers away) between May 2022 and January 2023. We planned the grid380
placement by creating a 100-meter raster and overlaying this over satellite imagery of the field site (inR381
using the packagessf [68], raster [69] and mapview [70]). We had some information on the tool using382
group’s home range, due to the high sampling since 2017, and placed the grid to fall within their expected383
range. For the non-tool-using group, we had very limited information on their movement patterns, and thus384
placed a grid in an area where we had previously captured photos of capuchins during camera trap surveys.385
After placement we evaluated whether we captured a single group or the boundary between several groups386
(see detailed methods below). We selected this area to be as far away from the tool-using group’s range as387
possible, while still sharing similar features (bordering the coast near streams and sea almond trees), and388
oriented both grids to be parallel to the coast.389
From the 100 meter raster, we generated coordinates of 40 possible camera locations using the center390
coordinates of each cell, of which 30 were preferred placement locations and 10 back up locations. Due to the391
steep topography on Jicarón, we aimed to have a range of randomly selected, equally spaced possible camera392
locations, and see how many were physically reachable. The placement went as followed: we navigated to a393
predesignated grid location stored on a handheld GPS (Garmin GPSMAP66i), and located a tree on which394
the camera could be mounted, ideally within 15 meters of the intended GPS coordinates. Each camera was395
placed on a tree at knee height (around 0.6 meters), facing a random direction (not facing into a hill or other396
obstacle). Cameras were tested using the Walktest function to see if it would trigger from movement on the397
ground. In the end, we successfully placed 26 cameras in the tool-using group’s range, and 26 cameras in the398
non-tool-using group. Each grid camera was placed on average 10.63 meters (range 0.96-31.41) from the399
intended point, on a tree facing a random direction. All cameras were still cameras (Reconyx Hyperfire400
HF2X), and programmed to take to take 20 pictures per trigger with no delays between-triggers (approx. 1 s401
between images).402
Annotation403
Following collection of all cameras, triggers within 30 seconds of one another were clustered together into a404
single sequence. Each sequence was coded on www.agouti.eu [71], coding how many animals were present405
and of which species. For each capuchin present, we determined their age and sex when possible, attempted406
to assign an individual ID, and coded their behavior following the ethogram standard to our project [72].407

Group size of tool-using and non-tool-using group413
Before proceeding with analyses, we had to verify that the grids actually each captured a single social group.414
Due to the long-term monitoring of the tool-using group, we can reliably identify most group members,415
allowing us to be confident that nearly all of our cameras in their range only captured known individuals.416

For the non-tool-using capuchins, we identified as many individuals as possible based on visual421

Statistical analyses433
We ran all analyses in R v. 4.3.1 [74]. All Bayesian models were fit via thebrm function in thebrms package434
v. 2.16.1 [75]. To evaluate the credibility of the difference between categories we used thehypothesis435
function in brms and estimated means on the response scale using theemmeans package [76]. We considered436
what proportion of the posterior probability (PP) of the contrast was greater than 0, using a cut-off of 0.95437
and above to reflect strong evidence for an effect, 0.80 and above as moderate evidence, and below 0.80 as438
weak evidence. For each model, we performed a prior predictive simulation to compare our chosen priors to439
default priors, and to evaluate parameter identifiability. For the final models, we ran three chains of 3000440
iterations each, with a 1500 iterations warm-up per chain. Our models were stable with Pareto k estimates441
below 0.7 and large effective sample sizes (Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS over 1000 for all estimates [75] and Rhat442
values ≤ 1.01 [77]. We visually assessed model fit and confirmed our choice of priors using the posterior443
predictive check function.444

Intra-diel activity445
We quantified intra-diel activity of the tool-using and non-tool-using groups using Bayesian hierarchical446
generalized additive models (GAMs), following the method described by [78]. For each group, we modeled447
the probability of activity (detecting a capuchin on a camera trap) as a cyclic smooth function of time of day448
(0-23 h) using a binomial likelihood and a circular spline (bs = “cc”), with camera trap location included as a449
random intercept. We generated posterior predictions over a fine temporal grid while marginalizing over450
camera traps, and computed the coefficient of overlap [79] by applying the posterior-draw-wise minimum451
function to the two predicted activity curves. This created a posterior distribution of overlap values ranging452
from 0 (no overlap in activity) to 1 (complete overlap). Additionally, we considered the locations of the first453
and last sightings of the day, to examine if sleep sites of the capuchins were likely captured in our grid.454
Party size455
With the term ‘party size’ we refer to the number of capuchins captured in a sequence together. Data was456
heavily 1-inflated — 69.02% of sequences only contained a single capuchin. Capturing larger parties of457
capuchins was rare at both sites. To account for this skew in analyses, we subtracted 1 from the party size to458
create a variable where 0’s reflect captures of individuals alone (no social partners), and numbers above459
reflect the number of partners present. We refer to this variable as ‘social party size’ in order to differentiate460
it from the normally used ‘party size’ for the total number of individuals present. To compare mean social461
party size between the tool-using and non-tool-using group, we ran a hurdle Poisson GLMM (model462
sps_bm1a). For the non-zero component of the model, the outcome was the number of partners in the463
sequence (total number of capuchins minus 1), and the predictor variables the grid location (tool-using or464
non-tool-using group, with non-tool-using as reference) as well as a random effect for each camera. For the465
zero-component of the model, we included the predictors of grid location and varying effects of camera466
location, under the assumption that both could affect single capuchin detection frequency.467
Party composition468
We were unable to reliably estimate age or sex in 25.88% of the capuchins observed, mostly because only part469
of the capuchin was visible, or because it was not possible to determine if they were subadult or juvenile.470
Adults were easiest to identify — we reliably classified their sex in 93.22% of cases. Therefore, to compare471
party composition between the tool-using and non-tool-using group, we focused on adult males and adult472
females as these age-sex classes could be identified most reliably. As these variables contained many zeros473
(ranging from 70-74%), and these zeros could both reflect true absence as well as be a result of sampling, we474
used zero-inflated models, since these models assume the presence of both ‘true’ and ‘sampling’ zeros. To475
compare the number of adult females in a party between the tool-using and non-tool-using group, we ran a476
zero-inflated Poisson GLMM (model pc_bm1). The outcome was the number of adult females in a sequence,477
and the predictors grid location and an interaction of grid location with the number of adult males in a478
sequence. Camera location was fit as a random effect. We ran another zero-inflated Poisson GLMM (model479
pc_bm2) with the same structure except the number of adult males as the outcome, and the number of adult480
females as a predictor.481
Spatial cohesion482
To compare covariance in time as well as space between the grid locations, we focused on subsequent483
capuchin sightings within the same day. Assuming both cohesive and less cohesive capuchin groups likely484
sleep together at night, we first separated all detections by day. Within a day, we assigned an increasing485
number to each observation of capuchins (so the first observation of the day a 1, the second a 2, and so on).486
The first sighting of a day served as the starting point. From this we calculated for each subsequent sighting487
i) how far away in space this observation was from the previous observation (0 meters for the same camera,488
and otherwise the distance between the cameras in meters) and ii) how far away in time this observation was489
from the previous (how many seconds passed). We then assessed the relationship between time and space490
between sightings for the tool-using and non-tool-using group separately, assuming that a cohesive group and491
14

PDF PAGE 15
less cohesive group would show different patterns. In a cohesive group, subsequent sightings would be492
expected to mostly occur close in both time and space to the previous sighting. In contrast, a fission–fusion493
group would also show subsequent sightings that are close in time but far apart in space. To test this494
hypothesis, we ran a hurdle gamma GLMM (model_spacetime). The outcome was the distance between495
subsequent sightings (in meters) and the predictors an interaction of grid location and the time in seconds496
(on the log-scale) between sightings, as well as camera location as a random effect.497
Furthermore, when individuals of the same group are detected close in time but far apart in space at498
cameras at a great distance, this can indicate that they have fissioned into sub-parties. Triggers of 150499
meters apart within 1 minute would be very unlikely to originate from one cohesive group of capuchins, given500
that observed capuchins’ travel speed (0.3-2 km/h; [53]) are far lower. We identified what we term as501
‘co-occurrences’ by extrapolating this conservative rule (so 300 meters in 2 minutes, and so on) and flagging502
observations that occurred within these criteria. Given the importance of being certain that both triggers503
came from individuals from the social group, rather than two distinct groups being captured, we focused on504
camera traps placed inside the tool-using group’s range, where we have never observed unknown individuals.505
Since we hypothesize that competition over anvil sites is driving the reduced cohesion in the tool-using group,506
we only included anvil sites. For this, we used our wider dataset (described here [41]), coded in Agouti in the507
same manner as the grid data. We focused on a sub-sample of the data, only including sequences that508
contained capuchins at anvil sites. We also filtered to a period when all camera traps were coded, and at509
least four camera traps at anvil sites were deployed at the same time, resulting in inclusion of 34 camera510
traps deployed at anvil sites in the tool-using group’s range between July 2017 and March 2019. Lastly, we511
visualized capuchin sightings within 10 days at the two grids. We selected a period when both grids had512
multiple capuchin sightings within a day, and animated these sightings using thegganimate package [80].513
Simulations514
To simulate camera trap observations of collectively moving groups with different levels of cohesion, first, we515
set up a100 × 100 arena with a reflecting boundary. Agents were initialized at uniformly random locations in516
a 2 × 2 box around a randomly chosen location in the arena. The movement of agents followed the Vicsek517
model, a commonly used model of collective movement [81]. The agents moved at a speed of 0.03 units per518
iteration, and could interact with other individuals in a radius of 1 unit. We used a constant Vicsekη noise519
parameter of 0.1. Group cohesion was controlled by using a pre-specified adjacency matrixA, wherein Aij520
was 1 if individualsi and j could interact, and 0 otherwise. We ran the models for 5,000 iterations. We521
performed 100 such repeats of each model.522
8 different camera traps setups were chosen. These represented varying camera trap densities, populating523
the arena with22, 32, 42 · · · 92 cameras respectively. Each camera was assigned a random sighting radius524
between 0.3 and 1.0 units. When any individual was present within a camera’s sighting radius, a sighting was525
recorded at that camera for that timestep.526
We explored three different group cohesion conditions. In the first batch of simulations, we set all527
elements of the adjacency matrix to 1 (i.e., all agents could interact with each other), resulting in one528
cohesively moving group. In the second batch of simulations, adjacency matrix values were 1 between all529
individuals in the first and second halves of the group respectively, but 0 across these two halves (i.e., there530
were two interacting subgroups, but interactions between members of these subgroups was not possible). In531
the third batch of simulations, all adjacency matrix values were set to 0 (i.e., individuals moved532
independently of each other). This was repeated for groups of sizes 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 individuals.533
As a result, a total of3 × 100 × 5 = 1 , 500 trajectories were generated. We then used the above mentioned534
eight camera trap setups to generate observations based on all these trajectories, resulting in535
1, 500 × 8 = 12 , 000 simulated camera trap datasets.536
