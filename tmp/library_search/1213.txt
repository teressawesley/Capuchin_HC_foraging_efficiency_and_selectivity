Percussive tool use for extractive foraging allows animals  to access otherwise inaccessible resources and  
forage more  efficiently, with  potentially important  implications for their fitness. The  development  of 
tool use proficiency has been  w ell documented  in nut-cracking chimpanzees,  Pan  troglodytes, and  robust 
capuchins, Sapajus spp., and  in shellfish-cracking longtailed macaq ues, Macaca  fascicularis, where  
mothers  and  proficient tool users are the most  important  models  for social learning. How ever, little is 
known  about  how  tool use develops in populations where  opportunities for social learning are scarce.
White-faced capuchins, Cebus capucinus imitator, on  Jicar� on  Island, Panama,  provide a unique  oppor-
tunity to address this question: stone tool use is entirely male  biased, meaning  juveniles cannot  learn 
from  their mothers, and  reduced  group  cohesion further limits opportunities for social learning. W e 
investigated  the acquisition and  development  of stone tool proficiency in this population using a data 
set collected over a 1-year period from  camera  traps placed at two  experimental  anvils. W e assessed 
differences in proficiency between  age classes, examined  the development  of tool use proficiency over 
time  and  explored patterns of social attention during tool use. Juvenile capuchins  were less proficient 
than subadults and  adults, but their proficiency remained  stable over the 12-month  study period, 
suggesting that skill development  ma y require prolonged  practice or physical maturation.  In contrast to
other primates, opportunities for social learning on  Jicar� on  appeared  to be limited and  scrounging w as
rare, yet when  social attention occurred, w e found  consistent patterns. Social attention to tool use 
mostly  came  from  juveniles too young  to use tools themselves, who  observed  proficient subadults that 
tolerated scrounging. Our  results contribute to understanding  how  comple x tool use behaviours are 
acquired and  maintained.  They  highlight that even  when  tool use is largely solitary, directed  social 
attention and  social tolerance  play an  important  role in the development  of tool use proficiency.

Tool use is a striking example  of behavioural flexibility in ani-
mals, and  the use of tools for extractive foraging can increase fitness 
by  allowing  faster access to a greater variety of resources (Biro et al., 
2013). How ever, becoming  proficient in tool use can take years, 
depending  on  the comple xity of the behaviour (Boesch et al., 2019). 
A behaviour is considered tool use when  an  animal  uses an  object 
that is not part of its own  body, is unattached to the surrounding
environment  or attached and  capable of being manipulated 
(Shumaker  et al., 2011) and  manipulates the object to achieve a 
useful outcome  (Beck, 1980). To describe the comple xity of tool use 
behaviour, a distinction can be made  betwee n first-order tool use 
involving a single object (e.g. pounding  a nut on  a fixed surface) and  
second-order tool use involving two  objects (e.g. pounding  a nut on  
a fixed surface using a hammerstone:  Fragaszy et al., 2004). Second-
order tool use is thought to reflect greater cognitive comple xity, as it 
requires more  planning and  physical coordination to correctly bring 
together all the separate objects to obtain the desired outcome.

hammerstone s and  anvils, is the focus of substantial scientific

relative rarity in the animal  kingdom,  despite its prominent  (and  
archaeologically preservable) role in human  evolution (de  la Torre & 
Hirata, 2015). The  only nonhuman  wild primates that regularly use 
stone tools for extractive foraging are chimpanzees, Pan  troglodytes 
(Sugiyama  & Koman,  1979), longtailed macaq ues, Macaca  fas-
cicularis (Gumert  & Malaivijitnond, 2012; Muhammad  et al., 2024), 
robust capuchins, Sapajus spp. (Canale et al., 2009; Lima et al., 2024; 
Ottoni & Mannu,  2001; Spagnoletti et al., 2011) and  gracile capu-
chins, Cebus spp. (Araujo et al., 2021; Barrett et al., 2018). Among  
Macaca  and  Cebus, tool use is further restricted to only a few  groups  
or spatially isolated populations. Examining how  stone tool use is 
acquired and  how  proficiency develops is key to understanding how  
a tool use tradition is maintained  within a group, as well as whether  
and  how  it spreads betwee n groups.
For  particularly comple x techniques like percussive stone tool 
use, acquisition of the skill may  require sufficient exposure at a 
young  age. In robust capuchins  (De  Resende  et al., 2008) and  
macaq ues (Tan, 2017), acquisition of tool use also mostly  occurs 
early in life. In chimpanzees,  insufficient exposure  to nut cracking 
during a critical learning period (between  3 and  10 years of age) 
may  hinder or completely impede  learning of the behaviour in 
adulthood  (Biro et al., 2003; Inoue-Nakamur a & Matsuzaw a, 1997; 
Matsuzaw a, 1994). Exposure to tool use has an  important  social 
component:  in both  robust capuchins  and  chimpanzees,  immatur e 
individuals are thought  to acquire nut cracking via social learning 
processes such  as local enhancement,  emulation  and  social facil-
itation (Biro et al., 2003; Coelho et al., 2015; Eshchar et al., 2016; 
Ottoni et al., 2005 ). Although  some  authors have argued  that social 
learning is not critical to the acquisition of nut cracking in captive 
chimpanzees  (Neadle  et al., 2020), evidence from  wild chimpan -
zees continues to support an  important  role for social learning 
(Koops  et al., 2022).
In chimpanzees,  most  transmission  of tool use behaviours oc-
curs from  mothers  to their offspring (Boesch, 1991; Lonsdorf, 
2005 ). Juveniles spend  a great deal of time  with  their mothers  
during the sensitive learning period, and  mothers  are highly 
tolerant of their offspring observing their tool use events and  
scrounging  from  opened  nuts. There is also some  evidence of fe-
male  chimpanzees  providing learning opportunities for their 
offspring through  ‘scaffolding’ (sharing nut-cracking materials 
such  as hammers  with  their offspring: Estienne et al., 2019; 
Musgra ve et al., 2016). In contrast, robust capuchin  mothers  were 
not common  models  for their offspring. Rather, juveniles prefer-
entially observed  tool use events performed  by  proficient tool 
users (Coelho et al., 2024; Ottoni et al., 2005 ), who  were older and  
more  dominant  than themselves (Coelho et al., 2015). Social 
closeness and  physical proximity did not affect whom  juveniles 
chose  to observe. How ever, they did prefer individuals who  were 
more  tolerant of observers, and  scrounging occurred in 75%  of tool 
use events (Coelho et al., 2015).
High  interindividual social tolerance  is thought  to be an  
important  precondition for tool use acquisition (van  Schaik et al., 
1999), as observational learning of a comple x skill requires close 
visual attention to tool use activities. Being in such  close proximity 
to an  intolerant tool user is risky for an  observer, who  may  be 
chased  aw ay or attacked. Empirical evidence from  bearded  capu-
chins, Sapajus libidinosus, supports the importance  of social 
tolerance, showing  that it facilitates information transfer in an  
extractive foraging task (Coelho et al., 2024). High  social tolerance  
often provides the opportunity to scrounge, which  not only gives 
naïve individuals the chance  to observe the end  result of tool use, 
but also allows  them  a share of the payoff, thus increasing their 
motivation to acquire tool use themselves (Fragaszy et al., 2013). 
After acquisition, honing  tool use skills can be a lengthy process.
Younger, active learners spend  more  time  acquiring tool use and  
are less proficient than experienced  adults (Tan, 2017). How ever, in 
bearded  capuchins, juveniles did not make  more  mistakes than 
adults during tool use. Rather, juveniles’ lower proficiency mainly  
resulted from  less expertise in how  to grip the hammerstone  
(Fragaszy et al., 2020). Because of differences in skill and/or 
strength, young er individuals may  also be limited in the resources 
they can open  (Spagnoletti et al., 2011). Furthermore, percussive 
stone tool use might  have an  upper  age limit. In chimpanzees,  
progressive old age led to a reduction in both nut-cracking fre-
quency  and  efficiency (Ho w ard-Spink et al., 2025).
While  the ontogeny of stone tool use for nut cracking is well 
studied in robust capuchins  and  chimpanzees,  little is known  
about  gracile capuchins, which  have only recently been  added  to 
the list of nonhuman  primate species that habitually use stone 
tools. In particular, the white-faced capuchins, Cebus capucinus
imitator, living in Coiba National  Park, Panam  � a, are an  important
model  system  for studying tool use acquisition and  proficiency 
development, as their social learning opportunities appear  to 
differ from  those of other tool-using primates. White-faced ca-
puchins  on  the islands of Coiba and  Jicar � on  habitually use stone
tools to crack open  a variety of food items, including fruits such  as 
sea almonds,  Terminalia catappa, coconuts, Cocos nucifera, and  
palm  fruits Bactris major  and  Astrocaryum  standleyanum , as well as 
invertebrates like Hallow een  crabs, Gecarcinus quadratus, hermit  
crabs, Coenobita compressus, nerite snails, Nerita spp., and  other 
freshw ater molluscs (Barrett et al., 2018; Carlson et al., 2025;
Montez a-Moreno, Dogand  � zi � c, et al., 2020). How ever, on  Jicar �on, 
despite this tradition persisting for at least 17 years, it seems  
entirely localized to a 1.5  km  stretch of coast occupied  by  two to 
three neighbouring  capuchin  groups  (Goldsborou gh  et al., 2023). 
Only  one  group  of capuchins  appears  to use tools habitually on  the 
full variety of resources, while their neighbours  have only been  
observed  to use tools in intertidal zones  or stream  beds. In the 
most  frequent tool-using group, stone tool use is fully male  biased: 
only males  have been  observed  to use stone tools, despite females 
regularly using tools on  the neighbouring  island of Coiba 
(Goldsbor ough  et al., 2024). The  absence  of tool use by  females
means  that juvenile capuchins  on  Jicar� on  cannot  learn tool use
from  their mothers, as is common  in chimpanzees.  Furthermor e, 
this group  of tool-using capuchins  appears  to have reduced  group  
cohesion (Goldsbor ough  et al., 2025), further limiting social 
learning opportunities. Taken  together, the tool-using capuchins
on  Jicar � on  present a unique  situation wher e tool use is highly
localized, yet persistent, and  naïve individuals have only limited 
opportunities to observe tool use because  of both reduced  social 
cohesion and  the male-exclusive nature of the behaviour, which  
makes  mothers  unavailable as a model.  Given  these constraints, 
understanding  how  tool use is acquired and  develops in this group  
provides insight into the flexibility of social and  individual 
learning mechanisms  in primates.
W e investigated  the acquisition and  development  of stone tool
use proficiency in one  group  of white-faced capuchins  on  Jicar �on  
Island. Using 12 months  of camer a trap data from  two experi-
mental  anvils, we  compared  features of tool use proficiency (effi-
ciency and  mistakes) between  juvenile, subadult and  adult 
capuchins. Furthermor e, we  conducted  a longitudinal investiga-
tion of improvements  in tool use skills in juveniles over a 1-year 
period, as well as an  initial exploration of tool use skill in old 
age. Finally, we  considered potential mechanisms  driving acqui-
sition of stone tool use on  Jicar � on  through  examining  social
attention to tool use sequences.  W e explored  who  paid attention to 
tool use events, which  tool users were most  likely to receive social 
attention and  how  social attention related  to scrounging and  tool 
user proficiency. Our  aim  w as to characterize the development  of

METHODS
Site and  Subjects
Jicar � on  Island (2002 ha) is part of Coiba Nat ional Park, an  ar-
chipelago consisting of nine islands and  100 islets off the Pacific
coast of Veraguas  Province, Panama.  Jicar � on  Island lies approxi-
mately 60 km  from  the mainland  and  is estimated to have been  
separated  from  the mainland  for 14  000—18 000 years (Titcomb  &
O’Dea, 2020). Archaeological evidence suggests that Jicar� on  Island
w as used  by  indigenous  communities  from  at least 250  CE until 
shortly before European contact (Isaza & Vrba, 2010) and  today it 
is uninhabited, with  only occasional visits by  scientists and  local 
people. White-faced capuchins  occur on  the three largest islands
in Coiba Nat ional Park (Barrett et al., 2018). Jicar � on  has depau -
perate mammalian  communities  and  a total absence  of mamma -
lian predators (Ib � a ~ nez  et al., 1997), and  white-faced  capuchins
show  increased terrestriality (Monteza-Mor eno, Crofoot, et al., 
2020) and  live at high densities (Barrett et al., 2018; Milton & 
Mittermeier, 1977) compared  with  mainland  populations. The
most  active tool-using group  on  Jicar� on  has been  monito red using
unbaited camera  traps since 2017 and  has been  using tools since at 
least 2004  (Barrett et al., 2018). Stone tool use for extractive 
foraging has been  documented  at three types of sites in this group, 
distinguishable by  differences in debris accumulation  
(Goldsborough  et al., 2023). For  the current study, we  focused on  
tool use at high-accumulation  sites, from  hereon  referred  to as 
‘anvils’, where  capuchins  habitually use tools and  large amounts  of 
debris accumulate  over time.
In 2022, the capuchin  group  w as estimated to comprise  around 
20—25  individuals (five to six adult males, five to six adult females,
four to five subadults and  seven  to 10 juveniles). These  estimates 
are based  on  identifiable individuals (Table 1) as well as the 
maximum  number  of capuchins  of a specific age—sex class 
observed  together in a single sequence.  Ages were estimated by  
visual assessment  based  on  body  size, shape  and  facial appearance  
in comparison  with  demographically know n mainland  pop-
ulations. W e were unable  to obtain precise estimates of group  size 
because  of the nature of data collection via camer a trapping, 
wher e the visual field is always limited and  juveniles are difficult 
to identify reliably from  images alone.
Experimental Anvil Set-up
W e set up  two  experimental  anvil sites in the range of the tool-
using group  in January  2022: a wooden  anvil at a site that has been  
heavily used  since 2017 (location name:  CEBUS-02) and  a stone 
anvil at an  entirely novel location under  sea almond  trees wher e 
there w as no  available anvil or cumulative debris, so on  evidence 
of habitual tool use (location name:  EXP-ANV-0 1). To set up  each  
experimental  anvil, we  first cleared the area of debris and  placed a 
1.8 m  2 piece of nylon mesh  on  the ground, on  which  we  placed the 
anvil. Anvils were selected from  nearby  based  on  the presence of a 
flat processing surface, as well as similarity in size to the pre-
existing anvil at CEBUS-02 (62 × 106 cm), which  w ashed  aw ay 
prior to these experiments.  Additionally, at each  anvil, we  supplied 
four uniquely marked  stones sourced  from  within 20 m  of their 
respective sites (Fig. 1a). Each site w as provided with  stones rep-
resenting two weigh t classes: two  stones weighing  <  50 0 g and  
two stones weighing  >  1 kg (Table S1). All provided stones fell 
within the weight  and  size range of hammerstones  that have been  
observed  in this population (Carlson et al., 2025). Materials 
included basalt and  sandstone, both of which  capuchins  in this 
population are known  to use as hammerstones  unprompt ed.

were included in the experimental  set-up. This  set-up w as also

Coding
All videos containing tool use events were coded  frame  by  
frame  in BORIS version 8.21.8 (Friard & Gamba,  2016) by  two 
coders (Z. G.  and  L. R.) using a split-half design. One  individual 
coded  days 1—15  and  the other coded  from  day  16 to the end  of 
each  month,  alternating which  individual coded  the first and  
second  halves across deployments.  W e considered individual tool 
use sequences, wher e the start w as defined as the moment  an  
individual placed an  item  on  the anvil for processing and  grabbed  
the hammerstone.  A sequence  ended  when:  (1) the item  w as 
opened  and  consumed,  (2) the individual relocated with  the item  
to a location out of view  of the camera, or (3) the item  w as 
abandoned,  including when  it had  been  opened  but not eaten. For 
each  tool use sequence, we  identified the age class (juvenile, 
subadult or adult) of the tool user and, when  possible, their 
identity. W e classified age based  on  visual appearance, with  ju-
veniles being individuals estimated to be <  6 years old, subadults 
6—8 years old and  adults >  8 years old. W e subsequently devel-
oped  an  ethogram  (Table S2; Videos  S1-S4) and  used  it to code  all 
behaviours performed  by  the tool user, including measur es of tool 
use efficiency (e.g. number  of successful pounds  and  number  of
mis-strikes) and  technique  (e.g. type of pound:  crouching, stand-
ing and  jumping). W e also coded  asocial (e.g. type of item  pro-
cessed and  hammerstone  material) and  social factors (e.g. 
displacements, social attention and  scrounging) for each  tool use 
sequence  (Table S3). Social attention w as defined as sustained (>  3 
s) attention directed  tow ards the tool user from  within 2 m, 
following  the established concept  of peering (Schuppli & van  
Schaik, 2019). A single video could contain one  or more  tool use 
sequences, and  sequences  could also span  several videos if the 
individual had  not finished processing when  the video ended  and  
continued  in the next triggered  video. L. R. w as trained by  Z. G.  on  a 
sample  data set before coding independently. Interrater reliability 
w as established by  double  coding a randoml y selected  sample  of 
121 videos containing 203 sequences.  Agreement  w as calculated 
for all categorical, numerical  and  timing variables (Table S5 ).
To obtain additional information on  social attention, sequences  
in which  other capuchins  were present in addition to the tool user 
were coded  again by  one  coder (Z. G.)  using a different protocol 
(Table S4). For  each  sequence, we  coded  the age—sex class of all 
visible capuchins, as well as the age—sex class of capuchins  paying 
social attention to, scrounging  from  or displacing the tool user. W e 
identified individual capuchins  whenever  possible. Juveniles could 
usually not be sexed  because  doing so required close-up images of 
their genitals, but the camera  trap image  quality and  camer a angle 
were insufficient. W e also did not observe any subadult females 
during tool use sequences, so we  coded  only subadult males.
Statistical Analyses
All statistical analyses were performed  in R version 4.3.1 
(R Core Team,  2022). All Bayesian regression models  were fitted 
using the ‘brm’ function in brms  version 2.16.1 (Bürkner, 2017). W e 
calculated  contrasts using the ‘hypothesis’ function in brms  to 
evaluate the credibility of differences between  categories (e.g. 
comparing  juveniles' time  to open  an  item  with  that of subadults).

proportion of posterior samples  (PP)  that exceeded  0. W e consid-
ered effects with  PP  >  80% as moder ately reliable and  those with  
PP  >  95%  to be strongly reliable. Conversely, effects with  PP  <  20% 
or PP  <  5%  indicated similarly reliable effects in the opposite

Proficiency
To compare  tool use proficiency between  age classes, we  
restricted  our analyses to sequences  in which  the item  w as suc-
cessfully opened.  Failures were rare (<  4%  of observed  tool use 
events), and  because  data were collected using camera  traps, the 
observed  failures are unlikely to represent an  unbiased  sample  of 
failed attempts. W e therefore report descriptive statistics for these 
rare events but do  not include them  in the inferential analyses. W e 
further limited our analyses of proficiency to sequences  in which  
the tool user could be clearly identified. W e also excluded se-
quences  that were split across multiple videos (<  7% of all se-
quences), as several seconds  of behaviour could have been  missed  
between  triggers. Additionally, we  included only sequences  in 
which  sea almonds  were the item  being processed because  they 
constituted the vast ma jority (98%) of all tool use sequences  and  
other items were under-represented. In our data set, each  row  
represented a tool use sequence, and  variables were aggregated to 
the sequence  level.
W e quantified tool use proficiency in each  sequence  in four 
w ays: (1) time  (s) taken to open  an  item, (2) number  of pounds  
required to open  an  item, (3) number  of times the item  w as 
repositioned on  the anvil between  strikes and  the number  of times 
the sea almond  w as peeled and  (4) number  of mis-strikes and  
strikes that propelled the item  off the anvil. To compare  sequence  
duration between  age classes, we  fitted a generalized linear 
gamma  regression model  (model  e1), with  sequence  duration (s) 
as the outcome.  As predictors, we  included the age class of the tool 
user (juvenile, subadult or adult, with  juvenile as the reference  
level), the colour of the sea almond  (brown,  green, red or un-
known,  corresponding  to its ripeness and  with  brown  as the 
reference  level), the material of the anvil (wood  or stone, with  
stone as the reference level) and  the identity of the tool user as a 
random  effect. W e included the colour of the sea almond  and  the 
material of the anvil because  we  expected  both  to affect the 
number  of pounds  required to open  a sea almond.  Fresh sea 
almond  fruits are green, then turn yellow and  finally red as they 
ripen (Perez  & Condit, n.d.). After drying, cached  fruits turn brown,  
and  we  expected  these dried fruits to be easier to crack than fresh, 
green ones. Stone and  wood  have different densities and  hard-
nesses, which  are likely to affect how  easily sea almonds  crack 
when  struck with  a stone hammer .
To compare  the number  of pounds  required to open  an  item  
between  age classes, we  fitted a generalized linear model  (GLM:  
model  e2) with  the same  structure as the model  described above. 
As the outcome  w as the discrete number  of pounds, we  used  a 
Poisson  distribution. W e also compared  the rate of pounding  (i.e. 
number  of pounds/s) between  age classes by  fitting a model  with  
the same  structure as model  e2 but with  the log of sequence  
duration (s) included as an  offset (model  e2b). W e used  the same  
model  structure, without  an  offset, to model  the number  of times 
an  item  w as repositioned (model  e3a) and  the number  of times the 
sea almond  w as peeled (model  e3b) during a sequence.  Mis-
strikes, in which  a capuchin  attempted  to strike the item  but 
missed  it entirely, were uncommon.  Thus, to compare  their 
occurrence between  age classes, we  fitted a zero-inflated  Poisson  
model  with  the same  predictors as described above (model  e4a). 
Finally, to compare  the occurrence of items being propelled  off the 
anvil between  age classes, which  w as similarly rare, we  fitted  
another zero-inflated Poisson  model  (model  e4b).
Development
To examine  the development  of tool use proficiency over time, 
we  investigated  how  the processing of brown  sea almonds  (the 
most  frequently processed) by  known  individuals changed  over 
the 12-month  observation period. W e visually assessed how  the 
number  of pounds, number  of repositions and  number  of mis-
strikes changed, with  the expectation that (sub)adults wou ld 
rema in relatively stable, wher eas juveniles wou ld show  fewer 
pounds, repositions and  mis-strikes over time  as their proficiency 
increased. W e modelled  the number  of pounds  using a Poisson  
GLM  (model  dev1) with  predictors including the interaction be-
tween  standardized days since the start of the study period and  
tool user identity. W e also considered a case study of one  adult 
male, estimated to be the oldest in the capuchin  group, to assess 
whether  and  how  his tool use changed  since sampling  began  at 
this site in 2017.
Social Attention
To examine  the role of social learning in tool use acquisition, we  
ran further analyses on  a subset of sequences  in which  capuchins  
other than the tool user were present. W e excluded sequences  split 
across several videos, as well as sequences  in which  we  were un-
able to assign one  of the capuchins  present to an  age—sex class, or 
wher e the end  of the tool use sequence  w as not captured  on  video 
(so the sequence  probably continued  after recording stopped). W e 
also restructured  our data set so that each  row  represented an  
individual present during a tool use sequence, with  a variable 
indicating whether  they paid social attention (yes/no).
To assess which  age class w as more  likely to receive social 
attention and  which  age—sex class w as more  likely to pay  social 
attention to tool use, we  fitted a Bernoulli generalized linear mixed  
model  (GLMM)  with  a logit link function (model  socatt1). The  
outcome  of this model  w as social attention (yes/no), while the 
predictors included the age class of the tool user (juvenile, sub-
adult and  adult, with  juvenile as the reference  level) and  the 
age—sex class of the observer (juvenile, subadult male, adult fe-
male  and  adult male, with  juvenile as the reference  level). W e also 
included location (CEBUS-02 or EXP-ANV-0 1) and  two  social fac-
tors that ma y affect the probability of social attention occurring, 
namely  the total number  of capuchins  present during the 
sequence  and  the number  of capuchins  scrounging during the 
sequence.  W e included an  offset of the log of sequence  duration to 
account  for longer sequences  having more  opportunities for social 
attention, and  a random  effect of sequence  ID to account  for 
nonindependence  of observations from  the same  sequence.
To assess whether  more  efficient tool users or specific in-
dividuals received more  social attention, we  analysed data from  
sequences  in which  the sea almond  w as opened  and  the tool user 
could be identified. W e ran another Bernoulli GLMM  (model  
socatt1b) with  social attention (yes/no) as the outcome,  and  tool 
user age class, age —sex class of observer, the number  of pounds  
used  to open  the item  and  the number  of mis-strikes during the 
sequence  as predictors. W e also included an  offset of sequence  
duration and  the identity of the tool user as a random  effect.
All models  were fitted with  regularizing normal(0,  1) priors for 
intercepts and  predictors and  exponential(1) priors for standard 
deviations of varying effects. For each  model, we  performed  a prior 
predictive simulation to compare  our chosen  priors with  default 
priors and  evaluate paramet er identifiability. The  final models  
were run  with  three chains of 3000 iterations each, with  a 150 0-
iterations w arm-up  per chain. Our  models  were stable, with  
large effective sample  sizes (bulk ESS and  tail ESS >  1000 for all 
estimates) and  Rhat values <  1.01 (Vehtari et al., 2021).

DISCUSSION
Stone tool use by  white-faced capuchins  on  Jicar � on  is a comple x
behaviour for which  proficiency takes years to develop. Compared  
to subadult and  adult tool users, juvenile tool users needed  more  
time  and  more  pounds  to open  an  item  using tools. Juveniles mor e 
frequently repositioned the item  in between  strikes and  made  
more  mistakes. Subadults and  adults show ed  compara ble levels of 
proficiency on  all measur es. Four identifiable tool-using juveniles 
did not markedl y improve in tool use proficiency over the course of 
approximately 1 year. No  other capuchins  were present in the vast 
ma jority of tool use sequences, and  social attention to the tool user 
w as rare (occurring in just 6% of all sequences). Nonetheles s, we  
uncovered some  robust patterns in who  paid social attention to 
whom  and  in what  context. Juveniles were most  likely to pay social 
attention to tool use, and  most  of these juveniles were too young  to 
be tool users themselves. Social attention w as directed  at sub-
adults and  adults, with  a slight preference  tow ards more  efficient 
tool users. Subadults received  the most  social attention. The  mor e 
capuchins  were present during a tool use sequence, the lower the 
probability that social attention occurred. Scrounging  show ed  the 
opposite relationship: the more  individuals scrounging, the higher 
the probability of social attention.
Differences in Tool Use Proficiency
It is unsurprising that tool-using juveniles were less proficient 
than adults and  subadults, regardless of the measur e of proficiency 
considered. This  pattern is similar to that previously been  
described in macaq ue  stone tool use (Tan, 2017) and  for this spe-
cies of capuchin  in other extractive foraging tasks (Barrett et al., 
2017). How ever, juveniles were still very successful in opening  
items, and  the difference  in their efficiency compared  with  sub-
adults and  adults w as small. This  highlights an  important  
distinction between  basic success (being  able to open  a nut most  of 
the time) and  the maximum  level of proficiency observed  in 
adults, which  probably reflected  a combination  of physical matu -
ration and  skill refinement. Over all, white-faced capuchins  had  a 
slightly higher success rate than that reported  in studies of other 
nut-cracking primates. Item abandonment  occurred in fewer than 
2% of all sequences, which  is much  lower than in chimpanzees,  
wher e failure occurs in 8% of tool use events (Berdugo et al., 2024), 
and  robust capuchins  where  failure rates range from  2 to 38%
depending  on  population and  resource being processed (Fal �otico 
et al., 2024; Fal � otico & Ottoni, 2016; Spagnoletti et al., 2011).
This  difference does  not necessarily indicate greater skill in white-
faced capuchins, but may  also reflect their high persistence  and  
mo tivation, reduced  need  to abandon  a fruit due  to perceived  
predation risk (Monteza-Mor eno, Crofoot, et al., 2020) as well as 
methodological  differences. In particular, the use of camer a traps 
means  that some  failures ma y have occurred outside the field of 
view  when  individuals relocated with  items. No te that we  
excluded tool use sequences  that spanned  more  than one  camer a 
trap video from  the analyses. The  age distribution in these 
excluded sequences  w as comparable  to that of the sequences  
retained for analysis. The  exclusion of split sequences  is unlikely to

The  clearest distinction betwee n juvenile and  (sub)adult tool 
use proficiency w as the much  higher incidence of mistakes among  
juveniles. Mistakes were rare in our sample, similar to rate reported  
for bearded  capuchins  (Fragaszy et al., 2020), although  nut type and  
traits such  as roundness  and  hardness  probably modulated  error 
rates (O'Malley et al., 2025). Contrary to findings in bearded  capu-
chins (Fragaszy et al., 2020), we  found  that juveniles were consid-
erably mor e likely to miss  the item  or propel it off the anvil. 
Juveniles also repositioned the food item  more  frequently between  
strikes. More  frequent repositioning  by  juveniles could reflect 
poorer control over hammerstones  than that show n by  adults. This  
ties in with  our surprising finding, that at both sites, a single 
hammerstone  w as used  in over 90%  of sequences, despite multiple 
other hammerstones  being provided. These  preferred  stones were 
large and  heavy and  therefore easier for (sub)adults than juveniles 
to handle. The  (lack of) hammer  selectivity relative to body  size at 
the experimental  anvils is an  interesting avenue  for future research, 
as it stands in stark contrast to findings in chimpanzees  (Boesch & 
Boesch, 1983), macaq ues (Gumert  & Malaivijitnond, 2013) and  
robust capuchins  (Visalberghi et al., 2009). The  preference  for 
larger, heavier hammerstones  for nut cracking appears  to be 
widespread  in this population (Carlson et al., 2025), as it is in other 
populations of Sapajus (Ferreira et al., 2010). This  may  be related to 
the fact that, for certain tasks, large hammerstones  might  yield 
substantial benefits for gracile capuchins, which  are the smallest 
and  lightest tool-using primates.
The  same  hammerstone  may  have been  used  so consistently at 
each  site because  there w as little variation in the food items being 
processed. In nearly all sequences, capuchins  processed sea
almonds.  How ever, the ripeness of the sea almond  did appear  to 
affect ease of processing: green, fresher sea almonds  required 
more  seconds  and  more  pounds  to open  than brown  sea almonds,  
were peeled more  frequently and  were more  likely to be propelled 
off the anvil when  struck. This  probably reflects the harder exocarp 
of the fruit at this stage, before it dries out and  becomes  more  
brittle. The  observation of tool users abandoning  opened  items  
without  eating them  further suggests that some  aspects of sea 
almond  quality may  only become  apparent after initial processing. 
The  lack of hammerstone  selectivity according to sea almond  
ripeness and  the higher incidence of peeling with  green sea al-
monds  suggests that the additional step of peeling off the exocarp 
between  strikes helped  overcome  the difference  in hardness, a 
technique  that juveniles might  also need  to learn to become  more  
proficient tool users. How ever, juveniles also peeled more  
frequently than (sub)adults, so it is also possible that they lacked 
the know ledge or ability to strike green  sea almonds  precisely 
without  them  being propelled off the anvil, which  green sea al-
monds  were more  likely to be, and  that peeling the fruit made  it 
easier to strike. In either case, becoming  a proficient stone tool 
user might  involve learning not only how  to process food items, 
but also which  items to process and  when.  Sea almonds  have 
variable fruiting seasons and, although  we  know  little about  sea-
sonal fluctuations in food availability on  Jicar � on, our data do  show
that sea almond  processing peaked  bimodally  (Fig. S1). (Sub)adults 
appeared  to focus more  of their tool-using efforts at specific times 
of the year (see Fig. 4e, f, g, h, i), wher eas juveniles used  tools at a 
more  consistent rate throughout  the year. Future studies could 
elucidate the causes of these seasonal fluctuations in tool use and  
explore whether  adults indeed  concentrate their tool-using efforts 
when  sea almonds  are most  abundant  (consistent with  the ‘op-
portunity’ hypothesis) or, alternatively, when  other, more  easily 
consumed  food resources are scarce (consistent with  the ‘neces-
sity’ hypothesis: Spagnoletti et al., 2012).

Development  of Tool Proficiency
The  lack of change in tool use proficiency by  juveniles over the 
course of our study period suggests that honing  these skills to 
adult levels of proficiency is probably a slow  process, or one  
limited by  physical features (such  as strength) that only mature  in 
adulthood.  White-faced  capuchins  are a long-lived  species, with  a 
life span  of up  to 50  years (Fragaszy et al., 2004), similar to bearded  
capuchins, in which  successful nut-cracking attempts are observed  
at around  2 years of age but adult-level proficiency can take until 5  
years of age to develop, depending  on  the resource (De  Resende  
et al., 2008; Fragaszy et al., 2023). Similarly, in chimpanzees,  
becoming  a proficient nut cracker can take 3—7 years of practice 
(Boesch & Boesch, 1990; Matsuzaw a, 1994). Our  results suggest 
that, in white-faced capuchins, becoming  a proficient stone tool 
user may  likewise be an  extended  process. Importantly, our ju-
venile sample  consisted primarily of older juveniles (>  3 years), 
who  ma y have already acquired basic tool-using skills and  were 
refining their technique  rather than still being in the process of 
acquiring it (De  Resende  et al., 2008; Ottoni et al., 2005 ). One  year 
of observation may  therefore have been  insufficient to capture 
gradual improvements  in proficiency at this stage of ontogeny. 
Although  failure w as rare overall, juveniles exhibited a higher rate 
of failure than subadults and  adults. Interestingly, we  observed  
relatively few  prolonged  unsuccessful object manipulations  by  
juveniles at these anvil sites. If tool proficiency develops gradually 
through  trial-and-error learning, one  might  expect extended  bouts 
of inefficient practice during early ontogeny. Instead, man y failures 
consisted of short attempts, with  abandonment  occurring after a 
single pound.  One  possible explanation is that prolonged  unsuc-
cessful attempts do  occur, but at locations other than the 
frequently used  anvil sites monitored  in this study, such  as 
streambeds  or the intertidal zone.
Furthermore, our finding that the oldest tool-using male  in the 
group, ABE, did not use tools during the 12-month  study period 
hints at a potential upper  age limit for stone tool use in white-
faced capuchins, as has been  documented  in chimpanzees  
(Ho w ard-Spink et al., 2025). ABE w as the only individual to 
displace tool users from  the anvil to steal the item  they had  
opened, which  suggests that he  w as still interested in the re-
sources but w as perhaps no  longer able or willing to invest the 
time  and  energy  required to use tools himself. Here  too, a more  
longitudinal approach  wou ld be valuable for examining  changes  in 
tool use proficiency across the life span.
The Role of Social Attention in Tool Use Acquisition
Stone tool use at the experimental  anvils appeared  to be a 
largely solitary activity (Goldsborough  et al., 2024). As such, op-
portunities for social attention appear  to be limited, and  even  
when  other capuchins  were present, social attention occurred in 
only 20% of the sequences.  Data from  camer a traps may  under-
estimate social attention, as individuals out of frame  could have 
been  looking at the tool user without  this being captured. How -
ever, attention from  a distance is likely to provide the observer 
with  only coarse information about  the tool use behaviour. The  
low  frequency  of close social attention in our sample  is surprising 
compared  with  that reported  for other nut-cracking primates. In 
one  study of tufted capuchins, Sapajus apella, conspecifics 
observed  36% of all nut-cracking events (Coelho et al., 2015), and  in 
chimpanzees,  juveniles observe hundr eds of nut-cracking events
performed  by  their mothers  (Estienne et al., 2019). Yet on  Jicar�on, 
juvenile capuchins  have no  opportunity to learn tool use from  
their mothers  because  females do  not use tools (Goldsbor ough  
et al., 2024). A similar male  bias has been  document ed  for probe
tool use in S. libidinosus (Fal� otico & Ottoni, 2014; Valença  et al., 
2024), wher e sex differences emerge  during development  and  
ma y reflect differential motivation to engage  with  tool-related
behaviours (Fal � otico et al., 2021). If comparable  processes oper-
ate here, social attention to tool users may  itself be sex biased, a 
prediction that we  could not test because  juvenile sex could not be 
reliably determined.
Individuals paying  social attention to tool use were mostly  ju-
veniles too young  to be tool users themselves, suggesting that 
social observation ma y be most  important  for the acquisition of 
tool use behaviour (Barrett et al., 2017). Although  scrounging w as 
not as common  as in tufted capuchins  (Coelho et al., 2015), the 
mor e individuals that we re scrounging, the higher the likelihood 
that social attention would  occur. Furthermor e, when  scrounging 
occurred alongside social attention, it w as nearly always tolerated 
by  the tool user. The  tool users who  received  the most  social 
attention, subadult males, are also known  to be tolerant of juve-
niles (Jack & Riley, 2014). Our  results support the idea that inter-
individual tolerance  plays a crucial role in the social learning of 
comple x behaviours (Coelho et al., 2024; van  Schaik et al., 1999). 
How ever, we  were limited in our ability to identify juveniles pre-
sent at the anvil site who  were not the tool user, so we  could not 
determine  whether  the individuals paying social attention were 
also the ones  scrounging.  The  development  of computer -assisted 
individual tracking methods  will facilitate future studies of these 
fine-grained interactions during tool use events. Furthermor e, 
capuchins  might  also rely on  local enhancement  and  interactions 
with  tempor ally enduring  artefacts of tool use to learn about  tool 
use behaviour, a form  of niche construction (Fragaszy et al., 2013; 
Odling-Smee  et al., 1996). In line with  niche construction theory 
(Fragaszy et al., 2013), the physical modifications generated  
through  tool use, such  as partially processed food items left on  
anvils and  wea r on  hammerstones  and  anvils, may  structure 
subsequent learning opportunities by  providing persistent cues to 
individuals visiting the site, particularly when  direct social
learning opportunities are limited. Capuchins  on  Jicar � on  using this
physical evidence of tool use as a cue could also explain the ‘burn-
in period’ at the novel anvil, EXP-ANV-0 1, as regular tool use by  
man y individuals ma y only have started once  enough  debris had  
accumulated  from  sporadic tool use events, mar king the location 
as a suitable anvil for tool use.
The Importance of Location
Here,  we  reported  data collected from  only two  anvils within 
the tool-using groups' range. How ever, we  have identified dozens  
of different anvils that are habitually used  for stone tool use and  
mor e than 300 ephemer al locations. While  CEBUS-02 is the most  
frequently used  anvil and  has, to the best of our know ledge, been  
in use for the longest time  (at least since 2004), it is possible that 
most  social learning of tool use occurs elsewhere, for instance 
during tool use in the intertidal zone  (Goldsborou gh  et al., 2023). 
W e did observe a difference  in the likelihood of social attention 
between  the two  experimental  anvils: CEBUS-02 provided more  
opportunities for social attention, but social attention w as more  
likely at EXP-ANV-0 1. This  could reflect the fact that to CEBUS-02 
w as already  an  established, well-used  anvil prior to the current 
study. W e infer that this anvil is used  so frequently because  it is 
located in a productive foraging area (next to a stream), which  
results in mor e capuchins  being present at the same  time. In 
contrast, we  deliberately placed the experimental  anvil EXP-ANV -
01 in a location without  evidence of prior tool use, meaning  that 
the location probably held less interest for the capuchins. This  
inference is supported  by  the fact that it took  capuchins  several 
months  to start using this anvil regularly. The  localized availability

greater competition  over this site and  lower tolerance  of ob-

anvil, further supporting the idea that the wooden  anvil at CEBUS-

tool users and  the notion of a ‘wear in’ period for new  anvils are 
another interesting avenue  for future research to examine  which  
physical properties drive these differences in tool use efficiency.
Spread of Tool Use to Other Groups
The  seemingly  slow  mastery of stone tool use by  white-faced 
capuchins  also has implications for the spread of this behaviour.
Despite persisting over time, the stone tool use tradition on  Jicar �on  
is localized (Barrett et al., 2018). In mainland  populations of white-
faced capuchins, males  are the dispersing sex and  first dispersal 
occurs, on  average, at 4.5  years of age in Santa Rosa (Jack et al., 
2012) and  7 years of age in Lomas  Barbudal (Perry et al., 2012).
On  Jicar � on, males  of this age might  not yet be proficient tool users
based  on  our data and, as such, might  cease using tools after 
dispersal, limiting the spread of the behaviour. Moreo ver, females
on  Jicar � on  do  not use stone tools despite being physically capable
of doing so (Goldsborou gh  et al., 2024). While  females are typically 
philopatric, switches in dispersal tendency  are known  to occur 
under  high-density conditions (Clutton-Brock & Lukas, 2012), such
as those on  Jicar� on, meaning  this sex bias could further constrain
the spread of tool use between  groups  if females disperse. Ob -
servations suggest that at least some  males  remain  in their natal 
group, as we  have tracked  some  individuals from  the juvenile stage 
to (sub)adulthood.  Further elucidating the dispersal tendencies of 
these capuchins  will be crucial for better understanding  the 
maintenance  and  spread of tool use behaviour.
Conclusion
W e have presented  the first description of variation in, and  
development  of, stone tool use proficiency in white-faced capu-
chin monkey s. Using camera  traps placed at two  experimental  tool
use anvils on  Jicar� on  Island, Panama,  we  collected tool use se-
quences  over nearly 12 months,  mostly  of capuchins  processing 
sea almonds.  Similar to other primates that use percussive stone 
tools, white-faced capuchin  juveniles are less proficient tool users 
than adults (in terms  of efficiency and  technique), and  the devel-
opment  of proficiency appears  to be a slow  process. Sea almond  
ripeness affected processing time  and  efficiency, with  less ripe 
green sea almonds  requiring more  time, more  peeling between  
pounds  and  resulting in mor e mistakes. In contrast to findings 
from  other tool-using primates, tool use appeared  to be a largely 
solitary activity, with  limited opportunities for social learning and  
even  fewer instances of close social attention. How ever, when  
social attention to tool use did occur, there were clear patterns that 
echoed  those reported  in other primates. Social attention w as 
mostly  paid by  juveniles to proficient subadults, who  were tolerant 
of scrounging. W e also found  that location affected the likelihood 
of social attention occurring, suggesting that social learning op-
portunities differ between  sites. In short, we  lay the groundwor k 
for comparisons  between  stone tool-using primate  species and  for 
future research on  other aspects of white-faced capuchin  stone 
tool use, such  as seasonality, hammerstone  selectivity and  anvil 
selectivity.
