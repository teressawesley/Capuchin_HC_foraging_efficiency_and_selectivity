SUMMARY
Tool use is a fundamental feature of human evolution. Stone tools are in the archaeological record from 3.4
Ma, even before Homo,1 and the use of stone tools probably predated the split between hominins and pan-
ins.2 Using tools (hereafter, tooling cf Fragaszy and Mangalam3) is hypothesized to have improved hominins’
foraging efﬁciency or access to high-quality foods. 4–7 This hypothesis is supported if feeding with tools posi-
tively contributes to diet quality in extant non-human primates or if foraging efﬁciency is increased by tooling.
However, the contribution of tooling to non-human primates’ foraging success has never been investigated
through a direct analysis of nutritional ecology.
8,9 We used multi-dimensional nutritional geometry to analyze
energy and macronutrients (nonstructural carbohydrates, lipids, and protein) in the diets of wild capuchin
monkeys ( Sapajus libidinous ) that routinely crack palm nuts with stone hammers.
10,11 We show that eating
nuts obtained through tooling helps monkeys to achieve more consistent dietary intakes. Tooling increased
the net energy gain by 50% and decreased the proportion of ﬁber ingested by 7%. Tooling also increased the
daily non-protein energy intake. By contrast, protein intake remained constant across foraging days, sug-
gesting a pattern of macronutrient regulation called protein prioritization, which is also found in contempo-
rary humans.
8,9 In addition, tooling reduced dispersion in the ratio of protein to non-protein energy, suggest-
ing a role in macronutrient balancing. Our ﬁndings suggest that tooling prior to tool making could have
substantially increased the nutritional security of ancestral hominins, sowing the seeds for cultural develop-
ment.
5,7
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We investigated two habituated wild groups of bearded capu-
chin monkeys ( Sapajus libidinosus ) living in Fazenda Boa Vista,
Piauı´, Brazil (hereafter, FBV). Capuchins have become an impor-
tant comparative reference point for understanding hominin evo-
lution as they combine a constellation of characteristics that, ac-
cording to Kaplan et al.’s 5 model of ‘‘developmental embodied
capital,’’ explain human life history evolution in terms of a shift
to higher-quality diets. These monkeys have an extended life-
span, a relatively large proportion of the lifespan spent as juve-
niles, proportionally large brains, and high manual dexterity.
12
Wild bearded capuchin monkeys in several locations use stone
tools in foraging, 13,14 and young monkeys are not proﬁcient at
tooling for several years. 15,16
The population of bearded capuchin monkeys at FBV affords
an ideal opportunity to study the effects of tooling on diet quality.
We have a variety of indirect evidence that tooling (speciﬁcally,
using stone hammers and anvils to crack palm nuts) is ecologi-
cally important for this population. First, tooling is routine in
both males and females from about 4 years of age.
11 The mon-
keys tool opportunistically across the year to crack several spe-
cies of palm nuts.
10 Second, monkeys compete for access to
nut-cracking sites, in accord with these being highly valued. 17
Third, young monkeys are strongly attracted to others cracking
nuts, and young monkeys practice tooling persistently before
they succeed at cracking a nut. 15,16 In addition, cracking nuts
with stones is energetically costly. Although adult monkeys
weigh 2.0–4.4 kg,
18 the average hammer weighs more than
1 kg, and adult monkeys can use hammer stones that weigh
72% more than they weigh. 19
We quantiﬁed daily food intake for 28 individuals (12 adults, 16
juveniles; Table S1 ), along with their activity budgets and daily
path lengths (m) during 129 focal days across 19 months
(Table S2 ). We measured daily metabolizable energy intake
and energy expenditure to derive net energy gain, with basal
metabolic rate adjusted per each individual for its body mass,
for days when the individual tooled to crack and consume
nuts, compared with days when she/he did not. On tooling
days, capuchins increased their total metabolizable energy

The time budgets for traveling, foraging, and socializing and
daily path length did not differ signiﬁcantly across days with tool-
ing versus days without tooling, but time budgets for resting and
eating did: monkeys spent less time resting (OR = 0.54, t =
/C03.45, df = 117, p = 0.002) and more time eating (OR = 1.36,
t = 2.76, p = 0.018) on tooling days compared with non-tooling
days. Furthermore, the monkeys expended 22% more energy
(using estimated costs of tooling; STAR Methods ) on days
when they tooled (186 kcal) versus days they did not (153 kcal)
(t = 6.74, df = 117, p < 0.0001). The ratio of energy intake to en-
ergy expenditure was 5.24 on tooling days, whereas 4.33 on
non-tooling days, a non-signiﬁcant increase (t = 1.32, df = 118,
p = 0.39).
To evaluate if the increase in the net gain of daily metabolizable
energy on days when the monkeys cracked nuts was accounted
for by the caloric content of the nut kernel obtained by tooling,
we repeated the GLMM analyses of net energy gain corrected
for body mass with the caloric intake from kernels removed
from the dataset. The energy net gain with kernels excluded
from the calculations did not differ signiﬁcantly between days
with and days without tooling (t = /C01.08, df = 118, p = 0.53).
This ﬁnding conﬁrms that the kernel of palm nuts was the source
of higher energy net gain on days with tooling.
The kernel of the palm nuts that we analyzed ( Orbignya sp.)
contained 420 kcal/100 g (97.8% NPE). Each nut contains ca.
5 g of kernel, and on tooling days, capuchins ate between 5
and 52 g of kernel (1–10 palm nuts). The energy value of palm
kernels derives almost entirely (90%) from lipids, more than
nearly any other diet item, except invertebrates.

Because ﬁber reduces absorption of nutrients, especially for
species lacking adaptations to digest ﬁber such as capuchin
monkeys with simple stomachs, a lower ﬁber diet improves
diet quality.
21 The ratio of neutral detergent ﬁbers (NDFs) to
NPE on tooling days was lower than on non-tooling days (t =
/C04.90, df = 115; p < 0.0001, Figure 3A). Accordingly, in addition
to increasing net energy ingested, we can assume that the capu-
chins absorbed a greater proportion of ingested NPE on tooling
days compared with non-tooling days because on tooling days,
they ingested proportionally less NDF. Thus, tooling improved
the quality of the monkeys’ diet by providing a rich source of
lipid, nonstructural carbohydrate, and total energy, as well as
decreasing the amount of ﬁber ingested. No difference was
found between tooling and non-tooling days in total dry matter
intake ( Figure 3 B) (t = 1.24, df = 117, p = 0.43), indicating that
the effect of tool use was speciﬁcally on diet quality and not
quantity.
The current evidence on tooling across populations of non-hu-
man primates indicates that tooling primarily occurs in the
context of food abundance when opportunities arise.
11,22–24
Our results indicate that even in a population in which tooling
is opportunistic, it improves diet quality by providing a rich
source of fat and carbohydrate energy which stabilizes dietary
macronutrient balance, a dietary metric that has been demon-
strated for many taxa to be strongly associated with ﬁtness.
25
Compared with other populations of Sapajus, the FBV popula-
tion spends a higher proportion of time resting and socializing. 26
Thus, this population experiences enhanced opportunities for
social interaction and for investment in lengthy socially biased
learning of the tooling tradition compared with others. This
pattern is reminiscent of the cascades proposed for early
Homo when tool use became routine.
7
Importantly, tooling in FBV did not increase macronutrient
intake per se, but speciﬁcally the intake of fats and carbohy-
drates, with protein remaining more stable. This suggests a
pattern of macronutrient intake called ‘‘protein prioritization,’’
in which the intake of protein is regulated more strongly than
fat and carbohydrate.
20 Protein prioritization has also been
observed in several other primates, 27–29 including humans, 20
but is not at all universal in this order. Mountain gorillas show
the opposite pattern, in which absolute intake of NPE is main-
tained more constant, whereas protein varies with P:NPE ratio
of available foods.
30 Rhesus macaques show an intermediate
pattern, in which P and NPE intake are regulated with equal
strength and consequently total energy intake remains constant
in the face of ecologically constrained variation in dietary macro-
nutrient ratios.
31 The reasons for these differences remain un-
clear, but in the present context, it is interesting that Sapajus
show the same pattern observed in humans. The likely role of
percussive tools as a means of sourcing fat-rich in-bone tissues,
marrow, and brain 32 suggests interesting parallels in the func-
tional role of tool use in these primates.
Here, we documented how tooling improves diet quality for
wild monkeys, providing a new reference point for understanding
the relationship between tooling and nutritional ecology in hom-
inins. Our ﬁndings show how tool use could have provided signif-
icant energetic and nutritional beneﬁts for early hominins even
when the forms of tooling were technically simple and tooling
was a small proportion of overall foraging activity. Thus, the
use of unmodiﬁed natural objects as tools could have served
as a catalyst for the construction of niches as proposed to
have characterized human cultural evolution.

Nutritional analysis
The food samples were analyzed for the percent of ash, neutral detergent ﬁber (NDF
40), crude protein (using N x 6.25), and lipids
following AOAC (1990) procedures. Total non-structural carbohydrates (TNC) was calculated using the formula: TNC=100 – (% crude
protein + % lipids + % ash + % NDF). We added the calories gained from non-structural carbohydrates (4 kcal/g) and lipids (9 kcal/g)
to estimate non-protein energy.

(4 kcal/g).
Nutrient intake was estimated by multiplying the total units of each food item ingested by their correspondent nutritional concen-
tration. Since it was not possible to record all feeding events for the whole day, we scaled the nutrient intake to 12 hours (correspond-
ing to the active period of the monkeys). To avoid bias related to individual’s body mass, the daily nutrient intake was divided by the
mean metabolic body mass of each individual (kg
0.762) during the study period.41 The body mass of the subjects from group CH was
available from Fragaszy and colleagues,18 and we estimated the body mass of individuals of group ZA by comparison based on their
age and body size.
To analyze the patterns of macronutrient intake we used the nutritional geometry, a state space modeling where each axis repre-
sents a different nutrient.42 We constructed 3-dimensional right angle mixture triangles with the proportional contributions of protein,
fat and non-structural carbohydrates. In nutritional geometry, diet composition is represented as Cartesian points (showing the
amounts of macronutrients in the diets) or as lines (nutritional rails) where the slope indicates a particular macronutrient balance.
Energy expenditure
To calculate daily energy expenditure, we multiplied time spent in different activities by the metabolic cost of each activity following
Key and Ross’s 43 estimates for primates. Metabolic costs of nut-cracking by capuchin monkeys are not available. Instead, consid-
ering that nut-cracking is energetically very expensive due to the lifting of heavy stones in relation to the animals’ body mass, 19 as a
reference, we used an estimate of the metabolic costs of swimming in humans (the measured human activity with the highest meta-
bolic cost 44) to estimate energy expenditure for the entire period spent foraging, including nut-cracking. We also used Coelho’s 44
estimate for all foraging activities for the entire day when tooling was observed, even though the monkeys typically crack nuts for
a few minutes per day, not the whole day. By adopting this approach, we avoided underestimating energy expenditure during nut
cracking.
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
We analyzed 129 observational days from 28 monkeys. For 14 monkeys, days both with and without tooling were available. Thirteen
monkeys were never observed tooling during our sampling. One monkey tooled on every day it was observed. The sampling days per
individual are reported in Table S2. This led us to deﬁne a tooling factor with three levels: days of monkey tooling (n=31), days when
these same monkeys did not tool (n=58), and days of monkeys which never tooled (n=40). While the days of monkeys that never
tooled in our sampling were not the focus of this report, we kept them in the dataset to allow ﬁtting the statistical models. We
only reported here the results of comparisons between levels 1 and 2.
We modeled the effect of tool use on response variables (described below) using Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) with
factors tool use (3 levels as earlier described), age (juvenile, adult), sex (male, female), together with the interactions of the three as
ﬁxed effects. Factors season (dry, rainy) and group (group CH, group ZA) were also included with ﬁxed effects. Because of multiple
observations per individual (range 1-13, median 5) random effects for individuals were introduced in the GLMMs. The results we show
are model based estimated medians for the ﬁrst two levels of the tooling factor (and differences or ratios of these), obtained by back-
transforming the estimated means on the link scale, averaging over the levels of the other ﬁxed factors of the models (Figures 1 and 2).
We used GLMMs with a gamma distribution and log link function for positively valued response variables: total caloric intake, non-
protein intake, protein intake, dry matter intake, energy expenditure, ratios of gained energy over expended energy, ratios of gained
energy excluding energy from nuts over expended energy and daily path length. To correct for observation duration and body mass
we included offsets (log(observation duration/12 hours) and log (body mass), with the exception of daily path length (only an offset for
observation time) and of the ratios of gained over expended energy (no offsets). We excluded one outlier for the analysis of energy
expenditure.
GLMMs with a beta distribution and logit link were used for the continuous proportion-valued variables: proportion of time spent
traveling, foraging and eating, and proportion of protein. Response variables proportion of time resting and socializing contained
