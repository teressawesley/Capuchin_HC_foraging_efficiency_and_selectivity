RESEARCH ARTICLE
Sex Differences in the Stone Tool-Use Behavior of a Wild Population of Burmese
Long-Tailed Macaques ( Macaca fascicularis aurea )
MICHAEL D. GUMERT 1/C3, LOW KUAN HOONG 1, AND SUCHINDA MALAIVIJITNOND2
1Division of Psychology, School of Humanities and Social Sciences, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore, Singapore
2Primate Research Unit, Department of Biology, Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand
We investigated sex differences in how Burmese long-tailed macaques ( Macaca fascicularis aurea) used
stone tools to open shelled food items along the shores of two islands in Laemson National Park, Thailand.
Over a 2-week period in December 2009, we collected scan and focal samples on macaques when they were
visible along the shores and mangroves. We found females used stones more often while feeding and used
smaller tools than males. Females also processed sessile oysters more than males, whereas males processed
unattached foods more than females. It was unclear w hich sex was overall more proﬁcient at stone tool
use, but males did perform signiﬁcantly better at opening unattached food items with large pounding
stones. Females also struck food items more times during tool-use bouts and at a faster rate, but no
signiﬁcant difference was found in average tool-use bout duration. Males processed foods slightly faster
within a tool-use bout, but we were unable to detect a signiﬁcant difference in the rate of food processing
while foraging with tools. In summary females chipped open sessile oysters with an axing technique more
than males, while males used larger stones to pound open unattached shelled food more often than
females. Despite using pounding more than females, males also regularly utilized the axing technique on
sessile oysters. Our results are the ﬁrst assessment of sex differences in macaque stone tool use, providing
a basis for comparison with tool use in other primates, and to nonfunctional forms of stone use in other
macaques. Am. J. Primatol. 73:1239–1249, 2011.

INTRODUCTION
Human ancestors are generally characterized as
having a division of labor between the sexes, where
food is reciprocally shared and bonds form around
the trade of food-related services [Isaac, 1978; Kuhn
& Stiner, 2006; Leakey & Lewin, 1977; Wrangham,
2009]. According to these theories, males most
contributed to the communities’ food supply through
hunting, and females most contributed by gathering
small foods and cooking. The differences in labor
pertaining to food extraction would likely have
impacted the types of tools used by males and
females, but ﬁnding evidence of sex-speciﬁc tool
usage is difﬁcult to attain from archeological data
[Kuhn & Stiner, 2006].
Tool use in living nonhuman primates provides
an opportunity to observe sex differences in how tools
are used, providing a comparative perspective for
understanding human sex differences. However, a
notable difference in the feeding patterns of monkeys
and apes is that feeding activity is primarily indivi-
dualistic and lacks the communal input that humans
exhibit (i.e. except for the communal hunting of
chimpanzees) [Boesch, 1994; Stanford, 1999]. Despite
this major difference, a limited record has shown
signiﬁcant sex differences in the habitual tool use of
chimpanzees and capuchins [Boesch & Boesch, 1984;
Lonsdorf et al., 2004; Moura & Lee, 2010; Spagnoletti
et al., 2011], and these biases could have formed the
foundation on which the historical sex-based division
of labor in human communities evolved.
The sex differences in chimpanzee and capuchin
stone tool use are not uniform. In chimpanzees
(Pan troglodytes ), females in wild populations
generally use stone hammers and termite ﬁshing
wands more frequently and with greater skill than
males [Boesch & Boesch, 1981, 1984; Lonsdorf, 2005;
McGrew, 1979, 1992]. In addition, females acquire
tool-use skills earlier in life and are more attentive to

In contrast, a male bias in tool use has been observed
in wild and captive capuchins, where males exhibit
more frequent and proﬁcient tool use than females
(Cebus libidinosus and C. nigritus ) [Falo´tico &
Ottoni, 2010; Fragaszy et al., 2004, 2010; Jalles-
Filho, 1995; Jalles-Filho et al., 2001; Moura & Lee,
2010; Ottoni & Izar, 2008; Ottoni & Mannu, 2001;
Spagnoletti et al., 2011; Visalberghi et al., 2010].
This variation across species indicates that well-
conserved sex differences in tool use do not exist
across tool-using primates, suggesting the speciﬁc
conditions affecting a species might have a large
inﬂuence.
Sex differences in chimpanzee tool use are
suggested to occur for a few reasons. McGrew
[1992] hypothesized that female chimpanzees rely
more on tool use than males because of energetic
needs, suggesting the nutritional costs and beneﬁts
that each sex must face may establish sex differences
in tool use. This functional explanation [Tinbergen,
1963] is based on the socioecological hypothesis that
access to food is the main factor limiting female
behavioral strategies [van Schaik, 1989; Wrangham,
1980]. A proximate and developmental explanation
[Tinbergen, 1963] suggests male chimpanzees are
more attracted to social activity than feeding and
therefore are more easily distracted from engaging in
tool use [Boesch & Boesch, 1984; Lonsdorf, 2005;
Lonsdorf et al., 2004]. Developmentally, this reduces
a male’s opportunity to acquire tool skills because
they more often select social activity over tool use
during their maturation [Lonsdorf et al., 2004].
Proximately, social interests disrupt tool bouts in
males more frequently than females because males
more often cease tool-use activities to follow their
male associates [Boesch & Boesch, 1984]. These
explanations are not mutually exclusive and account
for the sex differences at different levels.
Different factors affect the sex differences found
in bearded capuchin stone tool use. The main factor
driving differences in the stone tool-use patterns of
bearded capuchin is regarded to be sexual dimorph-
ism in body size. Female capuchins have only 60% of
the body mass of males ( # 5 3.5 kg, ~ 5 2.1 kg), and
thus larger males can more easily wield heavy stone
tools effectively [Fragaszy et al., 2010; Spagnoletti
et al., 2011; Visalberghi et al., 2010]. Chimpanzees
are much larger than capuchins ( /C2430–40 kg) and
have a lower degree of sexual dimorphism (80–85%
the mass of a male) [Pusey et al., 2005; Smith &
Jungers, 1997]. Consequently, capuchins are using
stone tools that are relatively much larger compared
with their body size, than chimpanzees, requiring
greater effort and utilization of strength for a
capuchin to successfully use stones. Males being
signiﬁcantly larger than females are less constrained
by this physical limitation, evidenced in that males
use heavier stones when processing nuts that require
greater force to open [Spagnoletti et al., 2011].
In contrast, there is no apparent sex difference in
the weight of tools used for softer nuts, owing to
relaxed strength demands because less force is
required [Spagnoletti et al., 2011]. Strength limita-
tions between male and female capuchins play a large
role in how each sex uses stones. However, strength
may not account for all aspects of the male sex bias
because some preliminary studies indicate male
biases also exist in probing and digging, two tasks
which do not necessarily require greater strength and
large tools [Falo´tico & Ottoni, 2010; Moura & Lee,
2010].
Macaques have not been considered as tool users,
but a recently discovered population of the Burmese
subspecies of long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis
aurea) is changing this view [Malaivijitnond et al.,
2007]. These macaques use stone tools to crack open
nuts and a wide variety of shelled marine organisms
in intertidal zones on islands and coastal regions of
peninsular Thailand and Myanmar [Carpenter,
1887; Gumert et al., 2009; Gumert et al., in review;
Malaivijitnond et al., 2007]. These macaques custo-
marily [McGrew & Marchant, 1997] use stone tools,
and on one island where studies have recently been
conducted, tool use occurs daily along the shores, and
over 80% of adult and adoloscent macaques use stone
tools to process food [Gumert et al., in review].
Although tool use in macaques has not been
studied very intensively yet, a nonfunctional form of
stone handling has been studied in Japanese maca-
ques (Macaca fuscata), since it was ﬁrst discovered in
1979 [Huffman, 1984]. The behavior is primarily a
play behavior, but several patterns of stone use have
been observed that are similar to functional tool use
(e.g. stone pounding, clapping, and ﬂinting) and can
occur within a feeding context [Huffman et al., 2010;
Leca et al., 2007a; Nahallage & Huffman, 2007a,b].
Owing to the similarity to tool use, the capacity to
perform nonfunctional stone handling has been
suggested to be an important basis for the evolution
of stone tool use in macaques, although no studied
models of macaque tool use were available at the
time these assertions were presented [Huffman &
Quiatt, 1986]. Research on individual variation in
stone handling across Japan has not shown any
stable sex differences across ten groups in stone use
patterns [Leca et al., 2007a]. However, in a different
study on a single group, adult females handled stones
less frequently and performed fewer types of pat-
terns [Nahallage & Huffman, 2007a]. Developmental
changes do occur in stone use [Leca et al., 2007b],
and learning the behavioral acts appears to be
socially mediated [Huffman, 1984, 1996; Huffman
et al., 2008; Nahallage & Huffman, 2007b].
No reports of sex differences in macaque tool use
have been published. The only reported study
showed variation in the tool-use styles and grips

toughness of shells [Gumert, unpublished data].
This population of macaques provides an
opportunity to expand comparative studies on sex
differences in the tool-use abilities of wild primates.
Tool-use skills in capuchins and chimpanzees are
considered analogous behavioral traits that formed
through convergent evolutionary processes [van
Schaik et al., 1999]. Macaques would provide a third
taxonomic comparison and we can predict that as
catarrhine primates, macaques and chimpanzees
should possess a set of more homologous traits
that are important in determining their tool-using
behavior. These traits might include greater simila-
rities in their nervous system, hand anatomy, and
behavioral dispositions. We therefore can predict that
some aspects of sex differences affecting tool-use are
phylogenetically linked and thus may be more similar
between macaques and chimpanzees than capuchins.
Phylogenetic distance will not be the only factor
though, as we expect behavioral traditions to be highly
affected by local conditions, including physical and
ecological constraints [van Schaik et al., 1999]. We
expect body size will play an important role in
determining sex differences. Similar to capuchins,
female Burmese long-tailed macaques are around 60%
of the mass of a male ( # 5 7.33 kg, ~ 5 4.54 kg)
[Hamada, unpublished data], and thus we predict
males to use heavier stone tools more frequently and
effectively. Moreover, other factors, such as differences
between the sexes in foraging strategies, character-
istics of the tools available, types of food processed, and
predation risks, may also be important determinants
for sex-differences in how tools are used.
In this article, we present results from a short
2-week study on sex differences in tool use among
long-tailed macaques living on Piak Nam Yai and
Thao Islands in Laemson National Park, Thailand.
We observed tool-use behavior on the shores of the
islands and used behavioral sampling methods to
collect data on their tool-use activities. We used our
data to test for differences between males and
females in the frequency and characteristics of their
tool-use, and also explored age differences to identify
developmental changes in sex differences. We tested
for differences by examining the frequency in which
tools were used during feeding, the type of foods
used, and the size of stones selected. We further
compared differences in proﬁciency by assessing tool
bout characteristics (i.e. number of strikes, bout
duration, and strike rate) and efﬁciency in tool use
(i.e. rate of cracking and success). We present our
ﬁndings in the context of the sex differences
observed in other primate tool users.
METHODS
Study Site and Subjects
Research was carried out on the long-tailed
macaques living in Laemson National Park in
Ranong Province, Thailand. We conducted our study
by observing macaques on Piak Nam Yai (N 9 135
0,
E981280) and Thao Islands (N9 1340, E98 1290). Piak
Nam Yai is a small 1.7-km 2 island approximately
750 m off of the coast of west Thailand. Thao Island
is a 1.5-km 2 island that is approximately 1,300 m
from the coast, and is separated from Piak Nam Yai
by only a small channel (50–100 m). Several habitats
exist on the islands, including rocky shores, man-
groves, sandy beaches, mudﬂats, freshwater stream-
beds, and mountainous tropical forest. Piak Nam Yai
is more mountainous and rocky, whereas Thao
Island is primarily mangrove with some rock out-
croppings and a sandy beach. Piak Nam Yai contains
nine groups of long-tailed macaques, which vary in
size between 12 and 40 individuals [Gumert et al., in
review], whereas Thao Island contains at least one
group of 20–30 animals.
Observations occurred between December 8 and
22, 2009. We used a boat to circle the island
repeatedly between the hours of 7 a.m.–10 a.m. and
4 p.m.–6 p.m., and stopped to observe macaques
whenever they were visible on the shore and using
stone tools. Observations were conducted between
10 and 60 m from coastline. We kept sufﬁcient
distance in order to not disturb their behavior
because these macaques were not habituated to close
human presence and ran away if the boat approached
too closely. The macaques were most easily obser-
vable when engaging in tool use on the shores and
mangroves. As a result, our data are not representa-
tive of the overall living activities of these macaques.
Despite this, our data should be a useful test of tool
activity. This is because most tool use occurs on the
shores based on evidence that tool-use debris was
much more commonly observed on the coastlines,
than in surveys into the mainland. Furthermore,
all tool use on marine organisms must occur on or
near the shores. Our data therefore provide a
sufﬁcient preliminary test of sex differences in
macaque tool use.

Behavioral Sampling
We used two sampling methods to collect
behavioral data—scan sampling and focal sampling
[Altmann, 1974; Martin & Bateson, 2007]. Scan
sampling was used to determine the general activity
budgets and time spent engaged in tools use, whereas
focal sampling was used to acquire more speciﬁc data
on the characteristics of tool-use bouts. When
observing macaques, scan samples were performed
once every 10–15 min, while 5-min focal samples
were conducted continuously when subjects were
available. Scoring was performed by recording
observations into a digital audio recorder and later
transcribing the data into score sheets.
During scan samples, we scored feeding activity,
tool use, social behavior, resting, traveling, and
distance to nearest partner. When tool use was
observed in a scan, we recorded hand use, tool size,
and food type. In focal samples, we recorded all tool-
use activity and scored the size of tool, type of food,
bout duration, number of strikes, and success of
bout. A bout was deﬁned as the time from which an
individual began using a tool (i.e. starts striking)
until the point in time where the individual either
consumed (i.e. successful) or discarded/left the food
item (i.e. unsuccessful).
Tool size was visually estimated using binocu-
lars by comparing the size of the tool to the hand size
of the macaque using the tool. Tool size was
therefore a relative measure of the stone compared
with the size of the hand of the macaque being
observed, and not an absolute measure of the actual
weight or size of the stone. A small tool was hand size
or smaller (i.e. approx 30–150 g), a medium tool was
1–2 times the size of the hand (i.e. approx 150–400 g),
and a large tool was greater than twice the size of
single hand (i.e. approx 4400 g with max size up to
5 kg).
Foods were categorized into three basic cate-
gories: attached oysters, loose oysters, and other
shelled foods. Attached foods were sessile rock
oysters ( Saccostrea cucullata ) that were cemented
to an immovable substrate (e.g. rock or tree
buttress). Loose oysters included rock oysters, or
clusters of rock oysters that were already broken
away from rocks, as well as oysters attached to small
rocks that the macaques could carry and move. All
other shelled foods were nonsessile or moveable food
items that included snails (e.g. Thais spp., Nerita
spp., and other gastropods), crabs ( Thalamita spp.,
Grapsus spp., Scylla spp., etc.), bivalves (e.g. Gafrar-
ium spp., Viridis spp., Asaphis spp. etc.), and nuts
(e.g. Terminalia catappa and other types .). In
addition to the listed species, some observations
contained unidentiﬁed foods, as we could not always
identify prey species during our observation. When
this occurred, the item was scored as either attached
or unattached.
In this study, we did not have time to individu-
ally identify the macaques, and thus each sampled
animal was categorized only by sex and age (i.e. adult
and immature). The immature class included juve-
niles and adolescents, and infants (0–1 year) were not
studied. Lacking individual recognition, we used the
following methods to ensure that a macaque was
not scored twice during a single scan or focal
sample cycle, limiting the possibility of over-repre-
senting certain individuals in the data set. For scan
samples, we started with the animal in the farthest
left of our ﬁeld of vision, and moved from this
position to the right scanning each individual until
we had scored the individual in the farthest right of
our ﬁeld of vision. Similarly, focal samples were
collected using the same basic method, starting with
the animal at the farthest left, and then moving from
left to right until all animals were sampled. Since
animals were more likely to move and change
position during the longer duration of our 5-min
focal samples, we kept track of the spatial locations
of each animal on the shore, ensuring that an animal
was not double-sampled in a single cycle. When only
a few animals were present, only one 5-min focal
sample cycle was performed in a 30-min period per
individual.
Data Analysis
We pooled all feeding scan samples ( N 5 392)
and tool-use bouts in focal samples ( N 5 352) for
analysis. Data pooling can violate statistical assump-
tions, but for this study, we were unable to
individually identify animals and thus were limited
in this respect. In data sets where intrasubject
variation exceeds between-subject variation, or
sample sizes are equal, data pooling does not bias
results [Leger & Didrichsons, 1994]. We thus
assumed these parameters to be true of our data
set, but present our data with the knowledge that
there are limitations and a greater risk of Type 1
error. We moreover, used the sampling methods
described above to minimize the effects of over-
representing any single individual in the data set.
Data were placed into spreadsheets and input
into PSAW 18.0 (SPSS, 2009). Scan sampling data
were used to assess differences in the frequency of tool
use during feeding, preferences for the types of foods
processed with tools and tool-size preferences. We
pooled all tool-use bouts from focal samples and used
this data set to compare differences in the number of
strikes per bout, duration of bouts, strike rate, and
success. Focal data were also used to assess rates of
food processing while foraging with tools. Compari-
sons were performed using ANOVA, MANOVA,
ANCOVA, and t-test statistical tests. Our ANCOVA
designs tested the dependent variables of strike rate,
bout duration, and number of strikes per bout, while
sex, age, and food type were input as factor. We

RESULTS
Differences in Tool-Use During Feeding
A2 /C22 ANOVA was used to assess sex and age
differences in the proportion of feeding scan samples
where tools were used. Our results showed that in 392
feeding scans, females more frequently used tools than
males did ( # 5 0.53; ~ 5 0.72) ( F 5 6.787; df 5 388.3;
P 5 0.010), and that adults used tools more frequently
than immature individuals (adult 5 0.65, imma-
ture 5 0.53) (F 5 4.477; df 5 388.3; P 5 0.035). We also
compared 284 samples with only adult males and
females, and found a signiﬁcant difference between
them ( # 5 0.55; ~ 5 0.76) (independent t-test:
t 5 /C03.819; df 5 282; Po0.001). In this data set,
females used tools more often than males; and adults
more than immatures.
Differences in Food Preferences
Using a MANOVA design, we used all tool-use
scans samples where food was identiﬁed ( N 5 188) to
compare the frequency of processing three food types
(i.e. attached oysters, loose oysters, and all other
loose food items), testing for sex and age differences.
We found signiﬁcant sex differences in our data for
food preferences in adults (Wilk’s Lambda:
F 5 21.968; df 5 185.2; Po0.001; Fig. 1). Adult
females extracted attached oysters in signiﬁcantly
more tool-using scan samples than did adult males
(# 5 0.40; ~ 5 0.81) (
F 5 42.066; df 5 186.1;
Po0.001). In contrast, adult males extracted sig-
niﬁcantly more loose oysters ( # 5 0.28; ~ 5 0.06)
and other unattached shelled foods ( # 5 0.32;
~ 5 0.13) in tool-using scan samples than did adult
females (loose oysters: F 5 20.118; df 5 186.1;
Po0.001; other unattached shelled foods:
F 5 10.396; df 5 186.1; P 5 0.001). We did not ﬁnd
signiﬁcant differences in food preferences when
comparing across age classes.

Differences in Tool Bout Characteristics
After pooling 352 tool-use bouts extracted from
our focal sample set, we tested for differences in tool-
bout characteristics (i.e. number of strikes per bout,
bout duration, and strike rate). In an ANCOVA
design, we entered sex, age, and food type as ﬁxed
factors and tool size as a covariate. We found that tool
size was correlated with strikes per bout, and that sex
and food type signiﬁcantly affected the number of
strikes per bout. Females struck their food items
more times per bout than males ( F 5 25.031;
df 5 341.8; Po0.001; Fig. 4), but we could not ﬁnd a
difference between adults and immature individuals
(F 5 2.086; df 5 341.8; P 5 0.694). More strikes were
used on attached foods per bout than unattached

DISCUSSION
Our study provides the ﬁrst assessment of
possible sex differences in the tool-use patterns of
long-tailed macaques living along the Andaman Sea.
In a 2-week period in December 2009, during the dry
season in Thailand, we found that when macaques
feed on the shores and mangroves, females used tools
more often than males. Furthermore, we found
evidence that males and females showed varying
preferences for the food sources extracted with tools.
Females extracted sessile oysters more fre-
quently than males, whereas males extracted
unattached items more than females. Sex differences
were also found for the size of the tool selected, with
females preferring small hand-sized tools and males
using larger tools more frequently than females. Less
clear sex differences emerged when examining the
characteristics of tool-use bouts. Males were more
successful than females at pounding open unat-
tached food items, and females were found to strike
more often and at a faster rate during tool-use than
males. Male tool bouts were shorter than females,
but their overall food intake while using a tool was
not found to differ from females. Neither sex showed
any large differences in their proﬁciency and
efﬁciency at processing foods with stone tools, except
that males may have been more proﬁcient at
pounding open loose food items.
We also found few differences across age class.
We found that adults used tools more frequently
than immature individuals, but could not ﬁnd
differences in food choice. The only relationship we

Our study was limited in scope for several
reasons. First, our data covered only a 2-week period,
and so was unable to observe changes in sex
differences throughout the year. Perhaps there is
seasonal variation in the tool behavior of either sex,
as weather conditions, water depth, fruit masts, and
availability of food sources (e.g. sea almond masts,
changes in oyster abundance, etc) change throughout
the year. Moreover, we did not assess changes in the
tool-using behavior of females across their reproduc-
tive cycles. We also remain unable to track macaques
on the island, due to the mountainous terrain and
cliff faces they inhabit, and thus have no data on
their inland tool use. Finally, our study lacks
identiﬁed subjects, which was a result of not having
enough time to complete the task in a 2-week period.
During a study recently conducted on other
aspects of tool use in this population between
January and June 2011, we observed females con-
sistently using tools when bearing young, and when
being heavily courted by males. Moreover, oysters
have remained proliﬁcally fed on between the
months of December and May. Furthermore, the
researcher of that study (M.D.G.) identiﬁed animals
and can now individually recognize 148 individuals
out of approximately 200 macaques living on Piak
Nam Yai Island [Gumert et al., in review]. Further
analysis will be completed later in 2011.
Comparison With Macaque Stone Handling
The closest behavior to stone tool-use in any
other macaque species is the nonfunctional stone
handling of Japanese macaques [Huffman & Quiatt,
1986; Huffman et al., 2010]. Our results on stone tool
use differed from the sex and age differences found
in stone handling studies in two ways.
First, in a study of ten groups of Japanese
macaques, no clear adult sex differences were found,
and only one of the groups showed a bias, with
adolescent (4–6 years) and young adult (7–10 years)
males engaging in more frequent stone handling
than females of the same age classes [Leca et al.,
2007a,b]. Another study on the same group showed
adult females performed fewer acts of stone handling
and fewer patterns than other age-sex classes
[Nahallage & Huffman, 2007a]. In our study, both
males and females used tools frequently (i.e. in over
1/2 of feeding scans), but females used tools
signiﬁcantly more often than males.
Second, studies have also found that stone
handling is more pervasive in young Japanese
macaques, but it becomes more complex with age
[Leca et al., 2007a,b; Nahallage & Huffman,
2007a,b]. Unlike stone handling, stone tool use is,
predictably, more common in adult long-tailed
macaques. Tool use is a more complex behavioral
pattern though, consistent with the increasing
complexity found in stone handling patterns. The
major reason stone handling occurs more frequently
in younger Japanese macaques is that it is largely a
form of play [Huffman, 1984].
We have not systematically studied nonfunc-
tional stone handling in our project, but we have
made ad libitum notes when noticed. We have
observed in our study population of tool-using
macaques several of the types of stone handling
patterns described by Leca et al. [2007a,b] and
Nahallage and Huffman [2007a,b]. However, non-
functional stone handling does not seem to be as
pervasive or common as reported in Japanese
macaques. Stone handling has only been seen to
occur in young animals, approximately aged
6 months to 5 or 6 years of age, with proﬁcient stone
tool use emerging as early as 1 year of age. Further
work will be needed to investigate the role of stone
handling in the development of tool-using skills.
Comparative studies between naturally emerging
functional and nonfunctional stone use in macaques
may be highly relevant to understanding the evolu-
tion and development of stone tool use.
Comparison With Chimpanzees and Capuchins
Our results on sex differences do not fully
overlap with previous ﬁndings on sex-differences in
the tool-use of chimpanzee or capuchins; however,
we did ﬁnd some similarities. We observed a female
bias in frequency of tool use, as in chimpanzees
[Boesch & Boesch, 1984; Lonsdorf et al., 2004], and
we found a male bias for using larger tools, as in
capuchin stone tool use [Fragaszy et al., 2010].
Female chimpanzees are further reported to be more
efﬁcient in their tool-using abilities, but we could not

As with capuchins, more frequent use of large
pounding tools by macaque males is likely the result
of the constraints smaller-bodied primates face due
to a larger tool-to-body size ratio and/or tool-to-hand
size ratio. Since male macaques are about 150% the
size of females, they may have signiﬁcant advantages
for being able to carry, control and skillfully use
larger tools. When the tool size constraint was
removed and we assessed only hand-sized tools, the
male bias was no longer observable, and we rather
found that females used small tools more frequently.
Similarly, in bearded capuchins, when the need
for a large tool is removed to crack low resistance
nuts, males and females use similarly sized tools
[Spagnoletti et al., 2011]. Size may not be the only
factor in a male bias in capuchin tool use though
because some preliminary studies show male
capuchins also more frequently use smaller tools
for probing and digging [Falo ´tico & Ottoni, 2010;
Moura & Lee, 2010]. If conﬁrmed in further study,
this would be inconsistent with the body size
hypothesis as a complete explanation for a male bias.
Although phylogenetic similarity may offer a
possible explanation for the similarity between
macaques and chimpanzees in frequency of female
tool use, with a sample of only three species this
remains inconclusive and speculative. In chimpan-
zees, the female bias in tool use is associated with sex
differences in the dependence on different food
sources [McGrew, 1992], as well as males being more
distracted than females from tool use by social
activities [Boesch & Boesch, 1984; Lonsdorf, 2005;
Lonsdorf et al., 2004]. Differences in attraction to
social activity over foraging might also be playing a
role in the macaque female bias we found in tool use;
however, in long-tailed macaques, females are
typically more gregarious and spend more time in
social activity than males [Gumert, 2010].
We found that females relied more heavily on
attached oysters than males, suggesting a sex
difference in foraging strategy. Females, being more
constrained by reproductive costs [Trivers, 1972],
might focus on a more sessile, easily captured food
than males. Oyster foraging requires carrying a
small tool and moving from oyster to oyster.
In contrast, pounding requires the collection of loose
foods and transporting them to large tools with
anvils by carrying the food in the hands or food
pouches. This method allows a large amount of food
to be cracked in a single tool-use episode, but
may also require greater effort and risk during
the collection phase. Collection of loose shells in
the intertidal zones requires greater exploratory
behavior that involves ﬂipping over heavy rocks,
reaching deeply in the mud, and foraging in the
shallow waters, potentially exposing the individual to
greater risks.
Differential vulnerability to predation can also
play a role in establishing sex differences in tool
activity. For example, in capuchins, it is reported
that females engage in food pounding less often than
males [Boinski et al., 2000]. Food pounding is not
tool use, but is a similar behavior that is also highly
conspicuous and noisy because it involves banging a
hard-cased nut on a rock anvil or tree buttress. It is
suggested that females refrain from this activity to
avoid predation, thus remaining more cryptic. This
same limitation could also be one constraint on
female capuchin tool use, however a very small
sample size showed that tool-using capuchins show
no sex differences in vigilance while feeding with
tools [Spagnoletti et al., 2011].
In the macaques we studied, natural predation
risks along the shores appeared minimal, and thus
would unlikely have historically put any signiﬁcant
constraints on macaque tool use. We occasionally
heard snake alarms, and observed very large reticu-
lated pythons ( Python reticulatus ) and venomous
mangrove vipers ( Trimeresurus purpureomaculatus )
along the shores and mangroves that could kill
macaques. Macaques rarely alarmed at raptors, and
the majority of raptors were sea eagles ( Haliaeetus
leucogaster), which prey on ﬁsh. Since 2008 though,
humans farm developers on Piak Nam Yai have
introduced hunting dogs ( Canis familiaris ), which
are affecting the shore usage and group composition
of the groups having the greatest overlap with the
disturbance [Gumert et al., in review]. If continued,
the anthropogenic disturbance could impact each sex
differently and potentially have detrimental impacts
on their ‘‘fragile’’ traditions [van Schaik, 2002].
Macaque Tool Use and Human Sex Differences
The chimpanzee model of tool use has been used
to help inform the evolution of tool use in humans,
and it suggests that primate tool use began as a form
of solitary processing of encased foods that was
probably female biased [Boesch & Boesch, 2000;
McGrew, 1981, 1992; Teleki, 1974]. It has been
argued that female gathering techniques have played
a key role in the evolution of human traits such as
tool use [Zilman, 1981], and the female bias found in
chimpanzee stone pounding and termite ﬁshing is
considered evidence that the earliest forms of
primate tool use were related to female gathering
techniques [McGrew, 1981, 1992]. Our preliminary
results in macaques are consistent with the hypoth-
esis that primate tool use ﬁrst emerged through the
individualistic extraction of plant foods and small
invertebrates, and that females utilized it more
frequently. If future work conﬁrms the female bias
