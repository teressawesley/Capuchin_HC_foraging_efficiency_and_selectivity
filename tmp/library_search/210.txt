Rise and spread of 
a social tradition 
of interspecies 
abduction
Zoë Goldsborough1,2,3,4,*, 
Margaret C. Crofoot1,2,3,4, 
Odd T. Jacobson1,2, Lisa Corewyn5, 
Evelyn del Rosario-Vargas4, 
Julián León1,6,4, 
and Brendan J. Barrett1,2,3,4,*
Cultural traditions lacking clear function 
are exceptionally common in humans, 
partially explained by our hyper-reliance 
on social learning
1. In non-human 
animals, it is unclear whether the same 
ecological and social conditions drive 
the emergence of both seemingly 
adaptive and non-adaptive traditions. 
Here, we describe the origins and 
spread of a tradition of interspecies 
abduction in the wild. We documented 
carrying of eleven different infant howler 
monkeys (Alouatta palliata coibensis) 
by ﬁ ve immature male white-faced 
capuchin monkeys (Cebus capucinus 
imitator) over 15 months on Jicarón 
island, Coiba National Park, Panama. All 
cases occurred in one capuchin group, 
which has been studied since 2017 
for their localized tradition of habitual 
stone tool-use, unique to Cebus
2. We 
captured the origin of this ‘howler 
abduction’ tradition, starting with one 
subadult male innovator, and its spread 
to four subadult and juvenile males 
(
Figure 1 and Supplemental information; 
see also interactive timeline: https://
www.ab.mpg.de/671374/Capuchin-tool-
use/interspecies-abduction-tradition
). 
We argue that the same conditions 
which favored adaptive cultural 
innovations like tool-use on Jicarón also 
underlie this non-adaptive tradition. 
Observations come from 86 unbaited 
camera traps deployed from January 
2022 to July 2023. After discovering 
the ﬁ rst evidence of capuchins 
carrying howler infants, we reviewed 
all multimedia — yielding 131 sightings 
(Supplemental information). Since this 
project’s initiation in March 2017, we 
deployed and processed data from 
76 cameras in the tool-using group’s 
Correspondence home range, with near-continuous 
temporal coverage. We also deployed 
47 cameras in multiple non-tool-using 
groups’ ranges on Jicarón. Due to this 
extensive longitudinal monitoring, we 
are conﬁ dent we captured the tradition’s 
origin near 26 January 2022, with the 
sighting of a juvenile capuchin carrying 
a howler infant. We never observed 
this behavior in other groups. In the 
following four months, a single subadult 
male emerged as the main carrier, 
carrying four different howler infants 
for up to nine days. Exposure occurred 
as other capuchins were present in 14 
of 20 sightings, but showed minimal 
interaction with howler infants — except 
howler 3 (H3), whose corpse was 
pulled on by the innovator and two 
subsequent carriers (
Figure 1). Starting 
in September 2022, the tradition spread 
and accelerated as we observed 
four additional male capuchins (two 
subadult, two juvenile) carrying howler 
infants. From September 2022 to March 
2023, we observed these capuchins 
carrying at least seven different howler 
infants (aged one to two days to four 
weeks) for two- to eight-day stretches, 
totaling eleven unique howler infants 
(Supplemental information). 
Most howler infants appeared healthy 
in initial sightings, but deteriorated 
over time. At least four howler infants 
died, seemingly from malnourishment.

wounding nor predation of howler 
infants. We believe these are cases 
of abduction rather than adoption 
following maternal abandonment for the 
following reasons: novel howler infants 
appeared at a high rate (including two 
simultaneously), howler infants were 
forcibly prevented from escaping, and 
adult howlers (out of frame yet audible) 
lost-called back and forth with infants 
for up to 30-minute periods. Given that 
adult howlers are nearly three times 
the weight of immature capuchins, 
abductions are likely to be risky. How 
capuchins abducted howlers remains 
unknown, because our camera traps 
do not capture activity in trees, where 
abductions are likely to have occurred. 
However, non-carrying capuchins 
aided in preventing adult howlers 
from retrieving infants, evidenced 
by capuchins threatening an adult 
howler during an attempted escape 
by H10 (Video S1; 
https://youtu.be/
s3Y3JWVvB9Q). 
Although howlers and capuchins 
are both well-studied species with 
extensively overlapping ranges, our 
observations are unique. Most reports 
of interspeciﬁ c interaction involve 
aggression (from capuchins to howlers), 
with some reports of infant play and 
grooming
3. In contrast, we observed 
no interspeciﬁ c play or grooming. 
Interactions were typically neutral, with 
dorsal or ventral carrying during normal 
activities. However, in two sightings, 
the carrier embraced the infant. In 
four sightings, the carrier nipped or 
swatted at the infant. All later adopters 
used stone tools while carrying infants 
(
Figure 1), a behavior risky for the 
howler, which once resulted in the 
howler infant falling off. 
What motivates the carriers? 
Dietary differences render interspeciﬁ c 
competition unlikely. Anecdotes of 
between-species adoption occur across 
taxa, but in most cases, females carry 
infants. In this population, we only see 
immature male carriers. Male white-
faced capuchins exhibit alloparental 
care, but this is limited to short periods 
of play and carrying
4. Immature males 
being the only howler carriers could 
reﬂ ect their high tendency to innovate 
and learn socially, also evidenced by 
them being proliﬁ c tool-users. Infants 
might also function as ‘status symbols’ 
or a way to avoid conﬂ ict (agonistic 
buffering
5). However, carriers do not 
receive positive social attention from 
group-mates (i.e., grooming). We 
did see adult capuchins threatening 
carriers, and juveniles observing them 
from a distance. The only infant transfer 
we observed was not competitive; 
instead, the carrier lost interest 
and another individual retrieved the 
discarded howler. 
Abducting and carrying howler infants 
appears to have no obvious ﬁ tness 
beneﬁ ts or positive social reinforcement. 
In contrast to later adopters, the 
innovator showed prosocial interactions 
with, and a complete absence of 
aggression toward, the infants he 
carried — misdirected alloparental care 
could explain his behavior. The behavior 
of later adopters, who engaged in risky 
behavior like tool-use while carrying and 
showed aggression to the infants, may 
best be understood as a fashion trend, 
similar to the ‘grass-in-ear’ behavior in 
chimpanzees
6. Capuchins appear to 
carry howler infants solely for carrying’s 
sake. 
Innovations are common on islands 
due to both ecological need and 
behavioral opportunity
7. Additionally, 
reduced predation risk coupled with 
‘under-stimulation’ resulting from 
low species richness and reduced 
social cohesion might yield free time 
and potentially ‘boredom’, which are 
linked to innovation in both humans 
and animals
8,9. Building on Henrich9, 
our ﬁ ndings suggest that necessity, 
while important, need not be the 
mother of invention — especially 
on islands, where both need and 
free time are often abundant. We 
demonstrate that longitudinal camera 
trapping can do more than quantify 
species and behavioral richness
10; it 
can successfully document cultural 
dynamics in unhabituated animals. The 
howler monkeys that inhabit Jicarón 
are an endangered subspecies. If this 
tradition of infant abduction persists, 
it poses a serious conservation 
risk. These patterns highlight that 
cultural behaviors, whether by 
humans or non-humans, can have 
negative consequences for vulnerable 
populations coexisting within the same 
ecosystem.
