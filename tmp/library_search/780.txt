Tool Use in Wild Capuchin Monkeys
(Cebus albifrons trinitatis)
KIMBERLEY A. PHILLIPS*
Department of Psychology, Hiram College, Hiram, Ohio
Department of Biology, Hiram College, Hiram, Ohio
White-fronted capuchins were observed to use leaves as cups to retrieve
water from tree cavities. On multiple occasions several individuals per-
formed this behavior. Thus, these capuchins engage in habitual tool use,
as defined by McGrew’s classificatory scheme of tool using behavior. Am.

Key words: tool use, Cebus, white-fronted capuchin
INTRODUCTION
Although capuchin monkeys ( Cebus spp.) readily use tools in captivity
[Visalberghi, 1990; Westergaard & Fragaszy, 1987], tool use in wild populations has
been reported only anecdotally and is typically observed as an isolated occurrence
[e.g., Boinski, 1988]. Here I report multiple observations of wild white-fronted capu-
chins (Cebus albifrons trinitatis) using leaves as cups to retrieve water from tree
cavities. These capuchins modified the leaves before use by changing the shape of
the leaf. However, tool fidelity was not observed; that is, the monkeys would use
another leaf for repeat visits to the tree cavity. These observations indicate that, like
chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes) [Boesch & Boesch, 1990], wild capuchins demonstrate
tool manufacture and use in foraging-related contexts.
The observations described here occurred during an ongoing study of the
behavioral ecology of white-fronted capuchins in Trinidad. The study site is the
1,550 hectare Bush Bush Wildlife Sanctuary, a seasonally evergreen forest lo-
cated in the southern portion of the Nariva Swamp. This wildlife sanctuary was
founded in 1968 and was managed by the Trinidad Regional Virus Laboratory
and the New York Zoological Society until the government resumed management
control in 1971. Red howler monkeys ( Alouatta seniculus insulanus ) and white-
fronted capuchins, Trinidad’s only endemic nonhuman primates, are both found
within the sanctuary.
In 1996 I began long-term research on the behavioral and ecology of these capu-
chins. As no prior research on social behavior has been conducted at this site, kin-
ship data amongst the capuchins are unavailable. During June 1997 I observed a
habituated troop of ten capuchins. One afternoon I observed two older juveniles (sex
undetermined) engaged in a typical play bout. At one point they rested, and the
larger juvenile collected water from a tree cavity. This monkey cleaned debris out of

the cavity and then proceeded to use one of the removed leaves as a cup from which
to collect and drink the water. The leaf was crumbled, so it likely was able to absorb
water more efficiently. The second juvenile also drank from the cavity but was not
observed to use a leaf in this manner; instead, it used the common pattern of using
its hands, feet, and/or tail. This combination of play and stopping to drink continued
for 34 min. The first monkey retrieved water using a leaf three times during the
observation period. Each episode lasted between 2 and 5 min, and each episode was
similar in the manner that it was performed. On two other occasions, a subadult
female and another juvenile of the same troop were observed to use leaves to aid in
retrieving water. Their pattern was similar in that they used one of the removed
leaves to aid in the process. The duration of these bouts was between 1 and 5 min.
Overall, this is an infrequent behavior pattern, with less than .001 events occurring
per hour. Two other capuchin troops present in the wildlife sanctuary were observed
periodically; however, no individual in these troops was observed using leaves in
this manner.
McGrew [1992] and McGrew and Marchant’s [1997] classificatory scheme of ha-
bitual and occasional tool use among Cebus and Pan provides a useful framework in
which to place these observations. Based upon these definitions, it appears that the
behavior described is an example of habitual tool use, as these are repeated events
by several individuals over time. These observations provide a further example of
wild capuchins using tools in a subsistence technology form, as Westergaard [1994]
reports.
Cebus are arboreal New World primates characterized by their behavioral flex-
ibility and manipulative abilities in foraging contexts [Fragaszy & Visalberghi, 1989].
The behavior of inserting an object into a hole and retrieving something from it is a
manipulative ability well documented in captive experiments involving capuchins
and is a similar behavior to that of chimpanzees of Gombe and the Mahale Moun-
tains [Boesch & Boesch, 1990]. The present behavior is much like the sponging-
water-with-leaves behavior that Goodall [1986] originally observed in the Gombe
chimpanzees. Unlike chimpanzees, laboratory studies have indicated that Cebus likely
do not learn through imitation or observational learning but rather through local
site enhancement [Fragaszy & Visalberghi, 1989]. That is, social influences on the
acquisition of tool-using behavior in Cebus appear to be limited to enhancing inter-
est in that object or location. This, as well as incomplete observations, might explain
the lack of troop-wide tool use in this instance.
CONCLUSIONS
Wild white-fronted capuchins ( Cebus albifrons trinitatis ) in Trinidad used
leaves as cups to retrieve water from tree cavities.
