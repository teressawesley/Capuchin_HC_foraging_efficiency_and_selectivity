Semifree-ranging Tufted Capuchins (Cebus apella)
Spontaneously Use Tools to Crack Open Nuts
Eduardo B. Ottoni1,2 and Massimo Mannu1
Received November 12, 1999; revision February 22, 2000; accepted May 16, 2000
Naturalistic studies on tool use by nonhuman primates have focused almost
exclusively on Old World monkeys or hominoids. We studied the cracking of
Syagrus nuts with the aid of stones by a group of semifree-ranging capuchins
living in a reforested area (Tietˆe Ecological Park, S˜ao Paulo, Brazil). Our data
are from direct observation and from mapping nut-cracking site utilization.
All adults, subadults and juveniles (plus one infant) crack nuts, but individual
differences in frequency and proﬁciency are marked. Juveniles do most of
the nut-cracking, but adults are, on average, more efﬁcient; the frequency of
inept stone manipulation decreases with age. About 10% of the nut-cracking
episodes were watched by other individuals—mostly infants and juveniles,
suggesting a role for observational learning, even if restricted to stimulus en-
hancement.
KEY WORDS: capuchin; foraging; tool use.
Experimental studies and naturalistic observations on the use of tools by
nonhuman primates brought important insights on the evolution of primate
cognition and challenged the notion of the unique nature of human techno-
logical capacities. Until recently, the studies were focused almost exclusively
on Old World monkeys or apes (McGrew, 1992).
Reports on the spontaneous use of tools by capuchins, date back to the
writings of Gonzalo Fern´andez de Oviedo in 1526 (cited by Urbani, 1998),

Many laboratory experiments on tool use in problem-solving have been
carried out with capuchins (Westergaard and Fragaszy, 1987; Anderson,
1990; Visalberghi, 1990; Westergaard and Suomi, 1993), and some
(Visalberghiet al., 1995) involve interspeciﬁc comparisons with chimpanzees,
bonobos and orangutans, which indicated a gap between monkey and ape
understanding of causal relationships.
Very few studies were conducted in the species’ natural environment
or under semicaptive conditions (Perondi et al., 1995), and there are only
sparse reports on spontaneous tool use by capuchins in the wild. Phillips
(1998) observed a few occurrences of the use of leaves as cups to retrieve
water from tree cavities by some individuals in a group of white-fronted
capuchins (Cebus albifrons trinitatis).
Spontaneous nut-cracking by a population of Cebus apella was inde-
pendently reported by Izawa and Mizuno (1977) and Struhsaker and Leland
(1977) and consisted, in this case, of beating the nuts of the palm ( Astro-
carium chambira) against the joints of a bamboo tree (Bambusa guadua)o r
beating two nuts against one another. A similar behavior was exhibited by
captives (Ferreira et al., 1996).
Cracking Syagrus nuts with stones has been reported by people living
near free-ranging capuchin populations, and its indirect evidence—the nut-
cracking sites, usually composed of some piled stones and broken nutshells—
were observed and described by Mannu and Ottoni (1996), Rocha et al .
(1996: Syagrus romanzofﬁana), and Langguth and Alonso (1997:S. oleracea).
We provide a general description and some demographic aspects of
the spontaneous use of tools in the cracking of Syagrus nuts by a semifree-
ranging group of tufted capuchins ( Cebus apella). Our long-term research
aims are the relations between tool use and social dynamics: between
individual learning and the eventual processes of social information
transfer.

Subjects
The subjects had lived in the park for about seven years. The older
adults (two males and three females) were released in the park islands after
conﬁscation by the IBAMA (federal wildlife agency) and formed the group
spontaneously, after escaping the islands by swimming. All other individuals
were born there.
The group comprised (in October 1998) 18 individuals: four adult males
(two of them castrated, one of which was probably the former alpha male),
three adult females, two subadult (5–8 years old) males, one subadult female,
four juveniles (2–5 years old) and four infants. Another adult male died at
the beginning of the study, and his data are not included in analyses. We
collected the data between August 1997 and October 1998.
The group is daily provisioned, but the capuchins do not limit them-
selves to the provided food (mainly fruits, eggs and hasty pudding). Instead,
they forage through the whole area, consuming naturally available items.
Sometimes they stay away from the food trough for‚2 days, and part of the
provisions usually rots there, which suggests that they might survive without
provisioning.
Procedure
We observed their nut-cracking activity directly and indirectly. In the
latter case, we cleaned once a week all identiﬁed nut cracking sites, comprised
of mounds of‚2 rocks (or, less frequently, of rocks and pieces of wood or old
cement pavements), inspected all remnants of Syagrus nuts, and registered
their maturation state.
For the direct observation, we employed an all-occurrences procedure
(Martin and Bateson, 1993): along 132 observation hours we registered any
events of stone or nut manipulation or both: date/time, subject, site, ma-
terials, observation by other individuals and some postural aspects of the
nut-cracking behaviour.

RESULTS
The group spent most of their activity time foraging and moving from
one food source to another. Besides provisions supplied in the morning
at the trough, they searched for and consumed a large array of seasonally
variable, naturally available food items, including plant parts, small animals,
eggs and larvae. Both search and consumption frequently involved complex
manipulatory behavior. For instance in the exploration of tree trunks and
branches with the apparent aim of locating areas where the bark is loose
due to the presence of larvae, we observed quick ﬁngertip tapping. We had
previously observed this during the manipulation of a wooden box in a ﬁeld
experiment (Perondi et al., 1995). It seems to be quite similar to percussive
foraging (Ericksonet al., 1998) during the exploratory behaviour of aye-ayes
(Daubentonia madagascariensis).
Nut-Cracking
Twice, an adult female plucked a green Syagrus nut, beat it repeatedly
against the tree trunk, orally peeled the epicarp of one extremity of the nut,
picked a hole in it with a canine tooth, and drank the liquid endosperm.
Consumption of the solid endosperm from mature nuts required far
more elaborated procedures. Mature nuts were usually cracked open with
the aid of two stones, one with a horizontal surface, laid on the substrate
(the anvil) and the other, smaller, held in the hands (the hammer). In a few
occasions, the anvil was a root or a half-buried piece of old cement pavement,
and a piece of wood was once used as a hammer. Hammer stones were usually
held in both hands (they seem to be quite heavy for the monkeys), raised
quickly to the level of the head and then slammed against the nut on the anvil.
The tail is held stifﬂy propped against the ground, acting as a third supporting
point (Fig. 1). In some occasions, the monkey actually jumped upwards,
but the tail tip maintained contact with the ground. Whereas hammering
involves mainly strength and bodily equilibrium, positioning the nut on the
anvil requires careful manipulation (Fig. 2), especially on sloping anvils.

DISCUSSION
Tufted capuchins (Cebus apella) are a generalist and opportunist species
with a very diversiﬁed diet including not only fruits, leaves, ﬂowers, buds
and other plant parts but also arthropods, eggs and small vertebrates. To
be located, consumed or both many of their food items require sophisti-
cated manipulation techniques, whose acquisition may involve mechanisms
of social information transfer (King, 1991). Our study is the ﬁrst to go be-
yond anecdotal reports and to provide information on sex-age differences
in spontaneous capuchin tool use in a naturalistic setting.
Anderson (1990) observed capuchin stone nut-cracking in the labora-
tory and suggested this could, to some extent, be an artifact of captivity, not
to be expected in the wild due to the arboreal lifestyle of capuchins. How-
ever, arboreality is a matter of degree: in some more open environments,
like the Cerrado (Brazilian bush savannah) or the Caatinga (northeastern
Brazilian dry thornbush), or even in the rain forest (P . Izar, J. R´ımoli, per-
sonal communications), capuchins may spend considerably more time on
the ground. Our observations of their nut cracking body postures–as well
as their dexterity when they walk bipedally while carrying stones or other
objects in their hands–suggest that capuchins are well adapted for terrestrial
activities.

Conversely, considering both the cognitive abilities and motor skills of
capuchins, Visalberghi (1978) predicted that tufted capuchins would be likely
to show innovative behavior and tool use for cracking nuts, if observed in
ﬁeld sites where nuts and stones were available.
Westergaard (1994) used Oswaldt’s classiﬁcation system to compare the
tool-kits of captive and wild capuchins, Tanzanian chimpanzees and Tasma-
nian aborigines, concluding that, though capuchins have smaller tool-kits,
like chimpanzees and aborigines, captive capuchins have tool-kits that in-
clude instruments, weapons, facilities, and subsistents produced by reduc-
tion and replication. He pointed out that additional research was needed to
determine if, like humans, capuchins can produce tools by linkage and con-
junction. Since hammers and anvils are physically distinct forms combined
to perform a particular purpose, they fulﬁll the deﬁnition of linkage.
Langguth and Alonso (1997) consider that cracking of Syagrus nuts
might indicate use of a less preferred resource in times of food scarcity.
Since it is not the case in our study, we hypothesize that the occurrence
of nut-cracking depends on a trade-off between predation risk, the kind
of vegetation (determining the rates of terrestriality) and food availability.
Accordingly, under favorable conditions, nut-cracking may occur even with
other food sources available.
Our qualitative observations on the frequently synchronized onset of
nut-cracking bouts suggest that there may be a social facilitation effect: indi-
viduals seem to be more prone to crack nuts when others are already doing
it, which would generate a dillution effect, diminishing individual predation
risks.
Juveniles seem to be the most active nutcrackers, perhaps because they
have restricted access to more desirable (more easily processed) food items,
or, being more dispersed, nuts are less subject to dominant adult monopoly.
Conversely, manipulatory activity seems to be very attractive to juvenile ca-
puchins, even when there is no contingency between manipulation and food
acquisition (Visalberghi, 1988; Fragaszy and Adams-Curtis, 1997). Further
juveniles are generally bolder explorers, while dominants tend to expose
themselves less to the risks of terrestrial activity.
Proﬁciency in nut-cracking apparently takes years to achieve and pos-
sibly not all individuals attain it fully, as suggested by the data on inept
manipulation. These results are analogous to those on chimpanzees (Inoue-
Nakamura and Matsuzawa, 1997), which, though having already acquired
the basic actions for nut-cracking by 2.5 years, do not combine them in an
appropriate sequence until 3.5 years old.
As with chimpanzees, infant capuchins are highly tolerated by adults,
and episodes involving the observation of older, more proﬁcient individuals
by younger ones, sometimes followed by manipulation of the stones by the
