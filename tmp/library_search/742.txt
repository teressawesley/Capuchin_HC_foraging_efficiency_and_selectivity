New Caledonian (NC) crows Corvus moneduloides are the most proliﬁc  avian tool users. In the wild, they
use
 at least three distinct tool types to extract invertebrate prey from deadwood and vegetation, with
some
 of their tools requiring complex manufacture, modiﬁcation  and/or deployment. Experiments with
captive-bred,
 hand-raised NC crows have demonstrated that the species has a strong genetic predispo-
sition
 for basic tool use and manufacture, suggesting that this behaviour is an evolved adaptation. This
view
 is supported by recent stable-isotope analyses of the diets of wild crows, which revealed that tool
use
 provides access to highly proﬁtable  hidden prey, with preliminary data indicating that parents pref-
erentially
 feed their offspring with tool-derived food. Building on this work, our review examines the
possible
 evolutionary origins of these birds’ remarkable tool-use behaviour. Whilst robust comparative
analyses
 are impossible, given the phylogenetic rarity of animal tool use, our examination of a wide range
of
 circumstantial evidence enables a ﬁrst  attempt at reconstructing a plausible evolutionary scenario. We
suggest
 that a common ancestor of NC crows, originating from a (probably) non-tool-using South-East
Asian
 or Australasian crow population, colonised New Caledonia after its last emersion several million
years
 ago. The presence of proﬁtable  but out-of-reach food, in combination with a lack of direct competi-
tion
 for these resources, resulted in a vacant woodpecker-like niche. Crows may have possessed certain
behavioural
 and/or morphological features upon their arrival that predisposed them to express tool-use
rather
 than specialised prey-excavation behaviour, although it is possible that woodpecker-like forag-
ing
 preceded tool use. Low levels of predation risk may have further facilitated tool-use behaviour, by
allowing
 greater expenditure of time and energy on object interaction and exploration, as well as the evo-
lution
 of a ‘slow’ life-history, in which prolonged juvenile development enables acquisition of complex
behaviours.
 Intriguingly, humans may well have inﬂuenced  the evolution of at least some of the species’
tool-oriented
 behaviours, via their possible introduction of candlenut trees together with the beetle lar-
vae
 that infest them. Research on NC crows’ tool-use behaviour in its full ecological context is still in its
infancy,
 and we expect that, as more evidence accumulates, some of our assumptions and predictions
will
 be proved wrong. However, it is clear from our analysis of existing work, and the development of
some
 original ideas, that the unusual evolutionary trajectory of NC crows is probably the consequence of
an
 intricate constellation of interplaying factors.

1. Introduction
For a long time, tool use was widely believed to be a uniquely
human trait. This view changed overnight when Jane Goodall
reported that chimpanzees at the Gombe Stream, Tanzania, East
Africa, use a diversity of tools in foraging and non-foraging con-
texts (Goodall, 1964; for earlier reports of animal tool use, see
Darwin, 1879). Since this discovery, tool use has been observed
in an increasing number of animal species (reviews: Beck, 1980;
Bentley-Condit and Smith, 2010; Shumaker et al., 2011). Despite
this expanding catalogue, however, and the realisation that tool use
may not be as ‘special’ as originally thought (Hansell and Ruxton,
2008), habitual and more sophisticated forms of tool use are notice-
ably scarce, and largely conﬁned  to the great apes and a handful of
other species (Shumaker et al., 2011). This phylogenetic rarity raises
intriguing questions about the possible evolutionary origins of this
behaviour.
We endeavour to gain fresh insights into these issues by inves-
tigating a tool-using bird species from a remote tropical island in
the South Paciﬁc  –  the New Caledonian (NC) crow Corvus monedu-
loides (Fig. 1). Given the phylogenetic distance between crows and
great apes, there is little doubt that their tool-oriented behaviour
has evolved independently. This independence of origin provides
a valuable opportunity to investigate the biological circumstances
that foster the evolution and maintenance of tool use, as well as its
possible dependence on cultural processes. In this review, we focus
on the evolution and ecology of tool use in NC crows, leaving aside
the question of culture. Some ﬁfty  years after Tinbergen (1963)
outlined the aims and methods of ethology, his ‘four questions’
still provide a robust conceptual framework for approaching the
task of understanding ‘why’ an animal exhibits a certain behaviour.
Whilst we felt that there is currently insufﬁcient  material to dis-
cuss the four different levels of explanation –  phylogeny, ontogeny,
adaptive function, and mechanism –  in separate self-contained
sections, we kept them very much in mind when writing this
paper.
Over the last few decades, research on NC crows, both in the
laboratory and in the ﬁeld,  has provided answers to some questions
and indicated fruitful avenues for addressing others. Our paper is
not meant to be an exhaustive review of the literature on NC crows,
let alone of that on the evolution of animal tool use in general.
Rather, we have tried to bring together published and unpublished
material in a way that allows us to offer some useful speculation on
topics where ﬁrm  evidence is still lacking. We think our piece is best
read in the context of earlier reviews on the species’ general tool-
related behaviour (Hunt and Gray, 2006), and the possible cognitive
mechanisms underlying it (Kacelnik et al., 2006; Bluff et al., 2007).

2. Tool-use behaviour in the wild
2.1. Current distribution and habitats
NC crows are endemic to the remote Paciﬁc  archipelago of New
Caledonia, inhabiting the main island, Grande Terre, as well as one
of the sister islands, Maré (Fig. 2). Delacour (1966) asserts that NC
crows were introduced by humans to Maré (subsequently cited by
several other authors, including Doughty et al., 1999; Hunt and
Gray, 2003; del Hoyo et al., 2009), but since he failed to provide con-
vincing supporting evidence, we consider this claim contentious. A
smaller population on another sister island, Lifou, which was also
possibly due to introduction by humans (Barré and Dutson, 2000),
is apparently now extinct (unpubl. data; Dutson, 2011).

NC crows live across a wide spectrum of habitats, from coastal
dry-forest remnants (Hannecart and Létocart, 1980), farmland
(Vuilleumier and Gochfeld, 1976), and human settlements (pers.
observ.), to rainforests along the main island’s central moun-
tain chain (Hunt, 1996). Densities vary considerably, from only a
few families in some high-altitude, rainforest sites (C. Rutz, pers.
observ.; cf. Hunt, 1996, 2000a), to larger numbers in some other
habitats, for example where wood-boring beetle larvae abound
(see Sections 2.3 and 3.2) and/or where hunting by humans pro-
vides good access to carrion (e.g., Rutz et al., 2007, 2010; Bluff et al.,
2010b).
2.2.
 Tool types
Throughout their range, NC crows use a variety of dead and liv-
ing plant materials to obtain prey from out-of-reach places (Fig. 3).
Using their bills, they exhibit remarkable dexterity in tool manu-
facture and use, with skills being honed during a prolonged, 1–2
year development period (see Section 3.7.2, Bluff et al., 2010b;
Holzhaider et al., 2010a,b). NC crow tools have a diversity of forms
(Hunt and Gray, 2006), and there appears to be some variation in
individual-level tool repertoires (Hunt and Gray, 2007). The most
basic tools are stick-type tools (henceforth stick tools), usually a
twig, stem section, fern stolon, or leaf petiole (Orenstein, 1972;
Hunt, 1996, 2008; Hunt and Gray, 2002, 2006; Rutz et al., 2007;
Troscianko et al., 2008; Bluff et al., 2010b). Stick-tools may be sub-
classiﬁed  into non-hooked (Fig. 3A) and hooked types (Fig. 3B).
The non-hooked type comprises relatively simple, approximately
straight tools which require minimal (or no) modiﬁcation  of raw
materials (Hunt, 2000b; Hunt and Gray, 2002; Bluff et al., 2010b),
whereas hooked stick tools are a more complex variant, usually
produced by the selective removal of parts of a forked twig and
deliberate ‘sculpting’ of the functional end (Hunt, 1996; Hunt and
Gray, 2002, 2004a).
Distinct from stick tools, pandanus tools are produced from the
barbed leaf edges of screw pines (Pandanus spp.) (Figs. 3C and 4),
using a combination of precise cutting and ripping actions. Like
stick tools, pandanus tools (Fig. 3C) may be further sub-classiﬁed

according to their shapes, which range from simple rectangular
strips (‘wide’ or ‘narrow’ tools, according to dimensions) to more
complex variants (‘stepped’ tools) which taper towards the working
tip (Hunt, 1996; Hunt and Gray, 2003). The naturally barbed edge of
pandanus tools means that they can function as hooked tools, and
crows indeed tend to manufacture and use them with the barbs
in the functional orientation (pointing away from the substrate;
Hunt, 1996; Holzhaider et al., 2008). Systematic surveys revealed
some variation in pandanus tool shapes across Grande Terre (Hunt,
2000a; Hunt and Gray, 2003): in most surveyed areas, crows manu-
facture stepped tools, but in a handful of localities, they also fashion
narrow (and wide) tools. Hunt and Gray (2003, 2006) suggested that
the observed geographic distribution of pandanus tool shapes is the
outcome of a process of diversiﬁcation  and cumulative evolution,
where across crow generations, a simple tool design (wide pan-
danus tools) was progressively reﬁned  to produce more complex
variants (stepped pandanus tools). This is an intriguing possibil-
ity, as cumulative technological evolution has so far been thought
to be a uniquely human trait (Boyd and Richerson, 1996, 2005;
Richerson and Boyd, 2006; Caldwell and Millen, 2008). We believe
that current evidence is insufﬁcient  to justify these claims (Bluff
et al., 2010a; see also Shumaker et al., 2011), but a detailed discus-
sion of these issues is unfortunately beyond the scope of this review
(for general reviews of the ‘animal-cultures debate’, see Laland and
Hoppitt, 2003; Laland and Janik, 2006; Laland and Galef, 2009).
Wide tools are the only pandanus tool design made by crows on
Maré, although there is apparently some variation in manufacture
(Holzhaider et al., 2010a,b) and deployment techniques (Hunt et al.,
2007a). It is worth noting that Pandanus spp. trees occur across a
range of habitats on Grande Terre, but according to unpublished
surveys, they are not always used by local crows for tool manufac-
ture (pers. observ.).
In captivity, NC crows opportunistically use unfamiliar materi-
als for making foraging tools, including garden wire (Weir et al.,
2002), aluminium strips (Weir and Kacelnik, 2006), and cardboard
(Kacelnik et al., 2006; Bluff et al., 2007), so it is reasonable to
assume that wild crows use a broader range of raw materials than
has so far been documented. At least one wild crow at our long-
term dry-forest study site on the central west coast of Grande
Terre (‘Gouaro-Déva’) has been observed using a moulted feather
for probing behaviour (unpubl. data). Wild crows are difﬁcult  to
observe, because much of their cursorial foraging activity takes
place out of sight high up in the forest canopy, and they are also
easily disturbed by humans –  either moving away to avoid them
or approaching them out of curiosity. Because of these constraints,
crow-borne video cameras have proved to be an invaluable study
tool (Rutz et al., 2007). Using this method, a hitherto-unknown tool
material (dry grass stems) was discovered within only 7.5 h of video
recording (Rutz et al., 2007), a ﬁnding  that was later conﬁrmed
through direct observation (Troscianko et al., 2008). Systematic
deployment of these ‘crowcams’ (Bluff and Rutz, 2008; Rutz and
Bluff, 2008) has the potential: (i) to document unknown tool mate-
rials and novel tool types; (ii) to ascertain the circumstances in
which different materials, tools and behaviours are used in the wild
(see also Section 2.3); and (iii) to allow estimation of the impor-
tance of tool-use behaviours through quantitative construction of
time and energy budgets (see also Section 3.2).
Like many other corvids, NC crows drop nuts (and snails) on
hard surfaces to crack them open (Layard and Layard, 1882; Hunt
et al., 2002), but this behaviour does not satisfy the widely accepted
deﬁnition  for ‘tool use’ by Beck (1980; for further reﬁnement
and critical discussion, see Shumaker et al., 2011; St Amant and
Horton, 2008). We mention this here because the nuts exploited
by NC crows are those of the candlenut tree Aleurites moluccana,
which also harbours the large and nutritious beetle larvae that are
extracted with tools (see Fig. 1 and Section 2.3). Since these trees

2.3. Tool function
Surprisingly little is known about the foraging contexts in which
NC crows deploy their tools, and about the prey types they tar-
get with them. Like other (tropical) corvids (cf. Goodwin, 1986; del
Hoyo et al., 2009), NC crows are opportunistic, omnivorous foragers
that are known, or suspected, to take a wide range of foods, includ-
ing larval and adult insects, snails, lizards, carrion, fruit, nuts, bird
eggs and other small animal prey (review: Rutz et al., 2010).
The best understood type of tool-assisted foraging is probably
‘larva ﬁshing’  (Figs. 1 and 5), which Le Goupils (1928) described in
what appears to be the ﬁrst  published record of NC crow tool use
(see introductory quote). Here, crows use stick tools (Fig. 3A), usu-
ally twigs or leaf petioles, to extract larvae (Figs. 1 and 5E and F)
of the longhorn beetle Agrianome fairmairei (Fig. 5B) from their
burrows in candlenut trees (Cochereau, 1970; Fig. 5A, C, D and
G). Similar to the well-known ‘termite ﬁshing’  behaviour of chim-
panzees (Goodall, 1964), crows persistently poke larvae with a tool
(see movie S1 in Rutz et al., 2010), ‘teasing’ them until defensive
behaviour is elicited. Sufﬁciently  agitated larvae will bite the tip of
the tool with their powerful mandibles, and once securely attached,
can be extracted from their burrow by carefully withdrawing the
tool (Fig. 5G). The technique requires remarkable levels of sensori-
motor control, and when we tried to emulate it in informal sessions
during ﬁeldwork,  it turned out to be surprisingly difﬁcult  to master
even for humans. In the wild, immature crows seem to hone their

Apart from larva-ﬁshing,  stick tools are used in a wide range
of foraging contexts, for extracting animal prey from holes and
crevices, but the composition of stick-tool derived diets has not
yet been quantiﬁed  (see Section 3.2). Sometimes tool use takes
place on the forest ﬂoor,  where leaf litter is screened for prey (Rutz
et al., 2007), but more commonly crows will deploy these tools
on fallen trees or in standing vegetation, to probe behind bark or
leaves, as well as in cavities (pers. observ.). Hunt (1996) observed
crows using hooked twig tools in holes in living and dead wood (cf.
Orenstein, 1972) and at the base of palm and Freycinetia longispica
leaves (see Fig. 1A–C  in Hunt, 1996), but was unable to identify the
prey they captured (although he found a range of invertebrates
when examining similar sites). In a farmland area, a crow was
photographed extracting a lizard with a non-hooked tool from
a fence post (Troscianko et al., 2008), illustrating the plasticity
of stick tool use behaviour. Hooked and non-hooked stick tools
were also used by crows at experimental feeding tables, to extract
meat from holes drilled into logs or from Perspex boxes (e.g.,
Hunt and Gray, 2004a, 2007; Hunt et al., 2006; Holzhaider et al.,
2008);
 whilst conﬁrming  the foraging function of these tools, such
observations do not enable inferences about natural diet choice.
The function of pandanus tools remains largely a mystery, as
only very few observations exist of wild crows deploying stepped
tools in natural foraging locations (Hunt, 1996). The vast major-
ity of observed pandanus-tool deployments are by birds extracting
meat at experimentally baited sites (one bird using stepped tools on
Grande Terre, Hunt and Gray, 2004b; many birds using wide tools
on Maré, e.g., Holzhaider et al., 2010a,b). From the few observa-
tions of natural foraging, and from the locations of discarded tools,
it seems likely that pandanus tools are routinely used in the crowns
of Pandanus spp. trees, although it is not yet clear what their main
target prey are. We will return to the question of NC crow tools’
energetic signiﬁcance  in Section 3.2.
3. Evolution and ecology of tool use
3.1. Nature or nurture?
Controlled experiments with captive-bred birds demonstrated
conclusively that NC crows have a strong genetic predisposition for

PDF PAGE 6
158 C. Rutz, J.J.H. St Clair / Behavioural Processes 89 (2012) 153– 165
basic stick tool use, and even the manufacture of simple pandanus
tools (Kenward et al., 2005, 2006). Kenward et al. (2005, 2006) doc-
umented the development of four hand-raised NC crows: two birds
received regular demonstrations by humans of how to use stick
tools for extracting food from artiﬁcial  holes and crevices (treat-
ment group), and the other two were raised (in separate aviaries)
with the same level of overall attention, but without ever seeing
tools, or tool-like objects, being handled by their human foster par-
ents or by conspeciﬁcs  (control group). Interestingly, all four birds
developed functional stick-tool use. Successful food retrieval from
a crevice was ﬁrst  observed in the tutored birds when they were
68 and 72 days old, and in the untutored birds at 63 and 79 days
old. One of the untutored birds later even produced basic pan-
danus tools after being supplied with suitable raw materials. An
independent study reported the development of stick-tool use in
a juvenile NC crow, and pandanus tool use, but not manufacture,
in two out of four (presumably) pandanus-naïve subjects; how-
ever, experiments were less controlled as birds were housed in a
zoological garden where humans regularly interacted with them,
handling tools in full view and even encouraging their use (Hunt
et al., 2007b).
Despite strong genetic predispositions (for similar results for
woodpecker ﬁnches,  see Tebbich et al., 2001), tutoring has mea-
surable effects on juveniles’ tool-use development, by elevating
levels of twig handling and insertion (in the sample of the four
birds studied by Kenward et al., 2006), and demonstrations of han-
dling increases their exploration of non-tool objects (Kenward et al.,
2006). It also remains possible that the production or deployment of
more complex tool types, such as stepped pandanus tools, requires
social input (e.g., Kenward et al., 2005; Holzhaider et al., 2010b).
Young crows may learn directly by observing proﬁcient  adults, or
indirectly by examining tool artefacts that have been left behind
(tools, or pandanus counterparts; see Figs. 1B and 5C), and recent
ﬁeld  studies conﬁrm  that both processes could be at work in the
wild (Bluff et al., 2010b; Holzhaider et al., 2010a,b). These issues
relate to the question of crow ‘cultures’, and are therefore not
explored any further here. Nevertheless, it is clear from the above
that tool use –  at least in its simplest forms –  is an evolved adapta-
tion in NC crows. This means that we can examine the behaviour’s
ecological, and adaptive, signiﬁcance  under contemporary ecolog-
ical conditions (Section 3.2), as well as its potential correlates and
drivers (Sections 3.3, 3.5–3.7 ).
3.2. Ecological signiﬁcance  of tool use
Knowledge of the factors that currently maintain tool use in NC
crows can inform speculation about the factors that have selected
for it in the evolutionary past. To this end, we may ask how impor-
tant, compared to other foraging modes, tool-assisted foraging is in
the everyday lives of NC crows, in terms of both overall energetic
intake and the provision of speciﬁc  dietary components. Findings
obviously require cautious interpretation, as ecological conditions
may change over time, but several studies have successfully used
this approach for a range of primate species (Nishida and Hiraiwa,
1982; Yamakoshi, 1998; Boesch and Boesch-Achermann, 2000),
and woodpecker ﬁnches  (Tebbich et al., 2002), and it is informative
to review the evidence that is accumulating for our study system.
As mentioned earlier (Section 2.3), very little is known about
NC crows’ general foraging behaviour. The observation that they
use tools to ﬁsh  for large, wood-boring beetle larvae (Le Goupils,
1928; Hunt, 2000b; Bluff et al., 2010b; Rutz et al., 2010) was an
early indication that tool use in this species may provide access to
particularly proﬁtable  prey. However, the reliance of these birds
on tool-assisted foraging remained unknown, largely because of
the difﬁculty  of observing wild crows in their natural habitats (see
Section 2.2; Rutz et al., 2007; Bluff et al., 2010b). A recent study
used
 state-of-the-art stable-isotope proﬁling  techniques to esti-
mate tool-assisted dietary intake at the level of the individual crow
(Rutz et al., 2010). Comparing the stable-isotope proﬁles  of crow tis-
sues (feathers and blood) with those of seven putative food sources
(wood-boring beetle larvae; other invertebrates; candlenuts; car-
rion; lizards; snails; fruit), enabled a ﬁrst  quantitative assessment of
crow diets. Crucially, a proxy measure of tool-use dependence could
be established, by exploiting the fact that tool-derived cerambycid
larvae (see Section 2.3) are exclusively xylophagous, which pro-
duces a distinct, and traceable, isotopic signature (low /H925415N). The
analyses revealed that beetle larvae can be an exceptionally impor-
tant source of protein and lipids for NC crows, and that they can
satisfy a large proportion of an adult’s daily energy requirements,
highlighting the substantial rewards available to competent tool
users (Rutz et al., 2010; note that a correction has been published
for this paper).
Intriguingly, when sampled crows were allocated to age cate-
gories (inferred from their gape colouration, which changes with
age), this study also found age-dependent variation in the esti-
mated contribution of larvae to dietary intake, with nestlings and
recently ﬂedged  crows consuming more larvae than both imma-
tures (ca. 1–2  years old) and adults. This suggests that parents may
preferentially feed their nutritionally dependent offspring with
tool-derived, highly nutritious foods. As noted by the authors of the
study, this pattern was only found when analysing isotopic ratios
in crows’ feather samples, but not with blood samples, so further
work is required to corroborate these results (Rutz et al., 2010).
It is important to remember that larva-ﬁshing  is only one facet
of NC crows’ tool-use repertoire (see Sections 2.2 and 2.3), and that
not all crow populations have access to the candlenut trees that
usually harbour the larva prey that is exploited by this foraging
technique. Even in those areas where candlenut trees grow, oppor-
tunities for larva ﬁshing  vary spatially, and temporally, as beetle
infested trees need to fall over to make larva burrows accessible
to crows’ tool use (Bluff et al., 2010b; Rutz et al., 2010; sometimes
humans aid in this process –  see Section 3.6). Nevertheless, judging
from the evidence available to date, crow tool use seems to provide
access to an energetically rewarding food supply. More work is nec-
essary, especially: (i) to understand the functional signiﬁcance  of
other forms of tool use (see Sections 2.2 and 2.3); and (ii) to demon-
strate conclusively the ﬁtness  beneﬁts  of tool use (for ‘sponging’ in
dolphins, see Mann et al., 2008; Patterson and Mann, 2011).
3.3. The limitations of comparative techniques
Any attempt to trace the possible evolutionary origins, and eco-
logical drivers, of tool use would beneﬁt  from comparative analyses
that control statistically for phylogenetic relatedness (Felsenstein,
1985; Harvey and Pagel, 1991). Whilst a recent update of Beck’s
(1980) seminal review on animal tool use has revealed consid-
erably wider taxonomic spread of avian tool-related behaviour
than previously recognised (see Table 3.1 in Shumaker et al., 2011;
see also Bentley-Condit and Smith, 2010), for the vast majority
of species, tool use has only been observed a few times, often in
captivity, and frequently in a single subject. Unfortunately, when
studying the emergence of habitual or customary tool-assisted
foraging in wild bird populations, one is left with a very limited
sample of species, including woodpecker ﬁnches  Cactospiza pallida,
NC crows, Egyptian vultures Neophron percnopterus, and perhaps
less well known, brown-headed nuthatches Sitta pusilla (hyacinth
macaws Anodorhynchus hyacinthinus, and some other species, are
less well studied, but may also belong to this group; for an exhaus-
tive review, and original sources, see Shumaker et al., 2011). The
fact that habitual tool use emerged in at least four different families
is in itself revealing (see Section 4), but this limited sample is insuf-
ﬁcient  for quantitative comparative analyses. This is admittedly a

PDF PAGE 7
C. Rutz, J.J.H. St Clair / Behavioural Processes 89 (2012) 153– 165 159
major constraint, but we feel that there is enough circumstantial
evidence to offer some informed speculation for our study species.
In primates, a palaeontological approach has been proﬁtably
used to inform our understanding of the evolution of tool mor-
phology and use (e.g., Mercader et al., 2002; Boyd and Silk, 2006).
However, unlike hominids and chimpanzees, wild NC crows pro-
duce all of their (known) tools from plant material (see Section 2.2).
These tools degrade within days or weeks after manufacture, and
even the ‘counterparts’ left behind in pandanus leaves where tools
have been excised (see Fig. 4, inset) only last for 1–3  years until
the leaves reach the end of their life, and are shed (Hunt and Gray,
2003). This means that, unfortunately, there is no archaeological
record of NC crow tools that would allow us to study the succes-
sion of different tool types, or changes in tool morphology, over
evolutionary time periods. As a consequence, even the simplest of
questions cannot be conclusively answered using this method, for
example, whether the ﬁrst  tool-using NC crows used stick tools
or pandanus tools. In this case, we suggest that the simpler stick
tools, which in their most basic form require no manufacturing or
modiﬁcation  (see Section 2.2), are the ancestral type.
3.4. Phylogenetic relationships
An understanding of the evolutionary origins of NC crows would
allow inferences about the time scales involved in the evolution
of their tool-related adaptations. A ﬁnely  resolved molecular phy-
logeny of the genus Corvus would be necessary to identify the extant
species most closely related to the NC crow, and to estimate the
time of divergence, but to our knowledge, no such phylogeny has
been published yet (but see the abstract by Gamauf et al., 2010).
However, by using geological and other circumstantial evidence,
we can attempt to derive a tentative colonisation scenario and
time scale, for comparison with preliminary results from ongoing
genetic analyses (R.C. Fleischer, pers. comm.).
We can be conﬁdent  that the ancestral population of NC crows
was isolated from other crows no earlier than the mid-Eocene (from
ca. 53 million years ago) because the radiation of the Corvidae fam-
ily has been dated to this time (Barker et al., 2004). We can further
infer that present-day NC crows descended from colonists that dis-
persed to the New Caledonian landmass by ﬂight  (but see below),
rather than having evolved in situ following the fragmentation of
Zealandia, the southern landmass from which New Zealand and
New Caledonia derived. This is because there is now some evi-
dence that present-day New Caledonia was submerged for ca. 20
million years, before its ﬁnal  emersion ca. 37 million years ago
(Grandcolas et al., 2008). It is of course possible that small island
refugia remained (or existed nearby) during submersion events,
and that these were inhabited by crows; such a scenario may
explain the presence of the Kagu Rhynochetos jubatus, a ﬂightless
New Caledonian endemic whose closest known relative is the South
American sun-bittern Eurypyga helias (Fain and Houde, 2004).
In reality, the isolation of the ancestral NC crows is likely
to have occurred considerably more recently than 37 million
years ago. This is because, if the NC crow lineage were indeed as
ancient as this, we would expect to see a level of morphological
divergence that would warrant allocation to a monotypic genus
or subfamily (for a review of avian divergence times, see Newton,
2003). Although some workers have considered the NC crow to
be relatively atypical within the genus Corvus (e.g., Dorst, 1947),
it is not distinct enough to suggest an independent origin, and we
should accordingly expect to ﬁnd  close relatives of the NC crow
amongst the crow species of South-East Asia and Australasia (Jollie,
1978). This close afﬁnity  implies a divergence time, and thus a
possible window for the evolution of tool-related adaptations, in
the order of millions of years rather than tens of millions. Our
proposed route and timing of colonisation agree with results
from
 unpublished genetic analyses that identify C. enca (Brunei,
Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines), C. validus (Moluccas), and C.
woodfordi/meeki (Bougainville Island, Solomon Islands; see Fig. 2)
as NC crows’ closest living relatives (R.C. Fleischer, pers. comm.);
we are eagerly awaiting deﬁnitive  results from this project, and
similar work by other groups. Recently excavated NC crow bones
are only a few thousand years old (Boyer et al., 2010) and therefore
too young to be useful for reﬁning  estimates of colonisation dates.
Our account, as well as ﬁrst  genetic results, suggest that ances-
tral crows ﬂew  large distances across open water to reach New
Caledonia. Modern NC crows are poor ﬂiers,  which typically make
short inter-perch ﬂights  when traversing their forested habitats,
and which employ a noticeably laboured ﬂight  to travel longer
distances (for wing morphology data, see Kenward et al., 2004).
The absence of NC crows from most of New Caledonia’s outlying
Loyalty islands – which  are situated around 100 km off the east
coast of Grande Terre, and which appear to offer suitable crow
habitat –i s  consistent with their weak ﬂying  ability. NC crows do
occur on the largest island of the Loyalty group, Maré, although
their presence there may possibly be due to recent introduction
by humans (see Section 2.1). Whilst their restricted distribution
in the archipelago suggests that the dispersal ability of modern NC
crows is limited, the extant population could easily have descended
from more strongly ﬂying,  or simply lucky, colonists (for a review
of overseas avian colonisations, see Newton, 2003).
A ﬁnal,  but important, caveat remains: we cannot yet ﬁrmly
reject the possibility that proto-NC crows were tool users prior to
their arrival in New Caledonia. Having established a likely South-
East Asian/Australasian origin of NC crows, it would be important to
examine whether some of the extant Corvus species in these regions
exhibit tool-use behaviour. Not surprisingly, given the extremely
remote tropical forest habitats of several of these species, very little
is currently known about the behaviour and foraging ecology of the
most promising candidates (see above; for species accounts, see del
Hoyo et al., 2009; Dutson, 2011). Nevertheless, in the remainder of
this review, we shall assume that NC crows’ tool use evolved in situ
(i.e., post-colonisation), as this seems the most likely scenario.
3.5. Key hypotheses for possible ecological drivers
Although a rigorous comparative analysis is so far lacking, a
number of hypothetical ecological drivers for the evolution of
extractive tool use have been proposed. These include an abun-
dance of concealed food resulting from an absence of competitors
adapted for extractive foraging (Orenstein, 1972; Kenward et al.,
2004), low or strongly seasonal availability of non-concealed food
sources (Tebbich et al., 2002), and a reduced risk of predation (see
Kacelnik et al., 2006).
In combination, the ﬁrst  two of these ecological variables, which
affect availabilities of concealed and non-concealed food sources,
must inﬂuence  the proﬁtability  of tool-use behaviour relative to
‘conventional’ foraging techniques, and thus affect the sign and
strength of selection for extractive foraging behaviours such as
tool use. The last-mentioned variable, a reduced risk of predation,
is thought to facilitate the evolution of tool-use behaviour in two
ways. First, relaxed selection for anti-predator traits such as vigi-
lance and neophobia may reduce cognitive load and allow greater
expenditure of time and energy on object interaction and explo-
ration. Second, a reduced level of extrinsic mortality may select for
the evolution of life-histories characterised by slow juvenile devel-
opment, delayed reproductive maturity, prolonged parental care,
and increased lifespan, with which cognitive adaptations are often
associated (Sæther,  1988; Stearns, 2002; Gonzalez-Lagos et al.,
2010).
We
 can now examine the role of these putative ecological
drivers in the evolutionary history of NC crows. New Caledonia

certainly lacks extractive specialists (tool-using or otherwise) such
as woodpeckers, tool-using monkeys or apes, aye–ayes  Dauben-
tonia madagascariensis or striped possums Dactylopsila trivirgata,
and it is likely that this has resulted in a greater abundance of con-
cealed invertebrate prey than in areas where extractive foragers are
present. It is also true that mammal carrion, which is the dietary
mainstay of many other crow species, was absent from New Caledo-
nia until after human colonisation (see Section 3.6), and anecdotal
evidence suggests that non-concealed invertebrate biomass may
be unusually low, at least in some habitats (Rutz et al., 2007, 2010).
Prey scarcity may possibly be more severe during the dry season
(cf. Tebbich et al., 2002), although this remains to be investigated.
We wish to clarify that general food limitation (i.e., a general lack of
suitable food in the environment), which is sometimes put forward
in the literature as a possible selective force, is an unsatisfactory
explanation for the evolution of tool use. In the absence of other lim-
iting extrinsic factors – like  suitable nest sites, predation, or disease
– all capacity-level bird populations are expected to be ultimately
limited by food supplies (review: Newton, 1998), yet in most cases
this does not select for extractive foraging behaviours. What mat-
ters is not the absolute amount of food in the environment, but the
relative proﬁtabilities  of concealed and unconcealed food. Recent
studies using animal-borne video cameras (Rutz et al., 2007) and
stable-isotope proﬁling  techniques (Rutz et al., 2010) suggest that
tool use does indeed provide NC crows with access to energeti-
cally proﬁtable  prey that is not exploited by other bird species.
Energetics aside, tool use may facilitate intake of certain dietary
components that are difﬁcult  to obtain through conventional for-
aging techniques, but this idea has so far remained unexplored.
Due to a species-poor predator community in New Caledonia,
it seems probable that NC crows have experienced a lower risk
of predation during their evolutionary history than have conti-
nental corvids. On Maré, Holzhaider et al. (2011) observed crow
chicks being attacked by goshawks, and inferred goshawk preda-
tion as the likely cause of death for at least one adult breeder.
The species was not identiﬁed  by the authors, but must have been
the brown goshawk Accipiter fasciatus, which is comparatively rare
across Grande Terre (Desmoulins and Barré, 2005). The smaller,
endemic white-bellied goshawk Accipiter haplochrous is more com-
mon (Desmoulins and Barré, 2005; Chartendrault et al., 2007), but
certainly no threat to crows (C. Rutz, pers. observ.). Past and present
owl species can also be ruled out as crow predators (see Boyer
et al., 2010). Native predatory mammals and terrestrial snakes
(with the exception of the burrowing blind snake Ramphotyphlops
sp.) are absent from New Caledonia, although dogs, cats and rats
were introduced by humans during colonisation (Vuilleumier and
Gochfeld, 1976; Gargominy et al., 1996; Spaggiari et al., 2007). Con-
sistent with the idea of low predation pressure, NC crows show
comparatively little vigilance behaviour, such as repeated scanning
of the sky when foraging on or near the ground. This is particularly
obvious in birds that manufacture or deploy tools, as they often
appear extremely focused on the task, paying little attention to their
surroundings. NC crows repeatedly give wak-wak ‘alarm’ calls (see
Bluff et al., 2010a), but most variants of these calls seem to have a
social rather than an anti-predator function.
It may be worth acknowledging that none of the ecological
drivers proposed above is likely to act in isolation, and that they may
select for very different adaptive solutions depending on the pre-
existing phenotypic envelope of the population under selection. We
revisit the question of possible predispositions in Section 3.7.
3.6. Tool materials, prey sources and the role of anthropogenic
change
Having established that the adaptive landscape currently
favours tool-use behaviours in NC crows, we may perhaps improve
our
 understanding of the evolution of this trait by asking how long
this situation has prevailed, assuming that it evolved in situ (see
Section 3.4). For example, the (hypothetical) discovery of (sub-)
fossil woodpeckers on New Caledonia might prompt us to argue
that the adaptive value of extractive foraging for NC crows was
higher after the woodpecker’s extinction than beforehand. We may
even speculate that this was a key event in the evolution of tool use
in NC crows. Given the potential insights this approach may offer,
we will here summarise what can be deduced about the foraging
ecology of NC crows in the distant past.
Some of the species’ tool-use behaviours are indeed amenable to
this kind of ‘forensic’ assessment. For example, the manufacture of
pandanus tools is by deﬁnition  reliant on the presence of pandanus
trees, and it is thus sensible to ask for how long this raw material
has been present on New Caledonia. In fact, nineteen of the twenty-
one species of Pandanus known from New Caledonia are endemic
to the islands (Jaffré et al., 1987; T. Jaffré, pers. comm.), indicating
that NC crows have had access to pandanus trees for most, if not
all, of their evolutionary history.
We may ask the same question of the candlenut tree, which is
now central to the foraging ecology of some NC crow populations
(see Sections 2.3 and 3.2). It is generally thought that candlenut
trees were introduced to New Caledonia and other Paciﬁc  islands by
early colonists (Smith, 1981; Mackee, 1985; Gargominy et al., 1996;
Walter and Sam, 2002; Elevitch and Manner, 2006). The earliest
archaeological evidence for human settlement on New Caledo-
nia consists of artefacts of the Lapita culture dating to ca. 3200
years before present, and although pre-Lapitan settlement cannot
be ruled out, it is considered unlikely (Spriggs, 1995). If candlenut
trees were indeed introduced by humans, it is therefore probable
that the introduction occurred within the last 4000 years. Unfor-
tunately, there is currently no evidence in support of the widely
expressed belief that candlenut trees have been introduced to New
Caledonia; to our knowledge, no stratigraphic analysis of the occur-
rence of candlenut wood, seed fragments or pollen is available,
and to date no molecular phylogenetic analysis of Paciﬁc  can-
dlenut populations, or of the beetle species that infests them, has
been carried out. In our opinion, it also cannot be ruled out that
candlenut trees have colonised New Caledonia naturally, through
long-distance dispersal of their hardy nuts via marine currents.
Two points regarding the crows’ beetle prey complicate assessment
even further. First, present-day Agrianome larvae are polyphagous
(Montrouzier, 1861; Hunt, 2000b; Bluff, 2008; pers. observ.), so the
species may have only secondarily switched to candlenut trees after
these trees were introduced to New Caledonia, having been pre-
viously available in other host trees (Bluff, 2008). Second, we do
not currently know whether the larvae of native xylophagous bee-
tle species can be extracted by crows using the same larva-ﬁshing
methods they use to obtain Agrianome larvae (see Section 2.3).
These knowledge gaps ensure that, for the time being, we cannot
assess the intriguing possibility that this sophisticated behaviour
arose during the last few millenia.
Whether candlenut trees were introduced by humans or not, the
case brings some interesting possibilities into focus. The human
settlement of New Caledonia was associated with profound eco-
logical changes, including effects on community composition and
on the structure and distribution of habitats (Stevenson, 1999,
2004; Stevenson and Hope, 2005; Boyer et al., 2010). These changes
include the introduction of novel food sources such as livestock
and game carrion, refuse and (possibly) candlenut trees, which
may have substantially altered –  or may still be in the process
of altering – the  costs and beneﬁts  to NC crows of different for-
aging behaviours, including tool use. Similarly, the widespread
degradation and fragmentation of the New Caledonian rainforest is
likely to have locally altered the opportunities for certain tool-use
behaviours (pandanus trees occur largely in rainforests) and also

C. Rutz, J.J.H. St Clair / Behavioural Processes 89 (2012) 153– 165 161
to have disrupted the ﬂow  of tool-related information, whether
genetic or cultural, within and between NC crow populations.
Accordingly, an understanding of past and ongoing anthropogenic
change may help us to explain current patterns in the expression
of tool-use behaviour by NC crows.
In a memorable conversation, one of the ﬁrst  author’s New
Caledonian friends (of indigenous Kanak ancestry) once made
a thought-provoking point when he asked whether crows had
learned how to use tools from humans, or vice versa. The idea
that humans may have facilitated (the evolution of) tool use in
NC crows in such a direct way is intriguing. As observed in other
hunter-gather societies (Bodenheimer, 1951), Kanak tribes in New
Caledonia traditionally harvest cerambycid larvae for food, felling
infested trees and breaking open logs. This gathering activity could
have (had) two possible effects on crows. First, in the past, Kanak
apparently used stick tools for ﬁshing  larvae from their burrows,
potentially providing a direct behavioural template for crows. Sec-
ond, the destruction of trees exposes larva-rich logs to crows and
makes them more accessible to subsequent exploitation. When
sufﬁciently  weakened, larva-infested candlenut trees can fall over
naturally, but human interference had the potential to provide
ancestral crows with greater exposure to this food source, by bring-
ing down trees before the majority of larvae had eclosed. We think
it is unlikely that tool use in NC crows evolved in response to the
possible (recent) introduction of candlenut trees, and subsequent
human facilitation, but their speciﬁc  larva-ﬁshing  techniques may
well have resulted from these factors. The question of why natural
selection favoured tool-use behaviour, rather than morphological
adaptations, is further discussed in the following section on possi-
ble predispositions (Section 3.7).
3.7. Evolutionary predispositions, and present-day adaptations
Anatomical and behavioural predispositions can facilitate the
expression of certain behaviours. Whilst we can offer some spec-
ulation for our study system, we must note at the outset of
this section that, on current evidence, it is impossible to estab-
lish ﬁrmly  for most of the traits discussed below whether they
are predispositions for, or adaptations to, tool-related behaviour.
Future work, especially more detailed interspeciﬁc  comparisons,
will no doubt provide a clearer indication of causality in some
cases.
3.7.1. Morphology
Many birds under selection for extractive foraging ability may
respond with physiological adaptations, such as long, thin or
chisel-like bills (e.g., Yamagishi and Eguchi, 1996), skull and mus-
cle architectures adapted for ‘jackhammer’-like drilling, and long,
barbed tongues (e.g. Bock, 1999), whilst mammals experiencing
a similar selective environment may respond with digital elon-
gation and diagnostic changes to skull morphology (e.g., Rawlins
and Handasyde, 2002; Beck, 2009). The evolutionary response to
a particular opportunity depends on the raw material on which
natural selection can operate, and crows are well-known for
their rather conserved generalist physiology (Goodwin, 1986; del
Hoyo et al., 2009) and their remarkable behavioural ﬂexibility
(Emery and Clayton, 2004; del Hoyo et al., 2009; Seed et al.,
2009).
First-time observers of NC crows often comment on the unusu-
ally straight bill of the species (Fig. 6A) –  unusual, that is, for the
genus Corvus whose members are noted for their hooked, curved
bills (cf. Delacour, 1966; Jollie, 1978; Goodwin, 1986; Doughty et al.,
1999; Kenward et al., 2004). NC crows hold their tools in the bill,
often with the non-functional (proximal) end pressed against one
cheek for lateral support (Rutledge and Hunt, 2004; Weir et al.,
2004). It is easy to see how a straight bill would aid the handling

turn may have enabled the handling of sticks for extractive for-

stick-type tools effectively (Bird and Emery, 2009) demonstrates

engaged in tool use, before selection for straighter bills kicked in.

identiﬁes  some candidates that, anatomically at least, may possess
the capacity to manipulate tool-like objects with some dexterity.
In fact, Goodwin (1986) observed that the Hawaiian crow Corvus
hawaiiensis has a bill shape similar to that of NC crows, comment-
ing that this may well be ‘adaptive’ and perhaps reﬂect  common
ancestry. This species is now extinct in the wild, and despite exten-
sive research efforts as part of a recent reintroduction scheme, its
natural feeding habits are not well documented (review: del Hoyo
et al., 2009).

Ancestral NC crows may have also possessed neurological fea-
tures that predisposed them to express tool-use behaviour. This
could include structures that support innovation or social-learning
abilities, or those that are necessary to enable relevant manipu-
lations of external objects (tools and hidden food items) and the
mapping of object interactions. The question of potentially ele-
vated levels of behavioural plasticity and/or cognitive capacities
is discussed in a dedicated section below (Section 3.7.2), but we
will examine here whether there is anything special about NC crow
brains. A recent study investigated the brains of ﬁve  NC crows and
compared them to those of selected ‘control’ species, claiming that
NC crows: (i) have one of the largest avian brains for their body size
(Cnotka et al., 2008); and (ii) exhibit relative enlargement of four
areas in the forebrain that are generally believed to be involved in
associative learning and motor control (mesopallium, pallidostri-
atal complex, septum, and tegmentum) (Mehlhorn et al., 2010). We
regard these results as preliminary, as both sets of analyses suffer
from methodological shortcomings. For example, an inspection of
the data for gross brain size makes it seem likely that the results
are driven by an outlier (see Fig. 3 in Cnotka et al., 2008) and, if only
for this reason, should be replicated with an independent sample
of NC crow brains. Amongst other things, we are also concerned
about the choice of control species and the pooling of data across
species in both studies. For the time being, we conclude that, whilst
some specialisations may be expected on the basis of comparative
analyses (e.g., Overington et al., 2009), the published evidence for
tool-related neurological adaptations in NC crows is weak at best.
3.7.2. Behaviour
Comparative studies have shown that members of the Corvi-
dae express more foraging innovations than other bird species
(Lefebvre et al., 2002), indicating considerable behavioural plastic-
ity (e.g., Emery and Clayton, 2004). It is obvious that an inclination
to explore novel food sources, and an ability to invent novel forag-
ing techniques in order to exploit them, could have facilitated the
evolution of tool use in NC crows.
Given its phylogenetic rarity, and the fact that humans are so
good at it, tool use has sometimes been seen as being depen-
dent on ‘advanced’ cognitive abilities. Whilst such anthropocentric
arguments are clearly dated, it is valid to examine whether the
manipulation and deployment of tools draws on domain-general
cognitive capacities, which are present in other species (at least
other corvids), or involves speciﬁc  mechanisms (for discussion,
see Emery and Clayton, 2009). If the latter is the case, these
cognitive operations may in turn be dependent on a particular
neurological architecture (see Section 3.7.1). There is no doubt
that NC crows excel at tool-related tasks (review: Bluff et al.,
2007). When facing an extraction problem, they choose tools of
appropriate properties (e.g., Chappell and Kacelnik, 2002, 2004;
Hunt et al., 2006; Bluff et al., 2010b), combine different tools
sequentially (Taylor et al., 2007; Wimpenny et al., 2009), and if nec-
essary, even spontaneously invent new tool designs (Weir et al.,
2002; Weir and Kacelnik, 2006; von Bayern et al., 2009). How-
ever, little is known about the exact cognitive mechanisms that
underlie these birds’ tool use, although it becomes increasingly
clear that seemingly sophisticated behaviours can be produced by
relatively simple processes (Wimpenny et al., 2009; von Bayern
et al., 2009; for a different view, see for example Taylor et al.,
2007). The recent approach to ‘deconstruct’ observed behaviours,
by manipulating subjects’ pre-testing experience and by ruling
out, one-by-one, known cognitive processes, seems productive
(Shettleworth, 2009). Related to this point, very few studies to
date have examined NC crows’ performance on non-tool-related
tasks or conducted controlled multi-species comparisons (but see
Auersperg et al., 2011), although the importance of such work has
long been acknowledged (e.g., Weir et al., 2002; Kacelnik et al.,
2006;
 Bluff et al., 2007). Both approaches would be necessary to
tease apart which cognitive mechanisms are involved in tool use,
and whether this behaviour indeed draws on specialised cognitive
adaptations. Whilst information for NC crows is still scarce, Bird and
Emery’s (2009) experiments with non-tool-using rooks are reveal-
ing, as these birds were clearly capable of making and deploying
tools when being provided with proﬁtable  opportunities to do so.
Even wider taxonomic context is afforded by recent studies that
compared the performance of tool-using woodpecker ﬁnches  to
that of non-tool-using congeners, ﬁnding  little systematic differ-
ence between the species (Tebbich et al., 2010; Teschke et al., 2011).
Much work in this area remains to be done.
NC crows exhibit an unusual ontogenetic pattern that seems
to facilitate their tool-related behaviours. Controlled experiments
with captive birds demonstrated that young NC crows have a keen
interest in objects and will, in the absence of any direct food
reinforcement, persistently engage in object-oriented behaviours
during their development (Kenward et al., 2005, 2006, 2011). Like-
wise, immature crows in the wild have been observed persistently
performing unsuccessful tool-use behaviours several months post-
ﬂedging  (Bluff et al., 2010b; Holzhaider et al., 2010a,b). Comparing
the ontogeny of object-oriented behaviours in captive-bred NC
crows and common ravens Corvus corax, Kenward et al. (2011)
observed that, over a 6-week post-ﬂedging  study period, object
combinations that are precursors to functional behaviour increased
in NC crows but decreased in ravens (see also next paragraph).
It thus seems possible that, what is special about NC crows
behaviourally, is an unusually strong, inherited motivation to inter-
act with and combine objects (Kenward et al., 2006, 2011; Kacelnik,
2009; adult NC crows also use tools in non-foraging contexts:
Wimpenny et al., 2011; Taylor et al., in press).
There are many examples of animal behaviours becoming
co-opted to serve in novel contexts (Baerends, 1975). In the com-
parative study reviewed above, there were striking qualitative
similarities in the ontogeny of tool-oriented behaviour in NC crows
and food-caching behaviour in ravens (Kenward et al., 2011). Food
caching, which is very likely to have been an established behaviour
in proto-NC crows (de Kort and Clayton, 2006), may therefore have
been an evolutionary precursor for tool use, and indeed provides a
more parsimonious account than three other possible candidates:
prey handling, nest building (Alcock, 1972; Hansell and Ruxton,
2008), and foraging in Pandanus spp. crowns. In particular, like
tool use, and as summarised by the authors of the study: (i) it
entails the insertion of objects into small cavities, with subsequent
repositioning; (ii) it involves twig insertion as an early develop-
mental precursor; (iii) it predominantly involves food; and (iv) it
provides the animal with privileged knowledge about the presence
of hidden food. Caching also has a developmental pattern strik-
ingly similar to tool use, and is a less common behaviour than
nest building, which is consistent with the observed rarity of avian
tool use (cf. Section 3.3). These results are noteworthy, as they
are the outcome of controlled interspeciﬁc  comparisons, provid-
ing rare quantitative glimpses into NC crows’ possible evolutionary
past.
NC crows have a slow life-history compared to similar-sized
birds, including other corvids. As has been hypothesised for
humans, an extended period of juvenile dependence could pro-
vide extra time to learn complex subsistence techniques (review:
Kaplan et al., 2000). This view is compatible with the recent ﬁnding
that young crows require at least one year post-ﬂedging  to reach
proﬁciency  in the skills related to both larva-ﬁshing  with stick tools
and the manufacture and deployment of (wide) pandanus tools
(Bluff et al., 2010b; Holzhaider et al., 2010a,b). During this time,
although capable of foraging independently, immature crows con-
tinue to receive food from their parents, including highly nutritious,
tool-derived beetle larvae (see Fig. S2 in Rutz et al., 2010).

4. Concluding remarks
Our analyses enable us to reconstruct a tentative course of
evolutionary events. A non-tool using ancestor of NC crows, of
South-East Asian or Australasian origin, arrived in New Caledo-
nia several million years ago. The presence of proﬁtable  hidden
food, in combination with a lack of direct competition for these
resources, created a vacant woodpecker-like niche on the island.
Crows may have possessed certain behavioural and/or morpholog-
ical features that predisposed them to express tool-use rather than
prey-excavation behaviour, although it is possible that the latter
formed an evolutionary springboard for the former. Low predation
levels, and exposure to novel energy-rich concealed food sources by
humans, may have further encouraged tool-use behaviour in proto-
NC crows. The Baldwin effect (Weber and Depew, 2003; see also
West-Eberhard, 2003) describes how regularly learned behaviour
can gradually be assimilated into a species’ genetic make-up (for a
discussion of woodpecker ﬁnch  tool use, see Tebbich et al., 2010),
but these processes remain poorly understood and we resist spec-
ulation.
We can now return to the broader questions of: (i) why, in gen-
eral, tool use is so rare in birds; and (ii) why NC crows are the
only known habitual tool users in the genus Corvus. Amongst the
four habitually tool-using avian species listed in Section 3.3, only
woodpecker ﬁnches  and NC crows use stick-type tools for extract-
ing invertebrates from hidden places. Whilst their tool use seems
to have derived from different precursor behaviours (for discus-
sion, see Kenward et al., 2011), the key ecological drivers may well
have been similar. It is notable that these two most proliﬁc  avian
tool users are both endemic to remote ‘Darwinian’ islands (i.e.,
oceanic islands with a composite biota that derives predominantly
from colonisation; sensu Grandcolas et al., 2008). Although this is
statistically unsurprising, given that about 17% of all extant bird
species are island endemics (Johnson and Stattersﬁeld,  1990), the
fact is consistent with an effect of the ecological drivers discussed in
Section 3.5. Darwinian islands are generally species-poor, lack non-
tool-using extractive foragers including woodpeckers and arboreal
mammals, and have depauperate predator communities. The other
two well-researched habitual avian tool users exhibit very differ-
ent behaviours, to solve different foraging problems in different
habitats (Egyptian vultures: use of stones to crack open ostrich
Struthio camelus eggs; brown-headed nuthatches: use of bark scales
of longleaf pines Pinus australis to pry off other pieces of tree bark,
presumably in search for invertebrates).
Crows in general may be predisposed to ‘discover’ tool use as
a proﬁtable  foraging mode (see Section 3.7.2), as long as environ-
mental conditions are favourable. This view is supported by the
observation that rooks have the cognitive capacity for tool use (Bird
and Emery, 2009), but do not habitually express the behaviour
in the wild (del Hoyo et al., 2009). Our analyses suggest that a
complex interplay of different environmental conditions, perhaps
in combination with a species’ behavioural and morphological
predispositions, may be necessary to foster the evolution of tool
use. As Bluff (2008) observed, the availability of wood-boring
cerambycid larvae, in combination with a lack of woodpeckers or
other competitors, may be a necessary condition, but is in itself
insufﬁcient,  as these circumstances have failed to produce tool use
in several other Corvus species. A good example is the sympatry of
the Torresian crow Corvus orru and the Australian longicorn beetle
Agrianome spinicollis over a large proportion of northeast Australia
(Rowley, 1970; Hawkeswood et al., 1997), outside the range of
extractively foraging striped possums (Handasyde, 2008; but see
Beck, 2009). There are other examples of Corvus species that live
in habitats that are woodpecker-free, but perhaps are less rich
in concealed prey resources. Clearly, if we wish to advance our
understanding of the evolutionary roots of NC crow tool use, and
its
 present-day ecological signiﬁcance,  we need to keep employing
innovative study approaches. Some of the ideas we sketched in
this review are testable, promising valuable future insights.
Widening the phylogenetic scope of inter-speciﬁc  comparisons
–  to include not just avian but also mammalian tool users –  would
advance our understanding of the subject matter, but even this
sample would be small and have poor taxonomic spread. What is
clear from an informal comparison of patterns is that similar factors
may have selected for tool use in birds (our review) and primates
(e.g., van Schaik et al., 1999), providing at least two statistically
‘independent’ data points (see Sections 1 and 3.3). We hope our
review serves to demonstrate that work on NC crows enables useful
tests of some hypotheses regarding the evolutionary and ecologi-
cal context of animal tool use, and perhaps more importantly, has
potential to generate new ones.
